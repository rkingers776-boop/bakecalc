1 Cup Powdered Sugar to Grams — the Sifted-vs-Unsifted Mess, Solved

Also called confectioners' sugar or icing sugar. 125 grams unsifted, 110 grams sifted — and a 15-gram grammar trap hiding in plain sight on every recipe card.

Gram Equivalent:

125 grams (125 g)

*Unsifted powdered sugar from the bag ≈ 125g/cup. If sifted first (aerate, then measure) ≈ 110g/cup — a 12% difference. Word order in recipes matters: "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" ≠ "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted."

The Frosting That Made Me Finally Buy a Scale

I was making American buttercream for my sister's wedding shower — 4 cups of powdered sugar, two sticks of butter, heavy cream, vanilla. The recipe I'd used a dozen times. Except this time I sifted the powdered sugar first — really sifted it, the way you do when you're trying to be "proper" for an occasion. Measured 4 cups of the aerated cloud and beat it in. The frosting was thin. Like, pourable thin. Not a spreadable consistency at all.

I had inadvertently used 440 grams of sugar (4 × 110g, sifted weight) instead of the 500 grams the recipe was developed with (4 × 125g, unsifted). I was missing 60 grams — 12% of the sugar the recipe counted on for structure. The frosting never firmed up properly because there simply wasn't enough sugar to hold the emulsion together. I added more sugar to fix it, which threw off the sweetness balance, which made me add more salt and vanilla, which by the end was a completely different recipe and entirely too much vanilla. The bride liked it. I knew it was wrong. That was the day I started weighing powdered sugar instead of measuring it.

The Grammar Rule That Actually Changes Your Frosting

There's a piece of recipe-writing pedantry that sounds like the kind of thing only a cookbook editor would care about — except it genuinely changes how much sugar ends up in your bowl:

  • "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" → Sift first, then measure. You're measuring aerated sugar. Result: about 110 grams in that cup.
  • "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted" → Measure first (unsifted, from the bag, 125g), then pass through a sifter into the bowl to break up lumps. The comma is doing a lot of work.

The difference is 15 grams per cup. For a dusting on brownies, it doesn't matter. For a frosting recipe calling for 4 cups, that's 60 grams of difference — enough to turn a stiff, pipeable buttercream into something that slides off the cake. The problem? Most recipe writers don't know the rule either. They write "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted" because it sounds nicer, even if they actually sift first in their own kitchen. Others write "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" because that's what their grandmother's recipe card says, even though they measure straight from the bag.

This is the practical case for weights over volumes. "125 grams powdered sugar" has zero ambiguity. It doesn't matter if you sift it, don't sift it, sift it halfway — 125 grams is 125 grams. The scale is the only tool in your kitchen that doesn't care about grammar. That Chalky Taste in Your Buttercream Is Cornstarch — Here's What to Do About It

Commercial powdered sugar isn't just ground sugar. It's 95-97% sucrose and 3-5% cornstarch (labeled as "anti-caking agent" on the bag). The cornstarch keeps the fine particles from clumping into a solid block during shipping and storage. It works — powdered sugar flows freely even after months in the cabinet. But cornstarch doesn't dissolve in cold fat. It needs heat to gelatinize and lose its raw-starch flavor.

In an uncooked American buttercream, that cornstarch never gets hot enough. The result is that faint "raw flour" taste that coats your tongue after a bite of frosting — most people can't identify it, but they know the frosting doesn't taste clean. Here's the fix hierarchy, from easiest to most involved:

  1. Beat longer. After all the sugar is incorporated, run the mixer on medium-high for a full 3-4 minutes. The friction from the paddle against the bowl generates enough warmth to partially hydrate the starch granules. It's not as effective as cooking, but it noticeably reduces the chalkiness. The frosting will also get lighter in color and fluffier — that's the aeration you want.
  2. Add salt. A scant ¼ teaspoon of fine sea salt per 4 cups of powdered sugar. The salt doesn't make the frosting salty — it suppresses your tongue's ability to detect bitterness, which includes the raw-starch note from cornstarch. This is a sensory trick, not a chemical fix, but it works surprisingly well.
  3. Switch to organic powdered sugar. Organic brands typically use tapioca starch instead of cornstarch as the anti-caking agent. Tapioca starch has a cleaner, more neutral flavor in uncooked applications. Same weight per cup (125g), same behavior in recipes, better taste. The trade-off: organic powdered sugar costs about twice as much as conventional.
  4. Grind your own. Put granulated sugar in a blender or spice grinder and run it for 30-60 seconds until it's a fine powder. Let the dust settle before opening the lid or you'll sugar-coat your entire kitchen. Result: pure powdered sugar, zero starch, zero chalkiness. Downside: it'll clump within 24 hours from ambient humidity, so only grind what you need for that batch. Also: DIY powdered sugar is slightly finer than commercial 10X, which means it dissolves even faster in liquids. For glazes and whipped cream, this is actually an upgrade.

10X, 6X, 4X — Powdered Sugar Grades and When You'd Need to Care

Powdered sugar comes in different grind sizes, designated by "X" numbers. The higher the number, the finer the grind. Standard grocery store powdered sugar is 10X — the finest commercially available grade, ground to a particle size of roughly 10-15 microns. (A human hair is about 70 microns, for reference.)

6X is coarser — about 30-40 micron particles — and mostly used in commercial donut sugar and some industrial bakeries where ultra-fine dust is a respiratory hazard. 4X is coarser still and essentially a niche product for certain candy coatings where you want a visible sugar crust. For home baking, every bag on the shelf is 10X. The numbering system exists for commercial buyers, not for you, but knowing it exists explains why "powdered sugar," "confectioners' sugar," and "icing sugar" are all the same 10X product in different countries with different names.

One practical application: if you're grinding your own in a blender, you'll get something closer to 12X — finer than commercial. That means it dissolves faster and absorbs moisture faster. Great for no-cook frostings and dusting. Not great for storage. Use it immediately or watch it turn into a sugar brick by tomorrow morning.

Dos and Don'ts for Frosting That Actually Holds Its Shape

Powdered sugar's main job is structure. In a buttercream, it provides the rigid scaffolding that lets you pipe roses and swirls that stay standing. Getting the ratio wrong — even by a few grams — changes everything. Some rules I've settled on after more frosting failures than I want to count:

  • Standard American buttercream. 1 cup butter (227g) : 4 cups powdered sugar (500g unsifted). This is the baseline. It's stiff enough to pipe, sweet enough to taste like a bakery cupcake. If it's too thick, add cream 1 teaspoon at a time — teaspoons, not tablespoons. Over-thinning is a one-way street; you can always add more liquid but you can't take it out.
  • Cream cheese frosting. Reduce to 3-3½ cups powdered sugar (375-440g) per 8 oz cream cheese + ½ cup butter. Cream cheese is softer than butter at room temperature, so it needs less sugar to reach pipeable stiffness. Too much sugar and you lose the tang that makes cream cheese frosting taste like cream cheese instead of just "white frosting."
  • For piping roses or intricate decorations: add an extra ½ cup (60g) powdered sugar to stiffen. The increased sugar makes the frosting slightly less pleasant to eat straight, but it holds detail work that would slump with a standard ratio.
  • Humidity is not your friend. Powdered sugar is hygroscopic — it pulls moisture from the air. On a humid day (say, above 70% humidity), your frosting will absorb water from the atmosphere and gradually soften. If you're frosting a cake for an outdoor summer event, use the stiff piping ratio above and keep the cake refrigerated until the last possible moment. The cornstarch in commercial powdered sugar actually helps here — it buffers some of the moisture absorption. Questions From People Who Actually Frost Cakes

How many grams is 1 cup of powdered sugar?

125 grams unsifted, straight from the bag. If you sift before measuring — which aerates the sugar and adds air volume — 1 cup weighs about 110 grams. The recipe's word order tells you which method: "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" (sift then measure, 110g) vs "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted" (measure then sift, 125g). If you're tired of parsing grammar in recipe instructions, weigh 125g and skip the ambiguity entirely. For the brown sugar equivalent — which has its own completely different measurement rules — see brown sugar cups to grams.

Why is powdered sugar lighter than granulated sugar per cup?

Granulated sugar = 200g/cup. Powdered sugar = 125g/cup unsifted. That's a 75-gram difference, from the exact same raw material (sucrose). The reason: granulated sugar crystals are uniform little cubes that stack efficiently and leave relatively little air between them. Powdered sugar is mechanically pulverized into irregular, fluffy particles that trap vastly more air per unit volume. It's the same principle as why a bag of feathers weighs less than a bag of rocks the same size — except both bags are sugar, just in different physical forms. Sifted powdered sugar (110g/cup) is even lighter because sifting deliberately adds more air. This is also why flour weighs only 120g/cup — fine powders share this low-density property.

My powdered sugar glaze is lumpy — what am I doing wrong?

You're probably adding liquid to the sugar all at once and stirring. That creates a paste around each sugar clump that the liquid can't penetrate, and you end up whisking aggressively to break them up (which incorporates air bubbles that show up as white specks on a clear glaze). Better method: put the powdered sugar in a bowl. Make a well in the center. Add your liquid (milk, lemon juice, water) one teaspoon at a time into the well, stirring from the center outward with each addition. The sugar hydrates gradually and evenly. If lumps persist despite your best efforts, push the glaze through a fine-mesh strainer — it'll catch the lumps and you'll have a perfectly smooth result. This works for any powdered-sugar-based glaze, including the one on a classic chocolate glaze.

Can I use powdered sugar in cookies or cakes instead of granulated?

Don't. Even though they're chemically identical (sucrose), their physical forms are so different that swapping them changes the entire structure of what you're baking. Granulated sugar crystals cut through butter during creaming, creating the air pockets that leaven your cookies and cakes. Powdered sugar can't do this — its particles are too fine to aerate butter, and the cornstarch actively inhibits gluten development. A cookie made entirely with powdered sugar will be dense, pale, and soft — almost shortbread-like. The exception: some shortbread and meltaway cookie recipes deliberately use powdered sugar specifically because they don't want aeration or spread. Those recipes know what they're doing.
