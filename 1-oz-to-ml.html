1 oz in mL — the short answer

One US fluid ounce is 29.57 mL — 29.5735 if you want the digits the definition actually produces. That is the figure to reach for in an American kitchen, and it is what this converter uses. If the recipe came from Britain or Australia, the same two words mean a slightly smaller pour: one imperial fluid ounce is 28.41 mL. The gap is 4%, which is nothing in a soup and a great deal in a cocktail, a medicine dose, or a concentrated flavouring.

The second thing worth settling before converting anything: an ounce on its own is not a volume at all. It is a weight, 28.35 grams. Recipes mix the two constantly, so the useful first step is working out which ounce is in front of you.

One Word, Two Different Quantities

US customary and imperial units both reuse ounce for volume and for mass, and that single overlap causes most of the confusion around this conversion.

  • Fluid ounce (fl oz) — a unit of volume. One US fluid ounce is 29.5735 mL. Liquids are measured this way.
  • Ounce (oz) — a unit of mass. One ounce is 28.3495 grams. Solids are measured this way.

The two numbers sit close together, 29.57 and 28.35, which is exactly why the mix-up survives. They are not interchangeable and they do not even measure the same kind of thing: a fluid ounce of honey weighs about 42 grams, while a fluid ounce of water weighs 29.57.

In practice the tell is what the ingredient does. If it pours, the recipe means a fluid ounce. If it needs a knife to level it, the recipe means an ounce of weight. The awkward middle ground is things like chocolate chips and shredded cheese, which look pourable but are almost always listed by weight — so 8 oz of chocolate chips is 227 grams, not 237 millilitres.

What One Fluid Ounce Looks Like in a Kitchen

One fluid ounce is rare in US baking recipes, which is the whole reason people look it up. Here is what it corresponds to:

  • 2 tablespoons, or 6 teaspoons
  • One eighth of a US cup
  • The small end of a standard bar jigger — the large end is 1.5 fl oz
  • A short pour of spirits. A US shot is conventionally 1.5 fl oz, so 1 oz is deliberately under it

In baking, one fluid ounce turns up for ingredients added in small, careful amounts: vanilla and other extracts, food colouring, citrus juice, the rum brushed over a soaked cake, the liquid in a thin glaze. In most of those a 4% error survives. But that is an argument for measuring properly, not for rounding 29.57 up to 30 and hoping.

Multiply any fluid ounce figure by 29.5735 for US measures, or 28.4131 for British and Australian ones. Both constants are exact under their own definitions. The rounding only begins when you decide how many digits to keep.

Where 29.5735 Comes From

The US fluid ounce is not a free-standing unit. It is defined downward from the US gallon, which is legally 231 cubic inches. Divide that gallon into 128 parts and a fluid ounce comes out at 1.8047 cubic inches, which is 29.5735 mL. The number is unround because it descends from a volume definition that predates metrication by two centuries.

The imperial fluid ounce descends from a different gallon entirely — the one set by the British Weights and Measures Act of 1824 and divided into 160 parts — and works out at 28.4131 mL. The two systems have been out of step since the United States kept the older gallon and Britain replaced theirs. Both names are still in everyday use, which is why a simple conversion has three plausible answers.

Rounding to 30 mL — When It Is Fine

Many charts list 1 oz as 30 mL. For a lot of cooking that is close enough: the error is 1.4%, and nutrient labels, medicine cups and cheap jugs are printed at 30 mL because a round number reads faster at a glance.

It stops being close enough in two places. The first is cocktails and liquid concentrates, where several ingredients each carry a small error and the drink is built on ratios. The second is anything with a narrow margin — liquid medicine, essential oils, brewing salts. There, use 29.5735 and round at the end rather than the beginning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many mL is 1 oz?

A1: 29.57 mL if the recipe means a US fluid ounce — multiply by 29.5735 for the exact figure. For an imperial fluid ounce, which is what British and Australian recipes use, it is 28.41 mL.

Q2: Is 1 oz the same as 30 mL?

A2: Close, but not exactly: 30 mL is 1.4% more than 29.57 mL. Measuring jugs and medicine cups use the round number because it reads more easily. The rounding is harmless in most cooking and worth avoiding in cocktails, flavourings, and anything dosed for safety.

Q3: Is 1 oz of weight the same as 1 fluid ounce?

A3: No — they measure different things. One ounce of weight is 28.35 grams and applies to solids. One US fluid ounce is 29.57 mL of volume and applies to liquids. The figures are near enough to each other that the two get mixed up constantly, but a fluid ounce of water weighs 29.57 g while a fluid ounce of honey weighs about 42 g.
