100 g Flour to Cups Converter

Convert 100 grams of flour to cups — five sixths, and the two-measure trick that gets you there exactly.

Cup Equivalent:

Roughly 4/5 cup (0.83 cups)

*Calculated using standard US customary baking cups via spoon-and-level method.

100 g of flour in cups — the short answer

One hundred grams of all-purpose flour is 0.83 cups, which is five sixths of a cup, or about 13 tablespoons and a teaspoon. It is not a cup, and the gap is not trivial — a full cup is 120 g, so treating 100 g as a cup puts 20% more flour into the bowl than the recipe intended.

That mistake is easy to make precisely because 100 is such a round-looking number. It reads like a convenient measure, and a cup is a convenient measure, so the two get paired up. They do not belong together.

Building Five Sixths From Two Measures

Five sixths is not a fraction most measuring sets offer, but it is one you can assemble exactly, because it happens to equal one half plus one third:

  • Half a cup of flour — 60 g
  • Third of a cup of flour — 40 g
  • Combined — 100 g, exactly

No estimating, no partial fills. This is worth remembering because 100 g shows up often in European recipes and in half-batches of American ones, and the two-measure route is the only way to hit it precisely without a scale.

If you have only a one-cup measure, fill it and stop short of the rim, leaving about a sixth of the cup empty. That is an eyeball estimate rather than a measurement, but it is a much better estimate than filling the cup level and adding 20%.

Where 100 Grams of Flour Shows Up

  • Half batches — a recipe calling for 200 g of flour, halved, lands here. Half batches are the most common reason people convert 100 g at all.
  • Small cakes — a single six-inch layer or a small loaf pan often uses 100 to 150 g of flour.
  • European recipes — metric recipes round to 100 g far more readily than they round to 120 g, because 100 is a natural metric quantity and the cup is not a metric unit at all.
  • Roux and thickeners — smaller quantities where flour is a supporting player rather than the structure.

Why the Cup Is 120 Grams

The 120 g figure is not derived from anything — it is measured. Recipe developers and flour companies weigh cup after cup of spooned-and-levelled flour and report the average, and 120 g is where most of them land. King Arthur's published chart uses it, and so does this site.

It is worth knowing that other charts say 125 g, and a few say 130. The differences come from how each tester filled the cup and which flour they sampled. What matters is not which number is correct in the abstract, but that you pick one and stay with it. If a recipe was built on 125 g per cup and you convert at 120, the flour is 4% light — small, but not nothing.

  • 120 g — 1 cup exactly
  • 150 g — 1.25 cups
  • 200 g — 1.67 cups

The Other Flours, at 100 Grams

Change the flour and the cup figure changes with it, because each flour packs differently. At 100 g:

  • All-purpose flour — 0.83 cups
  • Bread flour — 0.77 cups
  • Cake flour — 0.91 cups
  • Whole wheat flour — 0.83 cups
  • Almond flour — 1.04 cups, so here 100 g is a shade over a cup

Almond flour is the one that inverts the usual warning. Because it is light at 96 g per cup, 100 g is more than a cup rather than less — the opposite of the all-purpose answer. Anyone swapping flours in a recipe needs to check the density, not just the gram figure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Is 100 g of flour equal to 1 cup?

A1: No. A US cup of all-purpose flour measures about 120 grams by the spoon-and-level method, so 100 grams is 0.83 cups — five sixths of a cup, not a full one. Measuring a full cup instead gives you 20% more flour than the recipe asked for.

Q2: How do I measure 100 g of flour in cups?

A2: Combine a level half cup with a level third cup. A half cup is 60 grams and a third cup is 40 grams, and 60 plus 40 is exactly 100. That works precisely because five sixths is the sum of one half and one third.

Q3: What if my recipe says 100 g but I only have a 1 cup measure?

A3: Fill it and stop short. A cup measure filled to about five sixths of its rim holds 100 grams of spooned and levelled all-purpose flour. It is an estimate, but a better one than filling it level and calling it done, which adds 20 grams.
