50 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

Fifty grams of granulated sugar is exactly one quarter cup — or 4 level tablespoons. No partial measure, no rounding, no spooning technique to worry about.

That precision is not luck. Sugar runs 200 g per cup, and 50 divides into 200 four times. The same arithmetic that makes flour awkward makes sugar clean, and the reason is in the physical form of the ingredient rather than the numbers.

Why Sugar Converts Cleanly and Flour Does Not

A cup of flour can weigh 120 g or 145 g depending on whether you spooned it or scooped it, because flour particles are soft and irregular and pack down under pressure. Sugar does not do this. Granulated sugar is made of hard, uniform crystals that resist compression, so the same cup holds about the same weight however you fill it.

The practical consequence is a rule worth remembering:

  • Sugar converts reliably by volume. Spoon it or scoop it and the cup is within a few grams either way.
  • Flour does not. The same cup swings by 25 grams depending on technique.
  • Brown sugar is the exception among sugars. It is soft and moist, so it does compact, and recipes specify packed for exactly that reason.

Which means a 50 g sugar conversion is one of the few places where a cup measure is genuinely as good as a scale. That is not true of the flour pages on this site, and it is worth knowing which ingredient you are dealing with before deciding how much to care.

Where 50 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • Whipped cream — about 2 tablespoons of sugar per cup of cream, so 50 g sweetens a generous bowl.
  • Single servings of meringue — a small batch for two, where the sugar-to-white ratio matters more than the total.
  • Glazes and syrups — small quantities where sugar is dissolved rather than creamed.
  • Tea and coffee — roughly 12 level teaspoons, for anyone converting a recipe into drink terms.

What Sugar Does Beyond Sweetening

Sugar is usually treated as a flavour ingredient, which hides how much structural work it does. In a cake it delays gluten development by competing with flour proteins for water, which is part of why a high-sugar cake is tender. It raises the temperature at which the batter sets, giving the leavening more time to act. And it browns, both through caramelisation and by feeding the Maillard reaction with the proteins around it.

At 50 g the quantity is small enough that the structural effects are minor and the flavour effect dominates. That is why 50 g appears in glazes, toppings and drinks more often than in batters — there is not enough of it to change how a cake sets, but plenty to change how it tastes.

The Same 50 g in Other Sugars

Change the sugar and the cup figure changes, because the crystals and the moisture are different:

  • Granulated white sugar — 200 g per cup, so 50 g is 0.25 cups
  • Brown sugar, packed — 220 g per cup, so 50 g is 0.23 cups
  • Powdered sugar, unsifted — 120 g per cup, so 50 g is 0.42 cups
  • Powdered sugar, sifted — 100 g per cup, so 50 g is 0.5 cups
  • Coconut sugar — about 180 g per cup, so 50 g is 0.28 cups

Powdered sugar is the one to watch. It is ground so fine and carries so much air that 50 g takes nearly twice the volume of the same weight of granulated. Substituting one for the other by cup rather than by weight is how a frosting ends up either too stiff or too runny.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 50 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly one quarter cup of granulated sugar, or 4 level tablespoons. Sugar runs 200 grams per cup, and 50 divides into that cleanly, so no partial measure is needed.

Q2: Why is sugar easier to measure by cup than flour?

A2: Because sugar is crystalline and flour is not. Sugar grains are hard, uniform and do not compact under the pressure of a measuring cup, so a cup of sugar weighs about 200 grams whether it was spooned or scooped. Flour can vary by 25 grams in the same cup depending on how it was filled.

Q3: Is 50 g of brown sugar also a quarter cup?

A3: Not quite. Brown sugar packs to about 220 grams per cup when firmly pressed, so 50 grams is closer to 0.23 cups. The difference is small but it moves the wrong way from what most people assume — packed brown sugar is heavier, so a given weight takes up less room.
