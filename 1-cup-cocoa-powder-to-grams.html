1 cup of cocoa powder in grams — the short answer

One cup of cocoa powder is 100 grams when it is spooned into the cup and levelled off. That holds for natural cocoa and for Dutch-process cocoa, which are the same density. Black cocoa is a touch lighter at about 95 grams.

It is a clean number and it is also, in practice, close to meaningless on its own. Cocoa is the most compressible ingredient in the baking pantry, and the same cup can hold anywhere from 85 to 125 grams of it depending on how it got there.

Why One Cup of Cocoa Is Such a Slippery Number

Most ingredients have a density that survives handling. Granulated sugar is crystalline and hard, so it varies by about 1% whatever you do to it. Flour varies by 20% because its particles are soft. Cocoa goes further than either, for two reasons.

First, it is milled extremely fine — the particles are small enough to suspend in liquid and to clog a sieve, which means there is a great deal of air between them by default. Second, those particles are irregular and slightly plastic, so they interlock under pressure instead of sliding past each other the way sand does.

The result is a powder that behaves more like flour than like sugar, with a wider range at the extremes.

Sifted, Spooned, Scooped

  • 1 cup sifted — about 85 g. Sifting coats every particle in air and is the loosest state cocoa reaches.
  • 1 cup spooned and levelled — about 100 g. This is the reference figure and the one the converter above uses.
  • 1 cup scooped from the tin and levelled — about 120 g.
  • 1 cup scooped, tapped down, and levelled — 125 g or more.

That is a spread of 47%, and it runs in a direction that makes recipes fail quietly. A chocolate cake built around three cups of cocoa, measured by scooping instead of spooning, has 60 g more cocoa than intended — which brings extra starch, extra bitterness, and a batter that has absorbed noticeably more liquid than the recipe planned for.

The rule that follows from this is simple: for cocoa, spoon and level, never scoop. Or ignore the cup entirely and weigh.

Natural and Dutch-Process Weigh the Same

The distinction between natural and Dutch-process cocoa is about chemistry, not weight, and it is worth understanding because it changes what the cocoa does in the oven rather than how much of it you need.

  • Natural cocoa is simply ground roasted cocoa solids. It is acidic, with a pH somewhere around 5 to 6, and it tastes sharp and fruity. It is lighter in colour than most people expect.
  • Dutch-process cocoa has been treated with an alkaline solution, which raises the pH towards neutral, darkens the colour to a deep reddish brown, and rounds off the acidity into something smoother and more chocolatey.

Both are milled to the same fineness and both weigh about 100 g per spooned cup. So a converting recipe needs no adjustment for weight — but it may need one for leavening, which is a separate section below.

What 100 g of Cocoa Does to a Batter

Cocoa is not just colour and flavour. It is roughly a quarter starch by weight and carries a large amount of fibre, and both of those absorb water aggressively. Cocoa is also the only source of fat in a cocoa-based cake batter, at around 10 to 12% cocoa butter in a typical unsweetened powder.

At 100 g, the practical consequences are:

  • Liquid absorption — 100 g of cocoa will take up close to its own weight in liquid before the batter loosens, which is why chocolate cakes need more milk than the vanilla cakes they were adapted from.
  • Structure — the starch and fibre thicken the batter, so a cocoa cake sets firmer and dries faster than the equivalent plain one.
  • Tenderness — the cocoa butter is a fat, and fat shortens gluten, so more cocoa means a softer crumb, right up to the point where the starch overrides it.

The Leavening Rule That Catches People Out

Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate and needs an acid to react with. Baking powder carries its own acid and does not.

Natural cocoa is acidic, so a recipe pairing natural cocoa with baking soda is a balanced system: the cocoa supplies the acid, the soda supplies the base, and the reaction produces the lift and the deep colour. Swap in Dutch-process cocoa and the acid is gone. The soda has nothing to react with, and the cake comes out flat with an unpleasant soapy edge from unreacted bicarbonate.

The commonly published fix is to replace the baking soda with about twice its volume of baking powder. If the recipe asks for one teaspoon of soda alongside its natural cocoa, you would use two teaspoons of baking powder with Dutch-process cocoa instead.

Cocoa Without a Scale

  • By spoon — one cup is 16 level tablespoons, so 6 1/4 g each, which is the same 100 g.
  • By the technique — spoon from the tin into the cup, do not tap, then sweep level with a straight edge. This alone keeps you near 100 g.
  • By feel — if the cocoa in the cup looks dense and level like brown sugar, it is probably 120 g. If it looks fluffy and slightly mounded before levelling, you are near the sifted figure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of cocoa powder?

A1: 100 grams when the cocoa is spooned into the cup and levelled. That is the working figure for both natural and Dutch-process cocoa, which have the same density.

Q2: Why does my cup of cocoa weigh more than 100 grams?

A2: Almost certainly because the cup was scooped rather than spooned. Cocoa compresses easily, and a cup dug straight into the tin can hold 120 to 125 grams of the same cocoa.

Q3: Does Dutch-process cocoa weigh the same as natural cocoa?

A3: Yes. Alkalising changes the acidity, the colour and the flavour of cocoa but leaves its bulk density essentially alone, so one cup of either comes to about 100 grams.
