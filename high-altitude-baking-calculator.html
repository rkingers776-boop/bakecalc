Why Baking Changes Above 3,000 Feet

Air pressure at 5,000 feet is about 12% lower than at sea level. That one number does three things to your baking:

  • Leavening gases expand faster. Baking powder, soda, and yeast all produce gas that inflates more aggressively in thinner air. Cakes rise fast — then collapse before the structure sets.
  • Water boils at a lower temperature (about 203°F at 5,000 ft). Moisture leaves your batter earlier, leaving a dry, crumbly crumb — hence the extra liquid.
  • Yeast dough rises dramatically faster. A 90-minute bulk ferment at sea level might take 55-65 minutes at 7,000 ft. Follow the original timing and you get over-proofed, dense bread.

The deeper version of all this — including why cakes are the hardest thing to get right and what cookies need — is in our complete high-altitude baking guide.

How to Use the Adjustments

  1. Enter your elevation — the calculator picks the right band (3,000-5,000 / 5,000-7,500 / 7,500-10,000 / 10,000+ ft).
  2. Pick what you're baking — yeast bread gets extra guidance on yeast and rise times.
  3. Apply the adjustments to your recipe — per cup of each ingredient, not for the whole recipe.
  4. Bake, check early, adjust once. Change one variable at a time and record what worked — every oven and kitchen is different.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what elevation do I need to adjust my baking?

Below 3,000 ft, bake normally. Above that, start adjusting — leavening first. Most home bakers first notice problems around 3,500-4,000 ft.

My cake keeps collapsing in the middle — is it altitude?

Very possibly. Collapsed cakes are the classic altitude symptom: gas expands too fast, the structure can't hold it, and the center falls as it cools. Reduce baking powder/soda first (the single most effective fix), then check sugar and liquid. Our why cakes sink in the middle guide covers the other causes too.

Does altitude affect sourdough?

Sourdough is more forgiving than commercial yeast at altitude — wild cultures are slower to start with, which partially compensates. Reduce the starter a little, expect faster bulk fermentation once it's active, and rely on dough feel rather than the clock. See our sourdough troubleshooting guide for more.

Can I adjust by recipe instead of per cup?

The adjustments are per cup of each ingredient. For a recipe using 2 cups of flour, the flour adjustment applies twice. Our recipe scaler can help you re-work the whole ingredient list after you've applied the altitude adjustments.
