50 g of cocoa powder in cups — the short answer

Fifty grams of cocoa powder is half a cup, or 8 level tablespoons. Cocoa is taken at 100 g per cup, so 50 g divides it evenly, and this is one of the very few round numbers in the cocoa family.

The roundness is slightly deceptive. Half a cup is an easy thing to measure and an easy thing to measure wrong.

Why Half a Cup Is Easy to Get Wrong

Cocoa varies more than any other baking powder at the same volume, and the variation runs in one unhelpful direction:

  • Sifted, then spooned — 43 g in half a cup
  • Spooned from the tin and levelled — 50 g, the figure this page uses
  • Scooped from the tin and levelled — 60 g
  • Scooped, tapped on the counter, levelled — 63 g or more

Between the loosest and the firmest method there is a 20 g spread on a 50 g target. That is a 40% error, produced entirely by technique, and it is the reason a chocolate cake made from the same recipe can come out dark and dry one weekend and light and moist the next.

The eight level tablespoons are the safer route. Counting is tedious and accurate; judging the fill of a half cup measure against a photograph in your memory is neither.

Spooned Against Scooped, in Recipe Terms

What actually goes wrong when you scoop? Three things, all of them cumulative.

  • More starch — cocoa is about a quarter starch, so 60 g brings 15 g of it against the intended 12.5 g. The crumb sets firmer.
  • More bitterness — unsweetened cocoa is bitter, and a fifth more of it reads clearly in a lightly sweetened recipe.
  • Less free liquid — the extra 10 g of cocoa absorbs roughly 10 g of liquid, so a batter designed to be slack comes out stiff and bakes up dense.

None of these is catastrophic alone. Together, on a small recipe where 50 g is a leading ingredient, they are the difference between a good cake and a disappointing one.

Fifty Grams Is the Small-Batch Amount

Half a cup of cocoa is the quantity in a single 8 inch layer, a 9 by 13 pan of brownies, or one batch of chocolate cookies. Above this level most recipes switch strategy and bring in melted chocolate, because cocoa alone cannot supply enough fat to keep a large batch tender.

The landmarks around it are worth holding on to:

  • 25 g — 1/4 cup, a flavouring
  • 33 g — 1/3 cup
  • 50 g — 1/2 cup, a proper chocolate cake
  • 75 g — 3/4 cup, deep and dark
  • 100 g — 1 cup, usually blended with melted chocolate

Blooming 50 g of Cocoa

Cocoa is not soluble. It is a suspension of ground roasted solids, and if it goes into a batter dry the centres of the particles stay unhydrated and taste chalky. Whisking it into a hot liquid first — the step called blooming — fixes that and does two more things: it dissolves the starch clumps so the cocoa disperses evenly, and it drives off the aromatic compounds that carry the chocolate smell, which is why a bloomed batter smells strongly of chocolate while a dry one barely does.

The standard ratio is 1/2 cup of cocoa to 1/2 cup of boiling water, so 50 g of cocoa to about 118 g of water. The mixture becomes a thick paste that thins as it sits.

The one caution: that water is part of the recipe's liquid. A recipe that blooms 50 g of cocoa in half a cup of water and then adds its full written quantity of milk has counted the water twice, and the batter will be too slack to hold its rise.

Black Cocoa at 50 g

Black cocoa is more heavily alkalised than ordinary Dutch-process cocoa and it is slightly lighter, at about 95 g per cup. So 50 g of black cocoa takes 0.53 cups rather than 0.5 — a difference small enough to ignore in most recipes.

What is not small is the flavour. Black cocoa is very low in acid and distinctly bitter, and used alone it produces cookies that are spectacularly dark and slightly flat-tasting. Most recipes treat it as a portion of the total cocoa — a quarter to a half — with ordinary Dutch-process cocoa making up the rest.

Measuring 50 g Without a Scale

  • Spoon and level — eight level tablespoons. The spoon should be filled by dropping cocoa into it, not by pressing it in.
  • Half cup measure, spooned — same result, fewer counts.
  • Sieve it first — then you will need closer to two thirds of a cup, because sifted cocoa is much looser.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 50g of cocoa powder?

A1: Half a cup, or 8 level tablespoons, using the standard spooned measure of 100 grams per cup. It is one of the few genuinely round cocoa conversions.

Q2: Why does my half cup of cocoa weigh more than 50g?

A2: Because the cocoa was scooped rather than spooned. Cocoa packs down easily, so a half cup filled by digging the measure into the tin can hold about 60 grams of the same powder.

Q3: Is 50g of cocoa the same as half a cup of cocoa powder?

A3: Only if you measure the way the figure assumes. Half a cup spooned and levelled weighs 50 grams, half a cup scooped weighs about 60, and half a cup sifted weighs about 43.
