50 Grams of Cocoa Powder to Cups — The Half-Batch Cheat Sheet

Converting 50g of cocoa to cups for small-batch brownies, mug cakes, and scaled-down desserts.

Cup Equivalent:

½ cup (0.50 cups)

*Based on spooned-and-leveled natural cocoa at 100g/cup. Black cocoa will be a touch more generous in the cup.

50g Cocoa Powder = ½ Cup. Here's Why That's Actually Useful.

Most full-pan brownie recipes call for somewhere between 80g and 120g of cocoa. But when you're baking for one or two people, or testing a recipe before committing to a full batch, 50g is the magic number. It's exactly half a cup for natural and Dutch-process cocoa — a clean, easy measurement that lands right in the sweet spot for an 8×8 pan, six cupcakes, or a really good mug cake that doesn't taste like you're punishing yourself.

I keep a small notebook in my kitchen — the kind with oil stains on the cover — and roughly a third of the recipes in it are half-batch versions of things I tried once and immediately wanted to make again without feeding a small army. 50 grams of cocoa shows up over and over. It's the amount that gives you real chocolate flavor without committing to a full canister.

The Math Is Simple, the Density Is Fixed

Natural cocoa and Dutch cocoa both clock in at 100 grams per US cup, spooned and leveled. So 50g ÷ 100 = 0.50 cup. That's it. No weird fractions, no "scant" this or "heaping" that.

Black cocoa — the really dark, heavily alkalized stuff — is the outlier at 95g per cup. 50g of black cocoa works out to about 0.53 cup. In practice, that's ½ cup plus a very small pinch. Not enough to ruin anything, but worth knowing if you're the type who measures to the gram (and if you're on this page, you probably are).

What 50 Grams of Cocoa Actually Looks Like in a Recipe

Here's where the half-cup amount shows up in real kitchen terms — not abstract numbers, but actual things you might bake this weekend:

  • 8×8 brownie pan. The standard 9×13 brownie recipe wants about 100g cocoa. Cut everything in half and you land at 50g for a perfect 8×8 pan that serves 4–6.
  • 12 chocolate cookies. Not bakery-sized monsters — normal, palm-sized cookies with a decent chocolate kick.
  • 6 chocolate cupcakes. Half a standard dozen. Enough for a small gathering, not enough to have leftovers haunting your counter for three days.
  • 1 single-layer 8-inch cake. The kind you make on a Tuesday because you want cake and don't need to justify it.
  • 3 generous mug cakes. The real mug cake — not the sad "mix a tablespoon of cocoa with flour and pray" version, but one with actual depth.

The Cocoa Quality Equation

At 50 grams, the quality of your cocoa matters more than at larger amounts — there's less of it, so every gram is doing more work. Grocery-store natural cocoa (Hershey's, Ghirardelli) is perfectly fine for brownies where you've got chocolate chips and butter doing heavy lifting. But if you're making something where cocoa is the primary flavor — a cocoa-forward cake, chocolate sorbet, or hot cocoa mix — spring for a better brand. Droste, Valrhona, or Cacao Barry at 50g will cost you maybe a dollar more and the difference is noticeable. Not life-changing, but noticeable. Like the difference between diner coffee and the good stuff.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 50g of cocoa powder?

50 grams of natural or Dutch-process cocoa powder = ½ US cup (50g ÷ 100g/cup = 0.50 cup). For black cocoa at 95g/cup, 50g fills about 0.53 cup — ½ cup plus a tiny pinch extra.

Q2: I'm halving a recipe that calls for 100g cocoa. Do I just use ½ cup?

Yes — and this is one of those rare moments where the gram math and the cup math actually agree. 100g cocoa = 1 cup, so 50g = ½ cup. But this only works for cocoa. If the same recipe also calls for 120g flour (1 cup), halving to 60g doesn't give you ½ cup — it gives you about 0.42 cup. Don't blindly halve the cup amounts; do the gram math first, then convert.

Q3: What's the best cocoa for a small-batch recipe?

Natural unsweetened for brownies and anything with baking soda (the acid in natural cocoa activates it). Dutch-process for hot cocoa, ice cream, and recipes using baking powder. Black cocoa if you want an Oreo-like intensity — but blend it with regular cocoa (try 30g regular + 20g black) or it'll taste one-dimensional. Pure black cocoa in a brownie tastes like you baked a sleeve of Oreo wafers. Not necessarily bad, but not what most people expect.
