Why a Baking Site Has a Calorie Counter

Every baker hits the same question, usually while reaching for a second slice: "how many calories are in this cake?" Calorie apps don't make it easy to answer. Account, profile, five screens of setup, and only then can you look up "flour." This page skips all of that. Type a food, enter grams, done.

The numbers come from the USDA National Nutrient Database and NCCDB, the same references behind most nutrition apps. We kept 180+ foods people actually search for, with both raw and cooked entries where it matters. A raw chicken breast drops about a quarter of its weight in the pan while keeping its calories, so pasting the raw number onto a cooked portion overcounts by roughly a third.

How to Use It Without Overthinking

  1. Weigh raw when you can. Water loss changes weight, not calories. A 200g raw chicken breast might weigh 150g after cooking, but it's still ~240 calories. Weighing raw removes that variable entirely.
  2. Look up mixed dishes piece by piece. A "chicken stir-fry" in any database is somebody else's recipe with somebody else's oil. Yours has different amounts of everything, so weigh each part and add it up.
  3. Spend your accuracy on the dense stuff. A 50-calorie miss on broccoli is nothing. A 50-calorie miss on butter (717 kcal per 100g) or oil is two teaspoons. Watch the fats, nuts, cheese, and sugar first.
  4. The scale beats the calculator. Guess your portion and you'll get a precise-looking number that's off by 30%. A $12 digital scale fixes that overnight.

The Macro Math

Every food's calories come from four macros:

  • Protein — 4 cal/g
  • Carbohydrates — 4 cal/g
  • Fat — 9 cal/g
  • Fiber — about 2 cal/g (soluble fiber is partly fermented by gut bacteria; insoluble passes through)

Multiply each macro by its number and you'll land close to the listed total. You won't match exactly — labels round, databases average, and real food varies. A 10–20 calorie gap per 100g is normal and not worth chasing.

What This Tool Won't Do

It won't track your day, set calorie goals, scan barcodes, or log meals over time. Those features need accounts, upkeep, and a business model, which is why the apps that offer them charge or sell your data. This calculator does one job: food in, weight in, macros out. For full daily tracking, Cronometer (free, accurate database) or MacroFactor (paid, best adherence tools) are the usual picks. Keep this page open while you cook or meal-prep.

Baking-Specific Questions

How many calories are in a slice of my homemade cake?

Add up the ingredients once, then divide by the number of slices. A two-layer vanilla cake runs roughly 350–400 calories a slice before frosting, and the frosting can add another 150–250. Weigh each ingredient — flour, butter, sugar, eggs — and this calculator totals it for you.

Does whole wheat flour have fewer calories than white?

Barely. All-purpose is about 455 calories a cup; whole wheat is closer to 420. The real gap is fiber, not calories — whole wheat keeps you fuller longer. If you're counting, the flour swap matters less than how much butter or sugar rides along.

Why is butter so calorie-dense?

It's about 81% fat, and fat packs 9 calories a gram versus 4 for carbs and protein. One tablespoon of butter is roughly 100 calories, which is why croissants and shortbread add up fast. Use it, just measure it.
