Why I Built a Calorie Calculator on a Baking Site

Because every baker eventually asks the same question: "how many calories are in this cake?" Usually right before eating a second slice. Most calorie apps make you create an account, log in, set up a profile, and navigate through five screens before you can look up "flour." This calculator has none of that. Type a food. Enter grams. Done.

The food data comes from the USDA National Nutrient Database — the same source MyFitnessPal, Cronometer, and most nutrition apps use. We've curated 90+ of the most commonly searched foods and included both raw and cooked values where the difference matters (meat loses ~25% of its weight during cooking but keeps its calories; using raw values for cooked weight will overestimate your intake by about a third).

How to Actually Use This (Not Just Play With It)

  1. Weigh your food raw whenever possible. Water loss during cooking changes weight but not calories. A 200g raw chicken breast might weigh 150g cooked — but it's still ~240 calories. Weighing raw removes this variable entirely.
  2. For mixed dishes, weigh each ingredient separately and look them up one by one. Yes, it's tedious. But a "chicken stir-fry" in a calorie database is someone else's recipe with someone else's oil quantity. Your version has different amounts of everything.
  3. Don't track vegetables closely. A 50-calorie error on broccoli is irrelevant. A 50-calorie error on olive oil is two teaspoons. Focus your accuracy on calorie-dense ingredients: oils, butter, nuts, cheese, sugar, and grains.
  4. The scale matters more than the calculator. If you guess your portion size, the calculator will happily give you a precise-looking number that's wrong by 30%. A $12 digital scale fixes this.

The Macro Math (For People Who Want to Know)

The numbers come from standard nutritional analysis:

  • Protein: 4 calories per gram
  • Carbohydrates: 4 calories per gram
  • Fat: 9 calories per gram
  • Fiber: 2 calories per gram (soluble fiber is partially fermented by gut bacteria; insoluble fiber passes through undigested — the 2 cal/g is a consensus average used in most nutrition databases)

Multiply grams of each macro by its calorie value, and the total should roughly match the listed calorie count. It won't match perfectly — food labels round, databases average, and natural foods vary. A 10-20 calorie discrepancy per 100g is normal and not worth worrying about.

What This Calculator Can't Do

It can't track your daily intake. It can't set calorie goals. It can't scan barcodes. It can't log meals over time. Those features require accounts, databases, and ongoing maintenance — which is why every app that does them charges money or sells your data. This calculator does one thing: takes a food and a weight, and returns the macros. If you need daily tracking, use Cronometer (free, most accurate database) or MacroFactor (paid, best adherence algorithms). Use this calculator for quick lookups while you're cooking or meal-prepping.

Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is this calorie calculator?

Data is from USDA SR28 and NCCDB — the same sources used by major nutrition tracking apps. Accuracy is within typical food database range (±10-15% for mixed foods, ±5% for single ingredients). Natural variation in food means these are estimates, not guarantees.

Does cooking change calories?

No, but it changes weight (water loss). Weigh food raw for the most consistent results. If you must weigh cooked food, look for "cooked" entries in the database — the values account for water loss.

How do I track calories without a scale?

You can estimate with hand-size portions (palm = protein, fist = carbs, thumb = fats), but studies show 20-40% undercounting when estimating. A $12 food scale is the most effective calorie-tracking investment available.
