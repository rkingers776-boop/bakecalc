Feeding Ratios, Explained the Way I Wish Someone Had

A feeding ratio like 1:5:5 means starter : flour : water by weight — one part starter, five parts flour, five parts water. The ratio is the single biggest lever you have over your starter's schedule. Here's how to think about it:

  • 1:1:1 is the fast lane — equal weights of everything. Peaks in ~4 hours at 25°C. Use it for daily maintenance when your starter is strong, or when you're baking the same day.
  • 1:5:5 is the slow lane — five times more food than starter. Peaks in 10-12 hours, which lines up perfectly with an overnight feed: feed before bed, it's ready when you wake up.
  • 1:10:10 is fridge-revival territory — very little starter, lots of food, very gentle. Use it to bring a cold, acidic starter back to life.

Warmer kitchens need higher ratios (more food slows fermentation down); cooler kitchens can use lower ones. If your 1:1:1 starter peaks and collapses in 3 hours, it's hungry — step up to 1:3:3 or 1:5:5. Why Weight, Not Volume

A cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 110g (sifted) to 155g (packed) — a 40% difference that completely changes your ratio. Sourdough ratios are defined by weight precisely because volume is unreliable. If you're still using cups for your starter, switch to a scale for a week and you'll never go back. Peak-Time: Estimate It, Then Judge the Starter

This calculator estimates peak time using a Q10 model — fermentation rate roughly doubles per 10°C rise. It's a planning tool, not a promise: real timing shifts ±25% with starter health, flour protein, and humidity. The reliable signs of a peaked starter are the same ones bakers have used for a century:

  • Domed top that's just starting to recede
  • Visible bubbles on the surface and sides of the jar
  • Smell mildly tangy — like yogurt or ripe fruit, not vinegar or nail polish remover
  • Float test: a teaspoon dropped in water floats when there's enough trapped gas

Use the estimate to plan your day; use the starter's behavior to decide when it's actually ready.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a 1:1:1 sourdough feeding mean?

Equal weights of starter, flour, and water. 50g + 50g + 50g = 150g fed starter at 100% hydration. It's the standard daily feed — fast peak, slightly tangier flavor.

When should I use 1:5:5 instead of 1:1:1?

Use 1:5:5 for overnight feeds and building strength — it peaks in 10-12 hours instead of 4-5. Use 1:1:1 for daily maintenance of a strong starter. Warmer kitchens: higher ratios. Cooler kitchens: lower ratios.

How much starter should I keep when feeding?

25-100g is plenty for home baking — keep what your recipe needs plus a buffer. Enter your current jar weight in the calculator to get a discard estimate. Save the discard for pancakes, crackers, or creative baking.

My starter peaks too fast — what's wrong?

Nothing's wrong — it's hungry. If it peaks and collapses in under 4 hours at 1:1:1, step up to a higher ratio (1:3:3 or 1:5:5) to give it more food and slow the cycle down. Our sourdough troubleshooting guide covers this and eight other common problems.
