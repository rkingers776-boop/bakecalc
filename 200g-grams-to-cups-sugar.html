200 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

Two hundred grams of granulated sugar is exactly one cup. Among the conversions on this site it is the cleanest: no fraction, no spoon, no technique caveat worth mentioning.

The number is round because it had to be measured rather than derived, and measurement happened to land on 200. There is nothing structural about it — sugar is a dense crystalline solid, a cup of it settles predictably, and the average of many weighings is 200 g. The same process produced 120 for flour, a much less convenient figure, simply because flour is fluffier.

Why Sugar and Flour Disagree So Sharply

Both are dry ingredients measured in the same cup, and one weighs two thirds more than the other. The gap comes entirely from how much air is trapped between the particles:

  • A cup of all-purpose flour — 120 g, roughly two thirds air by volume
  • A cup of granulated sugar — 200 g, with crystals packed much more tightly
  • A cup of brown sugar, packed — 220 g, because moisture lets it compress
  • A cup of powdered sugar — 120 g unsifted, because the powder is fine and full of air

So the fact that both flour and powdered sugar land on 120 g per cup is a coincidence, not a category. And it is a trap in the kitchen: someone who learns that a cup is about 120 g from working with flour will badly undershoot when they apply that rule to granulated sugar, where a cup is 200.

Where 200 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • The standard layer cake — one cup of sugar is the backbone of most American butter cakes, usually paired with two to two and a half cups of flour.
  • Cookies — a single batch takes roughly one cup, often split between white and brown sugar.
  • Meringue — about four egg whites take 200 g of sugar, made into a syrup for an Italian meringue or added gradually for a French one.
  • Simple syrup — one cup of sugar to one cup of water by volume, the bartender's default.

What Happens if You Use the Wrong Sugar at This Weight

Because the weight is the fixed part and the volume is what changes, using the wrong sugar at 200 g moves the recipe in a direction people rarely anticipate. The weight of sugar stays the same, so the sweetness is unchanged; what changes is the volume those 200 g occupy, and therefore the ratio of sugar to the other ingredients by volume.

Substituting 200 g of packed brown sugar for 200 g of granulated puts less sugar into the bowl volumetrically — 0.9 cups against 1 cup. The effect is small and mostly harmless. Substituting 200 g of unsifted powdered sugar goes the other way and lands at 1.67 cups, which changes both the sweetness and the aeration, because powdered sugar carries cornstarch and behaves differently in a batter.

The safe habit is the one this whole series points at: decide whether the recipe is written in weights or volumes, and honour that choice. At 200 g in particular, the weight is the more reliable of the two figures for every sugar except the powdered kind, where a sifted cup is also exactly 100 g and therefore easy to reason about.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 200 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly one cup of granulated sugar. The figure is a round 200 grams because that is what repeated weighing of a spooned and levelled cup produces, and it makes this the simplest conversion in the pantry.

Q2: Is 200 g of sugar always one cup?

A2: Only for granulated white sugar. Brown sugar packed into a cup weighs about 220 grams, so 200 grams is 0.9 cups. Powdered sugar unsifted weighs 120 grams per cup, so 200 grams there is 1.67 cups.

Q3: Why is the sugar number so round when flour is 120?

A3: It is a coincidence of density rather than a rule. Granulated sugar is dense and its crystals settle predictably, arriving at 200 grams per cup; flour is fluffy and arrives at 120. Neither number was chosen, and both are simply what weighing produced.
