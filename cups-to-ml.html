Cups to mL — The "Standard Cup" That Isn't Standard At All

A cup is 236.6 mL. Or 240 mL. Or 250 mL. Or 200 mL. Depends who's writing the recipe. Here's how to tell which one they mean.

Milliliter Equivalent:

236.6 mL

Also = 8 fl oz · 16 tbsp

Fannie Farmer and the Invention of the Measuring Cup

Before 1896, American recipes didn't use "cups" in any standardized way. A cookbook might say "a cup of flour" and mean literally any teacup from your cupboard. Some teacups held 4 ounces, some held 8, some held 6. Recipes were written as rough suggestions, and cooking was an apprenticeship learned at your mother's elbow, not from precise written instructions.

Fannie Farmer changed that. Her 1896 Boston Cooking-School Cook Book introduced standardized measuring cups and spoons to American home cooking for the first time. She defined a cup as 8 fluid ounces, a tablespoon as 1/2 fluid ounce, and a teaspoon as 1/6 fluid ounce. This system — the US customary cup — is what almost every American recipe published since 1900 uses. It's 236.588 mL.

Farmer didn't invent the measurements. She borrowed them from apothecary measures and the existing fluid ounce system. What she did was popularize them and insist that home cooks buy standardized tools instead of grabbing whatever was in the cupboard. She was, in effect, America's first recipe standardization evangelist — and she succeeded so completely that we now take measuring cups for granted.

Four Different Cups — How to Tell Which One Your Recipe Means

Cup TypemLHow to Recognize It
US Customary236.6 mLRecipe is from a US cookbook or US-based website. Uses cups, tablespoons, teaspoons, ounces. "1 cup flour" in any American baking recipe means this cup.
US Legal (FDA)240 mLOnly on US nutrition labels. If the label says "1 cup (240 mL)," that's the FDA rounding. Not used in recipes.
Metric250 mLRecipe is from Canada, Australia, NZ, UK, South Africa, or most of Europe. Often paired with grams and mL measurements. "1 cup (250 mL)" or just "1 cup" in a metric cookbook.
Japanese200 mLRecipe is Japanese. Used in rice cookers (the plastic cup that comes with the cooker is 1 gō = 180 mL, not the same thing). Often uses the kanji 合 (gō) or explicitly says 200 mL.

When the Difference Actually Matters

The difference between a US cup (236.6 mL) and a metric cup (250 mL) is about 5.7%. For a single cup of milk in a pancake batter that serves four, this difference is undetectable. For bread dough where the hydration ratio needs to be within 2-3% for consistent results, a 5.7% error in your water measurement is significant.

Here's a concrete example: a basic sourdough recipe calls for 3 cups of water to 8 cups of flour (75% hydration). If you use metric cups (750 mL) instead of US cups (710 mL), you're adding an extra 40 mL of water — nearly 3 tablespoons. Your hydration jumps from 75% to 79%. The dough will be stickier, harder to shape, and the crumb will be more open (which might actually be what you want, but it won't match the recipe photo).

The Japanese cup difference is even bigger: 200 mL vs 236.6 mL is an 18% difference. A delicate Japanese cheesecake recipe using US cups will be completely different from the author's intention. This is why Japanese baking blogs almost always list grams — the cup is too ambiguous.

The Rice Cooker Cup — A Special Case

If you own a rice cooker, the plastic cup that came with it is probably marked in gō (合). One gō = 180 mL. This is a traditional Japanese unit for measuring rice, and it's different from both the Japanese cooking cup (200 mL) and the US cup (236.6 mL). One gō of uncooked rice makes about one bowl of cooked rice — it's a serving-based unit, not a volume-based one.

Never use the rice cooker cup for baking unless a recipe specifically calls for gō measurements. I have absolutely done this. The cake was not good.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many mL is 1 US cup?

Exactly 236.588 milliliters. Most recipes round to 237 mL or 240 mL. This is 8 US fluid ounces.

Why do different countries have different cup sizes?

The US cup (236.6 mL) comes from the US customary system (8 fl oz). The metric cup (250 mL) was an intentional simplification when Commonwealth countries adopted metric — 250 is a clean number divisible by 2, 4, 5, and 10. The Japanese cup (200 mL) predates Western contact. The FDA cup (240 mL) is a rounding convenience for nutrition labels.

Can I use a metric cup for American recipes?

For soups, stews, and casual cooking: yes. For bread and pastry where hydration ratios matter: no — the 5.7% difference will change your results. For best results when baking from US recipes, buy a set of US measuring cups ($8-12).
