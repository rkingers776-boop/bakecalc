50 g of butter in cups — the short answer

Fifty grams of butter is 0.22 cups, or about 3 and a half tablespoons. In American stick terms that is just under half a stick, since one stick weighs 113.4 grams.

Butter is the one baking ingredient where the cup measure is genuinely awkward, and it is worth understanding why before converting it. Solid butter does not pour and does not settle; press it into a cup and the volume depends entirely on how hard you pressed. That is why American butter is sold in sticks with the tablespoons printed on the wrapper.

The Wrapper Is the Measuring Tool

Every US butter stick is 113.4 g, which is 4 ounces, which is half a cup, which is 8 tablespoons. The wrapper is printed with tablespoon marks for exactly this reason: instead of converting grams to cups and cups to tablespoons, you cut the paper at the line you need.

For 50 g, that means cutting a stick just short of the halfway mark:

  • 1 stick — 113.4 g, 8 tbsp, 1/2 cup
  • 1/2 stick — 56.7 g, 4 tbsp, 1/4 cup
  • 50 g — about 0.88 of a half stick, or 3.5 tbsp

The gap between 50 g and half a stick is only 6.7 g — under a tablespoon. In most recipes that is close enough to treat as equivalent, and it is why 50 g and half a stick get used interchangeably even though they are not identical.

Where 50 g of Butter Shows Up

  • Finishing a pan sauce — a knob of cold butter stirred in at the end adds body and shine, and 50 g is generous for a sauce for four.
  • Greasing a tin — enough to coat a standard cake tin thoroughly, with some left over.
  • Small crumbles and toppings — rubbed into flour for a streusel or a short crust.
  • Browning — 50 g is the practical minimum for making brown butter without it scorching in a hot pan.

What Is Actually in Butter

US butter is required to be at least 80% butterfat, and most American butter sits close to that floor. The remainder is water, about 17 to 18%, plus a small amount of milk solids. Those proportions matter when you convert, because butter is not pure fat.

Fifty grams of butter carries roughly 40 g of fat and 9 g of water. That ratio is why substituting butter for oil is never a straight swap: oil is 100% fat, so matching the fat in 50 g of butter needs only 40 g of oil, and you still have to account for the water the butter would have brought.

European-style butter is churned to 82 to 85% fat, which leaves less water and gives a more workable dough for laminated pastry. In a croissant the difference is visible — more fat and less water means crisper layers and less shrinkage.

Replacing 50 g of Butter With Oil

Butter is not pure fat, so an oil substitution is not a one-for-one trade. US butter is at least 80% butterfat, which means 50 g contains about 40 g of fat, 9 g of water, and a trace of milk solids.

Matching the fat therefore takes 40 g of oil — roughly 3 tablespoons. The missing 9 g is the water the butter would have brought, and leaving it out makes the batter fractionally drier; a tablespoon of milk alongside the oil puts it back.

The trade is not symmetric in either direction. Oil is liquid at room temperature and coats flour proteins on contact, which holds gluten development back. Butter is solid and does not. So the oil version of a recipe gives a softer crumb that stays soft longer, while the butter version tastes of butter and holds structure better. Both are legitimate, and neither is a conversion of the other.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 50 g of butter?

A1: About 0.22 cups, or a little over three and a half tablespoons. Since a US cup of butter weighs 227 grams, 50 grams fills slightly more than a fifth of a cup.

Q2: How much is 50 g of butter in sticks?

A2: Just under half a stick. A standard US stick weighs 113.4 grams and measures half a cup, so 50 grams is about 44% of a stick. In practice that is the wrapped stick cut a little short of the midpoint.

Q3: Can I measure butter in a liquid measuring cup?

A3: It works for melted butter and not for solid. Solid butter leaves air gaps when pressed into a cup, so the volume depends on how firmly it was packed. The wrapper markings exist precisely to avoid that problem — each tablespoon is printed on the paper.
