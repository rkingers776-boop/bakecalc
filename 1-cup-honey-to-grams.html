1 Cup Honey to Grams — and Everything That Goes Wrong When You Treat It Like Sugar

340 grams. Not 200, not 236 — 340. That number is the reason your honey cake collapsed and your counter is sticky. Here's the whole story, including the substitution math nobody explains correctly.

Gram Equivalent:

340 grams (340 g)

*1 cup honey = 340g. Compare: 1 cup water = 236g, 1 cup sugar = 200g. Honey is dense — don't confuse volume with weight.

The Honey Cake Incident

The first time I tried to convert my grandmother's spice cake from sugar to honey, I did what seemed logical: measured 1 cup of honey (because the recipe called for 1 cup of sugar), creamed it with the butter, added the eggs and flour, and baked at 350°F. What came out of the oven 40 minutes later was not a cake. It was a dense, sunken, almost burnt-on-the-edges, raw-in-the-center disc that had bubbled over the sides of the pan and onto the oven floor, where it proceeded to carbonize into a substance I had to chisel off with a putty knife.

I had made every possible mistake at once. Let's count them: I used 340 grams of sweetener instead of 200 (honey is denser). I added 140 extra grams of liquid to the batter (honey is 17-20% water). I baked at the same temperature (honey browns faster). And I didn't add any baking soda to balance honey's acidity, so the leavening was completely off. Four separate failures, one bad cake, and a kitchen that smelled like burnt sugar for three days. The rest of this page is what I wish I'd known before I opened that honey jar.

340 Grams. Why?

Let's compare a measuring cup filled with three common baking ingredients:

  • 1 cup granulated sugar = 200 grams. Dry crystals, roughly 30% air space between granules. The crystals are dense but they don't fill the cup.
  • 1 cup water = 236 grams. Liquid, no air. The definition of a gram is based on water: 1 mL of water = 1 gram. A US cup is ~236.6 mL, hence 236 grams.
  • 1 cup honey = 340 grams. Liquid, no air, but the dissolved sugars make it far denser than water. Honey is a supersaturated solution — about 80% sugar dissolved in 20% water. That sugar adds mass without adding volume. The result: a fluid that weighs nearly half again as much as water and 70% more than granulated sugar in the same container.

This is the fundamental fact that makes honey substitution tricky. When a recipe calls for "1 cup honey," it's asking for nearly ¾ pound of dense liquid sweetener. When a recipe calls for "1 cup sugar," it's asking for 200 grams of dry crystals. They occupy the same space but have almost nothing else in common. The Two Solutions to Honey's Stickiness (Only One of Which Is Actually Good)

Pouring honey into a measuring cup is a three-part disaster: half the honey stays in the cup, your spatula becomes a sticky wand, and somehow — even though you were careful — there's honey on the counter. Every baker knows this dance. Every baker hates it.

The oil trick is the most commonly cited solution. Coat the inside of your measuring cup with a thin layer of neutral oil before adding honey. The honey slides out clean. It works. But you've now introduced a small amount of oil into your recipe — usually an immaterial amount, maybe a gram or two, but technically it's there. For a lean bread dough where every gram of fat matters, this matters. For cookies, it doesn't.

The scale method is the better solution. Put your mixing bowl on the scale. Press tare (zero). Pour honey directly from the jar into the bowl until the display reads your target weight. 1 cup = 340g. ¾ cup = 255g. ½ cup = 170g. ¼ cup = 85g. 1 tablespoon = 21g. No measuring cup. No spatula. No oil. No waste. The honey goes exactly where it needs to go, in exactly the right amount, and the only thing that needs washing is the mixing bowl — which you were going to wash anyway. I switched to the scale method three years ago and have not once regretted it. Bonus trick for stubborn jars: if your honey is thick and slow, put the sealed jar in a bowl of hot tap water for 5 minutes. The honey thins dramatically as it warms and pours like a much lighter liquid. Don't microwave the jar — most honey containers have a metal lid or a foil seal you forgot about, and metal in the microwave is a lesson you only need to learn once.

Not All Honey Bakes the Same

Supermarket honey is almost always clover honey — it's the most abundant, most affordable variety, and it has a mild, crowd-pleasing sweetness that works in everything. But honey is one of the few baking ingredients where the variety genuinely changes the final product in noticeable ways. If you're making honey the star of a recipe (honey cake, honey cookies, honey glaze), the varietal matters. Here's a quick field guide:

For recipes with 2+ cups of honey (honey-heavy gingerbread, certain Eastern European honey cakes), those small differences compound. When in doubt, weigh.

Crystallized Honey: Yes, You Can Still Use It

Honey crystallizes — it's not spoiled, it's just physics. The glucose in honey spontaneously precipitates out of the supersaturated solution and forms solid crystals, turning the honey grainy and eventually solid. Some honeys crystallize within weeks (clover); others stay liquid for years (acacia, tupelo). Crystallization is actually a sign of quality — it means the honey hasn't been overheated and ultra-filtered to delay the inevitable.

For baking, you have two options with crystallized honey. Option 1: measure it by weight as-is. The crystals will dissolve during baking — you won't find crunchy sugar bits in your finished cake. The water content and sugar concentration are unchanged; only the physical state is different. Option 2: gently reliquefy it. Put the open jar in a pot of warm water (not boiling — keep it under 110°F / 43°C) and stir occasionally. In 15-20 minutes the crystals will dissolve without destroying the volatile aromatic compounds that define the honey's flavor. Do not microwave crystallized honey to death — high heat breaks down those aromatics and you end up with sweet liquid that technically used to be honey but no longer tastes like anything in particular.

One exception: if you're making a no-bake recipe (honey mousse, honey whipped cream, a honey drizzle), reliquefy crystallized honey first. Undissolved crystals in a cold preparation stay crunchy, and "honey crunch" is not the texture experience anyone wants from a mousse.

Honey + Baking Soda: Why This Pairing Keeps Showing Up

If you look at honey cake recipes, you'll notice nearly all of them contain baking soda — even ones without other acidic ingredients like buttermilk or sour cream. That's because honey itself is acidic. Its pH ranges from about 3.4 to 6.1 depending on the varietal, with most honeys falling in the 3.9-4.5 range — roughly the same acidity as a mild vinegar solution. That acidity does two things in your batter: it reacts with baking soda to produce carbon dioxide (leavening), and it tenderizes gluten (soft crumb).

When you swap honey for sugar in a recipe that originally used baking powder (which contains its own acid and doesn't need external acidity), the honey adds acid that the recipe didn't account for. The extra acid can over-activate the baking powder, causing a rapid rise followed by a collapse when the structure can't hold. The fix: for every cup of honey you use as a sugar replacement, add ¼ teaspoon of baking soda — even if the original recipe didn't call for it. The soda neutralizes the excess acid and provides a steadier, more controlled rise. Things People Ask After Their First Honey Baking Disaster

How many grams in 1 cup of honey?

340 grams for standard liquid honey (clover, orange blossom, wildflower). Very dark, dense honeys like buckwheat or manuka can be slightly heavier at about 350g/cup. Creamed or whipped honey — which has air beaten into it — is lighter at about 300g/cup. A tablespoon of liquid honey is 21 grams; a teaspoon is 7 grams. For comparison: 1 cup sugar = 200g, and 1 cup brown sugar = 200g packed. Honey at 340g/cup is in a different weight class entirely, which is why you can't just do a 1:1 volume swap.

How do I swap honey for sugar without ruining the recipe?

Four adjustments, all required: (1) Use ¾ the amount by weight — 255g honey replaces 200g sugar. (2) Reduce total liquid in the recipe by ¼ cup per cup of honey — honey is about 20% water. (3) Drop the oven temperature by 25°F — honey caramelizes and browns faster than sugar. (4) Add ¼ teaspoon baking soda per cup of honey if the recipe has none — honey's acidity needs neutralizing for proper rise. If this sounds like a lot, it is. Converting sugar recipes to honey is genuinely a development process, not a direct swap. The full guide at baking with honey instead of sugar walks through each step with recipe examples.

Why does my honey cake always come out too dark on top?

Honey contains fructose, which caramelizes at a lower temperature than sucrose (the sugar in granulated sugar). A honey cake at 350°F will brown significantly faster on top than a sugar cake at the same temperature. Fix: tent loosely with aluminum foil after the first 20-25 minutes of baking. Don't seal it tight — you still want moisture to escape — just a loose tent that blocks direct radiant heat from the top element. Combined with the 25°F temperature reduction from the substitution rules above, this should give you an evenly browned top. Also check your oven temperature — if your oven runs even 15°F hot, honey-based bakes will show it in the color before anything else.

Is raw honey different from regular honey for baking?

By weight and sugar content, raw honey and regular (pasteurized) honey are essentially identical — both weigh about 340g/cup. The difference is in the living contents: raw honey still contains pollen, enzymes (like glucose oxidase), and sometimes tiny wax particles. In theory, these add flavor complexity. In practice, the heat of baking destroys the enzymes and volatilizes most of the delicate aromatics that distinguish raw from processed honey. My take: don't spend extra on raw honey for a baked recipe where it'll spend 40 minutes at 350°F. Save the raw honey for tea, toast, yogurt, and salad dressing — applications where you'll actually taste the difference. For baked goods, standard clover honey performs identically at a fraction of the price.
