170 Grams in Half a Cup — The Most Deceptive Measurement in Baking

1/2 cup of standard liquid honey weighs 170 grams. Raw, unfiltered honey runs a bit heavier — about 175g per half cup. Crystallized honey that's been gently warmed back to liquid sits around 173g. And Manuka honey, which is denser and more viscous than your grocery-store clover honey, tips the scale at 180g per half cup. That is a 10-gram range for what looks like the "same" ingredient. The real headline, though, has nothing to do with honey types: compare 170 grams of honey to 60 grams of flour or 100 grams of granulated sugar — same half-cup measure. Honey is 2.8 times heavier than flour by volume. If you are swapping honey for sugar in a muffin recipe and measuring by cup thinking "sweet is sweet," you have just dumped an extra 70 grams of material into your batter. That is why the muffins came out dense and gummy. Not your fault — you were measuring the wrong thing.

The Conversion Formula

  • Grams = Cups × 340 (standard liquid honey)
  • 1/2 cup honey (standard) = 170 grams
  • 1/2 cup raw/unfiltered = 175 grams
  • 1 Tbsp honey = 21.25 grams (340 ÷ 16)
  • 1/2 cup honey in a sugar-for-honey swap: use 75g of honey for every 100g of sugar (honey is sweeter + adds liquid)

The Oil-the-Cup Trick (and Why You Should Just Use a Scale)

Scraping honey out of a measuring cup is one of life's minor frustrations — right up there with peeling hard-boiled eggs and finding the end of the packing tape. The hack that actually works: before you pour the honey, coat the inside of your dry measuring cup with a thin film of neutral oil — canola, vegetable, whatever you have. Rub it around with a paper towel so there is a barely-visible sheen. The honey hits the oil barrier and slides out like a water slide, leaving virtually nothing behind. I learned this from a pastry chef who measured honey by the quart for a living. But here is the real tip: skip the cup entirely. Put your mixing bowl on a scale. Tare it. Pour honey straight from the jar until it reads 170g. The oil trick is brilliant. The scale trick is better. No cleanup, no waste, and you do not even need to wash a measuring cup.

Honey + Baking Soda: The Chemistry You Did Not Know You Were Doing

Honey is acidic — pH around 3.9, similar to orange juice. Baking soda is alkaline. Put them together in a batter and you get carbon dioxide bubbles — the same reaction you get from buttermilk and baking soda, just from a different acid source. This is not trivia. It means that when a honey-based recipe calls for baking soda even though there is no buttermilk, yogurt, or vinegar in the ingredient list, the recipe is not broken — the honey is the acid. That baking soda is giving your honey cake its lift. It is also neutralizing some of the honey's acidity, which affects browning. (Honey's natural acidity plus its fructose content mean honey-sweetened baked goods brown faster and darker than sugar-sweetened ones — if your honey cornbread looks done at 18 minutes when the recipe says 22, trust your eyes, not the timer.)

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/2 cup of honey?

A1: 170 grams for standard liquid honey — nearly double what 1/2 cup of sugar weighs (100g). Raw or unfiltered honey is slightly denser at about 175g. That 70g gap between honey and sugar per half cup is why substituting honey for sugar by volume in a recipe — instead of by weight — will throw off your liquid-to-dry ratio and produce something dense and gummy.

Q2: Does crystallized honey weigh the same as liquid honey per half cup?

A2: Close enough that you will not notice in any real recipe. Warmed and re-liquefied crystallized honey is about 173g per half cup — 3g heavier than honey that has never crystallized. The tiny bump likely comes from a bit of water evaporating during gentle warming. Use 170g as your number. If you are baking something extremely precise like macarons (unlikely with honey, but you never know), weigh it.

Q3: How do I measure half a cup of honey without making a sticky mess?

A3: Coat the measuring cup with a thin layer of neutral oil (canola, vegetable). The honey will slide out cleanly — no scraping, no residue. Even better: skip the cup. Put your mixing bowl on a kitchen scale, press tare, and pour honey straight from the jar until the display reads 170g. Zero cleanup and perfect accuracy. This is the method I use every single time and I have not washed a honey-coated measuring cup in years.
