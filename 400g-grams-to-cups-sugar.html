400 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

Four hundred grams of granulated sugar is exactly 2 cups. It is a round figure in both systems, and it is the quantity most butter cake recipes pair with an equal weight of butter.

At this scale the sugar stops being a flavouring and starts being a piece of equipment. Four hundred grams is enough to cream into butter for a two-layer cake, and the creaming step is where sugar does its most underrated work.

What Creaming Actually Does

Creaming is the step where soft butter and sugar are beaten together until pale and fluffy. It is usually described as mixing, which undersells it. Sugar crystals have sharp edges, and as they are driven through the butter they cut into the fat and open thousands of small pockets. Air fills those pockets, and the mixture visibly lightens as a result — that change in colour is air being incorporated, and it is the first leavening a cake gets.

Everything that follows depends on how well that step went:

  • Butter too cold — the crystals cannot cut properly and the mixture stays grainy. Less air, denser cake.
  • Butter too warm — it holds no structure, the pockets collapse as fast as they form, and the batter greases rather than aerates.
  • Under-creamed — the mixture looks sandy after three minutes. The lift will come entirely from chemical leaveners, giving a coarser crumb.
  • Well creamed — pale, noticeably increased in volume, and holding a ridge when the beater is lifted.

Room temperature butter, around 18 to 21 °C, is the condition creaming needs. It is a narrow window, and it is the reason cakes fail more often at this step than at any other.

Where 400 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • Two-layer cakes — 400 g of sugar with 400 g of butter is the standard cream-and-sugar pairing.
  • Large cookie batches — three to four dozen, depending on size.
  • Fruit cakes and dense sponges — where sugar is high relative to flour by design.
  • Jam making — a large batch, at roughly equal weight to the fruit.

Why Crystal Size Matters at This Quantity

Four hundred grams is enough sugar that the texture of the crystals affects the result, not just the amount. Superfine or caster sugar has smaller crystals with more edges per gram, so it creams faster and produces a finer, more even crumb. Standard granulated works and takes longer. Coarse or turbinado sugar cuts larger, more irregular pockets and leaves a looser, more rustic texture — pleasant in a muffin, coarse in a sponge.

The one sugar that cannot be creamed is powdered sugar. Its particles are too fine to cut into butter and it carries cornstarch, which absorbs liquid and would make the batter stiff. It belongs in frostings and glazes, where it is mixed into fat that is already soft and needs no aeration.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 400 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly two cups of granulated sugar. Sugar measures 200 grams per cup, so 400 divides into a whole number of measures with nothing left over.

Q2: Why do cake recipes cream sugar and butter together?

A2: The sugar crystals are sharp-edged and cut into the butter as it is beaten, opening tiny pockets that fill with air. Those air pockets expand in the oven and give the cake its lift. Creaming is an aeration step, and the sugar is the tool that performs it.

Q3: Does the type of sugar affect creaming?

A3: Yes. Fine granulated sugar creams faster and traps air more evenly. Coarse or raw sugars cut larger, irregular pockets and give a looser crumb. Powdered sugar contains cornstarch and is not suitable for creaming at all, because the particles are too fine to cut into the fat.
