1 Cup Butter to Grams Converter

Convert 1 US customary cup of butter into grams — covers stick, melted, and whipped butter accurately.

Gram Equivalent:

227 grams (227 g)

*Calculated using standard US butter densities. 1 cup solid butter = 2 sticks = 16 tbsp.

How Many Grams in 1 Cup of Butter?

In American baking, 1 cup of butter = 227 grams = 2 sticks = 16 tablespoons. No other ingredient has a conversion this exact, and the reason is regulatory rather than culinary: American butter is sold in quarter-pound sticks with a legally defined weight and tablespoon markings printed on the wrapper. There is no measuring technique that can throw it off. If you own a scale, you can still skip it and read the wrapper instead — every stick is 113 grams, so two sticks is 227 and half a stick is 57.

That certainty only covers solid butter in its standard form. Melted butter settles differently in the cup, whipped butter is pumped full of air, and clarified butter has had its water and milk solids taken out. Each one changes what a cup weighs, and the whipped case changes it enough to wreck a cake.

The Conversion Formula (Cups → Grams)

Multiply cups by grams per cup for your butter's state:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • Standard Stick Butter: 1 × 227 g = 227 grams (exactly 2 sticks)
  • Melted Butter: 1 × 240 g = 240 grams
  • Whipped Butter: 1 × 150 g = 150 grams
  • Clarified Butter / Ghee: 1 × 205 g = 205 grams

This is the one conversion in baking where you can skip the arithmetic entirely. Two sticks is a cup. One stick is half a cup. A quarter stick — the paper says so, right on the wrapper — is a tablespoon. The sticks were the original measuring tool; the scale just confirms what the packaging already told you.

Solid, Melted, or Whipped: Three Different Cups

The three butter states don't measure alike, and the gap between them is wider than most people expect.

  • Solid stick butter — 227 g per cup. Pressed into the measure, it leaves small voids between the surface of the butter and the walls of the cup.
  • Melted butter — about 240 g per cup. As a liquid it fills those voids completely, so the same volume holds more butter.
  • Whipped butter — about 150 g per cup, and some brands come in under 120. Whipped butter is aerated on purpose, so a cup of it is largely air. Substituting it one-for-one for stick butter is one of the most reliable ways to get a greasy, flat cake.

The practical rule: when a recipe says "1 cup butter," it means solid stick butter unless the word "melted" appears. And when it says "1 cup melted butter," melt first and then measure — measuring solid and melting afterwards leaves a different amount in the bowl.

How Much of Your Butter Isn't Butter

Standard American butter has to be at least 80% milkfat. The rest is water — up to 16% — plus a little milk solids and, if it's salted, salt. Run the numbers and a 227-gram cup of butter carries roughly 36 grams of water. That water isn't incidental: it counts as liquid in the recipe, and it's the reason butter behaves so differently from shortening or oil measured to the same volume.

European-style butter changes the balance. It's churned to 82–85% milkfat, which means less water per gram of butter. In laminated dough — croissants, puff pastry, Danish — that lower water content is the entire point. Less steam during baking means crisper layers and less shrinkage. If you swap European butter for standard butter in a cake recipe, keep the weight identical and expect a slightly richer, slightly drier result. In pastry, make the swap deliberately and enjoy it.

Salted vs Unsalted: The Hidden Three Grams

American salted butter carries roughly 1.5 to 2% salt by weight. In a standard stick that's about 1.8 grams of salt; in a full cup of two sticks, about 3.7 grams — close to two-thirds of a teaspoon.

Four grams of salt sounds trivial until you check what the recipe already asked for. A typical cookie or cake formula calls for half a teaspoon of salt in total. Bake with salted butter and you've added more salt than the recipe intended before you even reach for the shaker, and there's no way to take it back out. This is why professional recipes specify unsalted butter almost universally: it lets the formula control salt exactly, and it keeps the water content consistent from brand to brand.

Butter Temperature Matters More Than the Gram Count

Once the weight is right, temperature becomes the next variable — and in cakes and cookies it matters more than a few grams either way.

  • Cold, straight from the fridge — too hard to cream, and exactly what you want for pie dough and biscuits, where cold fat flakes create steam pockets and flaky layers.
  • Room temperature, about 18–21 °C — the creaming range. At this temperature butter traps air when beaten, and those trapped air cells are what leaven a butter cake. Push past about 24 °C and the butter slumps and loses the air you just beat into it.
  • Melted, above 30 °C — no air-holding capacity at all. Melted butter gives dense, chewy cookies and flat cakes. Sometimes that's exactly right, as in brown butter cookies or financiers, and completely wrong when the recipe was built around creaming.

A faster test than a thermometer: press the butter with a fingertip. It should dent easily without squashing flat, and your finger should come away greasy rather than coated.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of butter?

A1: 227 grams of solid stick butter — exactly 2 sticks or 16 tablespoons. American butter is the one baking ingredient with a legally defined package weight, so you can skip the scale entirely and count sticks instead.

Q2: Does melted butter weigh the same as solid butter per cup?

A2: No. A cup of melted butter weighs about 240 grams against 227 for solid, because liquid fills the voids that solid butter leaves against the sides of the measuring cup. Melt first and then measure, exactly as the recipe asks — and don't assume the two cups are interchangeable.

Q3: How many sticks of butter equal 1 cup?

A3: Two sticks. Each American stick is a quarter pound — 113 grams, 8 tablespoons, or half a cup — with tablespoon markings printed along the wrapper. A half stick is 57 grams and a quarter stick is 28 grams, so you can cut down a recipe without ever touching a scale.
