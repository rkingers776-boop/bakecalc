10 oz of water in mL — the short answer

Ten US fluid ounces of water is 295.74 mL — 10 × 29.5735 = 295.7350. Because it is water, it also weighs 295.74 grams. Under the imperial fluid ounce the same measure is 284.13 mL.

In cup terms it is 1¼ cups, which is an awkward fraction to measure: the standard set has a quarter cup, so building 1¼ means a full cup plus a quarter. That is easy enough with two measures, and impossible to do in one.

Water Is the Fixed Point Everything Else Is Measured Against

The reason a millilitre of water weighs a gram is that the metric system was defined that way: the original kilogram was the mass of a litre of water at its temperature of maximum density. Every other ingredient's density is expressed relative to that, which is why water keeps turning up as the baseline in baking arithmetic.

In practice this gives water a property no other ingredient has — a scale and a measuring jug agree. If a bread formula calls for 296 g of water, you can weigh it or pour it and get the same dough. Try the same trick with honey and you will be out by 40%.

Ten Ounces in the World of Bottles

Ten fluid ounces is a common packaging size, which is why this conversion gets looked up by people reading a bottle rather than a recipe:

  • Water bottles — 10 fl oz is a standard small bottle, just under 300 mL.
  • Baby bottles — 10 oz is a large feed, and one of the printed marks on the side.
  • Coffee — a large mug or a small takeaway cup sits near 10 fl oz.
  • Sports bottles — 10 oz handheld bottles are sold for running.

What Water Does in a Bake

Water is easy to overlook as an ingredient because it is invisible in the finished product, but it is doing three jobs at once. It hydrates flour proteins so they can form gluten. It is the solvent that lets yeast ferment and enzymes convert starch. And in laminated doughs and choux it turns to steam, which is what actually creates the lift.

That is why the quantity is set by weight in any serious formula. Hydration is expressed as a percentage of flour weight, so 10 fl oz of water against 400 g of flour is a 74% hydration dough, while the same 10 fl oz against 500 g of flour is 59%. Identical water, radically different bread — and the difference is entirely in the ratio, not the volume.

Swapping Water for Something Else

Water is the neutral choice. Anything substituted for it brings additional solids and changes the outcome, so a straight volume swap is rarely a fair trade:

  • Milk — adds fat, protein and lactose. The proteins and sugars brown faster, so expect a darker crust at the same oven setting.
  • Buttermilk — adds acid, which reacts with baking soda and gives a finer, more tender crumb.
  • Juice — adds sugar, which feeds yeast faster and browns sooner.
  • Egg — not a liquid substitution at all; it contributes structure and fat that water cannot replace.

If you swap, do it by weight and expect to adjust the flour. Water leaves the dough entirely in the oven; milk and juice leave solids behind, and those solids change the texture whether or not the volume was right.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many mL is 10 oz of water?

A1: 295.74 mL for 10 US fluid ounces, which also weighs 295.74 grams. Water is the one liquid where the millilitre and the gram give the same reading.

Q2: How many cups is 10 oz of water?

A2: One and a quarter cups. A US cup is 8 fluid ounces, so 10 fluid ounces is 1.25 cups, or 20 tablespoons.

Q3: Is 10 oz of water the same as 300 mL?

A3: Not quite — 300 mL is 1.4% more than 295.74 mL. The rounding is harmless in most cooking, but it matters when water is being added to a bread dough, where hydration is calculated as a percentage of flour weight and small errors accumulate.
