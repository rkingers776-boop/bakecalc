A Cake Does Not Have One Serving Count

The same 8-inch round cake is either a 20-person cake or a 24-person cake, and nothing about the cake changes. Only the knife does. A party slice runs about 1½ × 2 inches — dessert on its own plate, the way you would serve it at a birthday. A wedding slice is about 1 × 2 inches, cut for a long guest list where cake arrives alongside everything else on the table.

That single detail causes more friction between home bakers and customers than any other question about cake. Two bakers can quote the same 10-inch cake at very different prices per serving and both be right, because one is quoting party slices and the other is quoting wedding slices. Settle the cut before you quote a number, and write it on the order.

The calculator above shows you both numbers for whatever pan you pick. If you are pricing the cake rather than baking it, take that count into the cost calculator — cost per serving is only honest when the serving count is honest.

Layers Change Height, Not the Count

A two-layer 8-inch cake is twice as tall as a single layer, not twice as wide. You cut one wedge through the whole stack, so the number of pieces comes from the area of the pan’s top surface, and the height only decides how generous each piece feels.

Wilton puts a number on this: a 6-inch round cake that measures 6 inches high yields the same servings as a 6-inch round that measures 3 inches high. Both are 12. The rule cuts off below 3 inches — a cake shorter than that yields half the servings listed for its pan, because a 2-inch-tall slice of a single layer is too short to plate as dessert. That is the only height rule that changes the count.

Square Beats Round at Every Size

Round pans leave curved edges that cannot be cut into full slices without waste. Square pans have no waste. At small sizes the gap is invisible, and then it widens:

  • An 8-inch round gives 20 party servings; an 8-inch square gives 20 as well, but 32 wedding servings against the round’s 24.
  • A 10-inch round gives 28 party servings. A 10-inch square gives 30.
  • A 16-inch round gives 77 party servings. A 16-inch square gives 80 — and 128 wedding servings against 100.

If a customer is choosing between a 10-inch round and a 10-inch square for the same price, the square feeds two more people and cuts into even squares, which matters when someone is plating 30 portions in a hurry.

Sheet Pans: The Number Nobody Believes

A 9 × 13 sheet cake is 36 party servings or 50 wedding servings in the chart. Cut that pan into a strict 2 × 2 inch grid and you get about 24 pieces. Both numbers are real; they describe different cuts. The chart’s party figure works out to roughly 3¼ square inches per portion, which is a bakery-style slice, slightly smaller than the 1½ × 2 inch cut used for round pans.

This is why sheet cakes are the cheapest way to feed a crowd and why the per-serving price on them looks impossibly low. If you are quoting a sheet, say which grid you will cut. “36 pieces” and “24 pieces” from the same pan are a 50% difference in perceived value.

How Much Batter Does the Pan Take?

The cups column in the chart is batter for one 2-inch layer, not for the finished cake. An 8-inch round takes 4 cups per layer, so a two-layer cake needs 8 cups. A 9 × 13 sheet takes 10 cups for a single 2-inch layer — double it only if you are building a two-layer sheet cake, which most people are not.

Do not confuse batter with pan capacity. An 8-inch round pan holds about 7 cups if you filled it to the rim; you fill it halfway to two-thirds because the batter has to rise. That is why 4 cups of batter belongs in a 7-cup pan. How Much Frosting?

Frosting is the number people underestimate, then run out of halfway around the cake. Per Wilton: a two-layer 8-inch round takes about 3½ cups to ice and decorate; a 9 × 13 sheet takes about 7 cups; a 10-inch round takes 5 cups; a 12-inch round takes 6. Larger pans scale faster than you expect because the side surface grows with the diameter.

Those figures assume a medium-consistency buttercream at normal thickness. A loose American buttercream spreads thinner and covers more area per cup; a stiff ganache or a Swiss meringue piped into borders eats more. Batch sizes are the practical answer: most home buttercream recipes make about 3 cups, so a 9 × 13 sheet is roughly one and a half batches. Deep pans hold more batter and bake longer, and Wilton publishes a separate table for them. They are filled only halfway, and the finished cake is one layer torted and filled to reach 3 inches. What the Chart Does Not Cover

  • Stacked tiers. A three-tier cake is not one pan, and stacking covers the top of each tier below the board. Treat each tier separately and expect slightly fewer usable portions on the covered ones.
  • Novelty and carved cakes. Carving wastes cake, so a carved design serves fewer than the pan it started in.
  • Cupcakes. A standard cupcake takes about 1/4 cup of batter. A two-layer 8-inch recipe, roughly 8 cups, makes about 32.
  • Deep pans of unusual shapes. Wilton publishes deep-pan figures for round pans only. For square or novelty deep pans, weigh the batter and compare against the pan’s capacity rather than trusting a serving column.
  • Bundt and tube pans. They are measured by capacity in cups, not by diameter, and servings depend on how deep you cut the wedges.

Where These Numbers Come From

Serving counts, batter volumes, frosting amounts, oven temperatures and bake times are all taken from Wilton’s published cake serving guide, which reflects their test kitchen’s cuts: party slices about 1½ × 2 inches, wedding slices about 1 × 2 inches. The chart’s own note is worth repeating: serving counts are a guide, and actual results vary with the person holding the knife.

Pan capacity figures elsewhere on this site are geometric and calculated separately. If you need to know what a pan holds rather than what batter a recipe should put in it, use the pan size converter.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many servings are in a 9x13 sheet cake?

36 party servings or 50 wedding servings, following Wilton’s published chart. That assumes a 2-inch layer and the standard cuts: about 1 1/2 x 2 inches for a party, 1 x 2 inches for a wedding. Cut a strict 2 x 2 inch grid instead and you get about 24 pieces, so agree on the cut before you quote a number.

How many servings does an 8-inch round cake serve?

20 party servings or 24 wedding servings for a two-layer, 4-inch-high cake. The count comes from the top surface, not the height, so a taller 8-inch cake serves the same number as long as it is at least 3 inches tall. Below 3 inches, halve it.

How much cake do I need for 100 guests?

Three 9x13 sheets at the party cut, which is 108 servings, or two sheets at the wedding cut, which is exactly 100. If you would rather bake rounds, it takes five 10-inch rounds at 28 party servings each and you will have leftovers.

Do more layers mean more servings?

No. You cut each slice through the whole stack, so servings come from the pan’s top surface and the cut size. A 6-inch round that is 3 inches tall and one that is 6 inches tall both serve 12. The only height rule that changes the count is the 3-inch mark: shorter cakes are cut in half.

How much frosting do I need for a two-layer 8-inch cake?

About 3 1/2 cups to ice and decorate. A 9x13 sheet takes around 7 cups. Consistency matters as much as volume: a loose American buttercream covers more area per cup than a stiff one, so make a little extra rather than exactly enough.
