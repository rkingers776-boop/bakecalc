100 g of brown sugar in cups — the short answer

One hundred grams of brown sugar is 0.45 cups when it is firmly packed, which is a scant half cup — half a cup less about three quarters of a tablespoon, or roughly 7 level tablespoons. At 220 grams per packed cup, that is the whole calculation.

But the phrase "when it is firmly packed" is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and it is worth unpacking — the word is unfortunately apt.

A Scant Half Cup, and Why Recipes Write 1/2 Anyway

Half a cup of packed brown sugar weighs 110 g. So 100 g is not half a cup; it is half a cup with three quarters of a tablespoon missing. In a cookie dough that 10 g gap is invisible. In a recipe where the brown sugar is the only sugar and the ratio against butter is doing structural work, it is small but real.

This is why volume-written recipes tend to round. A developer converting a 100 g original into cups will usually write "1/2 cup packed" and accept the 10% error, because no measuring cup set contains a 0.45 cup and most bakers would rather scoop a half cup than spoon out seven tablespoons and then three quarters of one more.

If you have a scale, the argument is over. If you do not, the seven level tablespoons are more accurate than the half cup, and they take perhaps twenty extra seconds.

Packed Against Loosely Spooned — a 24% Swing

Brown sugar is the most technique-sensitive sugar in the pantry, because the sugar itself is compressible. The molasses film on each crystal is sticky, and pressure squeezes the crystals together into whatever space is available.

  • Packed firmly into the cup — 220 g per cup, so 100 g is 0.45 cups
  • Spooned in and levelled — 180 g per cup, so 100 g is 0.56 cups
  • Poured straight from the bag — around 160 g per cup, so 100 g is 0.63 cups

Between the firmest and the loosest, the same 100 g changes volume by 40%. No other sugar comes close. Granulated sugar varies by barely 1%, and even flour, for all its reputation, only swings about 20%.

Packed has a precise meaning, incidentally: press the sugar into the measure with the back of a spoon until the block holds the shape of the cup when you turn it out. If it slumps, it was not packed.

Why Brown Sugar Packs at All

Brown sugar is granulated sugar with molasses left in or added back. Light brown runs about 3.5% molasses by weight and dark about 6.5%, which sounds like a small difference and completely changes the behaviour — molasses is thick, dark, and slightly acidic, and it forms a film around every crystal.

That film is why the sugar clumps, why it holds a shape when pressed, and why it dries into a brick if you leave the bag open. The molasses is hygroscopic: it pulls water out of the air when the air is humid and gives it up when the air is dry. A bag left open in a dry kitchen loses its moisture, the crystals fuse at the contact points, and what was a soft scoop becomes a single hard mass.

Measuring 100 g Without a Scale

  • With a measuring cup — fill the half cup, pack it firmly, then remove about three quarters of a tablespoon by eye. This is approximate and fine for cookies.
  • With a tablespoon — seven level tablespoons, lightly pressed into the spoon rather than heaped. This is the more reliable of the two.
  • With a scale — 100 g, no packing decision required, because packing does not change mass.

The last point is the one worth remembering. Every argument about packing is an argument about volume. Weigh the sugar and the entire question evaporates, which is why professional recipes specify brown sugar in grams.

Light, Dark, and Muscovado at 100 g

  • Light brown sugar — 100 g, about 3.5 g of molasses, a mild caramel note.
  • Dark brown sugar — 100 g, about 6.5 g of molasses, a stronger, slightly bitter treacle edge.
  • Muscovado — 100 g, more molasses still and a much wetter texture, which makes it pack tighter and measure heavier.
  • Coconut sugar — 100 g, a different product entirely, drier and darker, and not a like-for-like swap.

Light and dark have essentially the same density, so 100 g converts to the same volume either way. Muscovado is the exception: it is wet enough to compress further than either, so a cup of it can weigh 250 g or more.

What 100 g of Brown Sugar Does in a Recipe

At this quantity the sugar is usually a supporting player rather than the main sweetener — a little in a marinade, a spoonful in a rub, a partial swap in a muffin. What it brings that granulated sugar cannot:

  • Moisture — the molasses holds water, and baked goods made with brown sugar stay soft for days longer.
  • Acidity — molasses is acidic, so brown sugar nudges a batter's pH down, which is why recipes leaning on it often specify baking soda rather than baking powder.
  • Colour and flavour — caramel and a faint bitterness, strongest in the crust.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 100g of brown sugar?

A1: 0.45 cups when the sugar is firmly packed, which is a scant half cup, or about 7 level tablespoons. Packed brown sugar is taken as 220 grams per cup.

Q2: Is 100g of brown sugar half a cup?

A2: Not quite. Half a cup of packed brown sugar weighs 110 grams, so 100 grams comes up about three quarters of a tablespoon short of the half cup mark.

Q3: What happens if I do not pack the brown sugar?

A3: A cup of loosely spooned brown sugar weighs about 180 grams rather than 220. So 100 grams spooned loose takes 0.56 cups instead of 0.45 — roughly a fifth more volume for the same weight.
