Sixty Grams or Fifty? The Powdered Sugar Fork in the Road

1/2 cup of unsifted powdered sugar weighs 60 grams. Sift it first, then measure, and that same half cup weighs closer to 50 grams. A 20-gram difference from a single action. That is not a rounding error — it is a ~17% swing in the amount of sugar going into your bowl, and it's why your grandmother's frosting recipe that says "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" behaves nothing like your friend's recipe that just says "1 cup powdered sugar." They are not the same recipe. They are not even close.

Here is what's happening: powdered sugar (also called confectioners sugar in the US and icing sugar in the UK, Australia, and Canada — same stuff) is granulated sugar ground to a fine powder with 3-5% cornstarch added to stop it from turning into a solid brick. Those particles are microscopic. Left to sit, they settle and compress like fine dust. A cup scooped straight from the bag is dense — about 120 grams. Put that sugar through a sieve, and you have just filled the gaps between particles with air. The same physical cup now holds maybe 50 grams of actual sugar and roughly 17% more air. If you were baking a cake, 20 grams of extra sugar might go unnoticed. In a buttercream, which is often just butter, powdered sugar, and a splash of liquid? Ten extra grams of sugar absorbs moisture, stiffens the mixture, and suddenly your "spreadable frosting" is a crumbly paste that won't stick to the cake.

The Conversion Formula

  • Grams = Cups × 120 (unsifted) or Cups × 100 (sifted)
  • 1/2 cup unsifted powdered sugar = 60 grams
  • 1/2 cup sifted then measured = 50 grams
  • 1/2 cup scooping after sifting = ~70 grams
  • 1 Tbsp unsifted powdered sugar = 7.5 grams (120 ÷ 16)

Why Your Buttercream Keeps Splitting

Here is a buttercream postmortem you did not ask for. The most common buttercream failure — a curdled, separated mess that looks like scrambled eggs suspended in soup — has two root causes, and powdered sugar measurement is one of them. Buttercream is an emulsion: fat (butter) suspended in a sugar-water matrix. Powdered sugar, with its built-in cornstarch, is a moisture sponge. Dump in 50 grams of sifted powdered sugar when your recipe expects 60 grams of unsifted, and you have just added less starch than the butter can hold — sifted sugar is lighter, so a scoop holds fewer grams and sugar than the butter can hold. The emulsion breaks. If your buttercream splits, the first question is: did you weigh the sugar, or did you scoop it? If you scooped it from a bag that's been sitting in the pantry for three months — packed and settled — you may have added more sugar than you think. Weigh it. Every time. For buttercream, 60 grams per half cup is the safe baseline; adjust from there after you see the texture.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/2 cup of powdered sugar?

A1: Depends on sifting. Unsifted (straight from the bag) = 60g. Sifted first = about 50g. Most American recipes assume unsifted unless the word "sifted" appears before or after the measurement — and the word order matters. "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" means sift first, then measure (100g). "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted" means measure first, then sift (120g). When in doubt, weigh 60g for a half cup and sift after weighing — the sifting won't change the weight, only the volume.

Q2: Why does sifting change the weight so much?

A2: Powdered sugar particles are microscopic and pack like settled dust. Sifting forces air between particles, dropping the weight per cup from about 120g to about 100g. The cornstarch anti-caking agent (3-5% of the product) also forms small clumps over time that collapse when sifted. The resulting 20g-per-cup spread means a "cup of powdered sugar" is one of the least precise measurements in baking — which is ironic, because powdered sugar is used most often in precision applications like frosting and fondant.

Q3: Can I substitute granulated sugar for powdered sugar by weight?

A3: No — 60g of powdered sugar is not interchangeable with 60g of granulated, even though they weigh the same. Powdered sugar contains cornstarch (which thickens and stabilizes) and dissolves almost instantly because of its fine grind. Granulated sugar takes longer to dissolve and will leave a gritty texture in uncooked applications like frosting. That said, in a blender emergency: buzz 1 cup of granulated sugar with 1 teaspoon of cornstarch until it looks like snow. It works. I have done it. The frosting won't know.
