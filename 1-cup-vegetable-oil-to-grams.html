1 Cup Vegetable Oil to Grams Converter

Convert a cup of vegetable oil into grams — 218, and the reason that number is physics rather than convention.

Gram Equivalent:

218 grams (218 g)

*1 US cup vegetable oil ≈ 218g. Oil is less dense than water (237g/cup) — density ≈ 0.92 g/ml.

1 cup of vegetable oil in grams — the short answer

A cup of vegetable oil is 218 grams. Not 237, which is the number people tend to arrive at by assuming a cup of anything weighs a cup of water, and not 200, which is the granulated sugar figure and unrelated.

The 218 is not a convention anybody agreed on. It falls out of the volume of a US cup and the density of oil, and it is worth seeing the arithmetic once because it makes the number memorably solid.

Why It Is 218 and Not 237

A US customary cup is 236.6 millilitres. That is fixed by law and not in question.

What varies is what you put in it. Water has a density of almost exactly 1.00 grams per millilitre, so a cup of water weighs 236.6 grams — which is where the 237 comes from. Oil is lighter than water, which is the same fact that makes it float on top of a vinaigrette. Vegetable oil runs about 0.92 grams per millilitre.

So:

  • 236.6 ml × 0.92 g/ml = 217.7 g, which rounds to 218
  • 236.6 ml × 1.00 g/ml = 236.6 g, the water figure

The 8% gap between the two is the whole story. A recipe written in cups is a recipe written in volume, and converting it to grams requires knowing what the volume is filled with — not just how big it is.

Every Cooking Oil Weighs About the Same

One thing that makes oil unusually easy: the density barely moves between types. The variation across the oils a home baker is likely to use is around 1%, which is smaller than the precision of most kitchen scales.

  • Canola — about 0.915 g/ml, or 216 g per cup
  • Soybean and generic vegetable oil — about 0.92 g/ml, 218 g per cup
  • Corn oil — about 0.92 g/ml, 218 g per cup
  • Sunflower — about 0.918 g/ml, 217 g per cup
  • Olive oil — about 0.915 g/ml, 216 g per cup
  • Coconut oil, melted — about 0.92 g/ml, 218 g per cup

So the site's standard of 218 g per cup is good for all of them, and the differences are not worth acting on unless you are scaling a commercial batch. This is the opposite of the situation with flour or cocoa, where the technique of filling the cup produces a spread ten times wider than the difference between brands.

Is Oil Easier to Measure Than Flour?

Yes, and for a reason that has nothing to do with oil being a liquid. Oil is incompressible. You cannot pack more of it into a cup by pressing, because there is no air in it to squeeze out.

That means a cup of oil measured sloppily is still 218 g, where a cup of flour measured sloppily can be 120 g or 145 g. If you are working without a scale, oil is the ingredient you can be relaxed about, and flour is the one you cannot.

The Oil Left in the Cup

Oil has one measuring problem that flour does not: it sticks to the vessel. When you pour a cup of oil out of a measuring cup, a film remains on the walls and the base, and that film is not part of the recipe.

It is a small quantity. A one-cup measure has something like 175 square centimetres of internal surface, and a retained film of around 0.05 millimetres works out to roughly a gram of oil. In practice a well-scraped cup gives up all but a gram or two.

Where that becomes interesting is at small volumes. A gram lost from a one-cup pour is 0.5% of the fat. The same film left in a tablespoon measure is 1 g out of 13.6, which is 7%, and if you measure four separate tablespoons the losses compound. If a recipe is sensitive to its fat — a shortbread, a génoise, a small batch of mayonnaise — oil is worth pouring onto the scale rather than into a cup.

Swapping Oil for Butter by Weight

Oil and butter are not interchangeable one for one, because butter is not pure fat. Standard US butter is about 80% butterfat and roughly 16 to 18% water, with the remainder milk solids.

So the comparison is fat to fat, not gram to gram:

  • 1 cup butter — 227 g total, of which about 182 g is fat and about 40 g is water
  • 1 cup oil — 218 g total, all of it fat
  • Oil to replace 1 cup of butter — about 182 g, which is 3/4 cup plus 1 tablespoon
  • Butter to replace 1 cup of oil — about 272 g, or 1 1/4 cups

The last line is the one most often quoted, and now it is clear where it comes from: oil is concentrated fat, so you need more butter to deliver the same amount of it.

There is a second adjustment that people forget. Replacing butter with oil removes about 40 g of water from the recipe — three tablespoons of it. In a cake that water was doing real work, so a straight swap produces a slightly denser, drier crumb unless you add the liquid back in the form of milk or water.

Why Oil Cakes Do Not Cream

Butter and sugar creamed together trap air, and that air is part of what makes a butter cake rise. Oil cannot do this — it is a liquid, and whipping it with sugar produces a slick paste rather than a lightened foam.

So oil-based cakes depend entirely on chemical leavening and on steam. What they gain in return is moisture that lasts. Butter firms up again when it cools, which is why a butter cake can taste dry the next day, while oil stays liquid at room temperature and keeps the crumb soft for longer. It is also why oil cakes hold up better in the refrigerator, and why carrot cake and banana bread are traditionally oil-based.

Measuring 1 Cup of Oil Without a Scale

  • Liquid measuring cup — set it on the counter, pour to the line, and read at eye level. A dry measuring cup read from above will be short by a tablespoon.
  • By tablespoons — 16 level tablespoons, which is 218 g. Tedious but exact.
  • By the scale — 218 g, poured straight into the mixing bowl, which also removes the film problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of vegetable oil?

A1: 218 grams. A US cup holds 236.6 millilitres, and vegetable oil has a density of about 0.92 grams per millilitre, which gives 217.7 grams.

Q2: Why is a cup of oil only 218g when a cup of water is 237g?

A2: Because oil floats on water. Oil is less dense — 0.92 grams per millilitre against water's 1.00 — so the same volume weighs less. The cup is unchanged; the substance is lighter.

Q3: Do all cooking oils weigh the same per cup?

A3: Very nearly. Canola, corn, soybean, sunflower and olive oils all sit between 0.91 and 0.92 grams per millilitre, so a cup of any of them lands within a couple of grams of 218.
