How Many Grams in 1 Cup of Whole Wheat Flour?

About 120 grams spooned and leveled — the figure this converter uses. Whole wheat is measured the same way as any other flour: fluff it in the bag, spoon it into the cup without packing, then level the top with a straight edge.

You will also find 113 grams for that same cup, published by King Arthur Baking, and the difference is not a typo on either side. Whole wheat carries bran and germ, and those particles are coarse and irregular. They do not settle the way the fine, uniform particles of refined flour do, which means one mill's whole wheat can measure meaningfully heavier than another's. Both numbers are inside the normal range, and either will produce a loaf you can eat. Consistency matters more than the extra seven grams — pick a figure and stay with it so your results are repeatable.

Why Whole Wheat Reads Lighter Than Its Reputation

Whole wheat has a reputation for being heavy, and in a finished loaf that reputation is earned. In a measuring cup it is not. Coarse bran particles leave bigger gaps between themselves, so a cup of whole wheat ends up weighing roughly the same as a cup of all-purpose — 113 to 120 grams against 120 for the white flour.

The heaviness you feel in the dough comes from somewhere else entirely: water. Bran absorbs a startling amount of liquid, and it does so slowly, on its own schedule, well after the rest of the dough has come together.

The Weight Is the Easy Part — Whole Wheat Drinks More

This is where most bakers get caught. You weigh 120 grams of whole wheat, swap it straight into a recipe written for all-purpose, and the dough feels perfectly normal for ten minutes. Then it stiffens as the bran pulls water out of the mix. Reach for the jug now and you will add water the dough did not need, and you will have a slack loaf an hour later.

The fix is mechanical rather than clever:

  • Add 10–15% more liquid than the recipe calls for from the start.
  • Let the dough rest 15 minutes after mixing, so the bran has time to hydrate before you pass judgement on it.
  • Keep whole wheat to about 30–50% of the flour unless the recipe was written for whole wheat from the beginning. Past that, the bran starts cutting the gluten network faster than a home baker can compensate for.

Substituting by weight, rather than by the cup, is what makes this arithmetic clean. Say a recipe wants 2 cups of all-purpose — 240 grams on this site's numbers. Trading 30% of it for whole wheat means 72 g whole wheat plus 168 g all-purpose, no conversion gymnastics required, because the two flours weigh close enough per cup to be treated alike.

Storing Whole Wheat — It Goes Off Faster

White flour will sit in a cool cupboard for the better part of a year without complaint. Whole wheat will not. The germ carries oil, and oil turns rancid, so whole wheat is noticeably stale within a few months of opening the bag — you will smell it before you taste it.

If you bake through whole wheat slowly, keep it in the freezer. There is no need to thaw it before use; you can weigh it straight out of the cold, and the flour will come to room temperature as the dough proofs. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of whole wheat flour?

A1: About 120 grams using the spoon-and-level method, which is what this converter uses. King Arthur publishes 113 grams for the same cup, so anywhere in the 113–120 gram range is defensible.

Q2: Why do charts disagree about whole wheat flour?

A2: Because bran and germ are coarse and irregular, whole wheat settles differently from bag to bag and from mill to mill. Published figures run from about 113 to 120 grams per cup, and both ends are within the normal range.

Q3: Is whole wheat flour heavier than white flour?

A3: Not in the measuring cup. Whole wheat and all-purpose land within a few grams of each other at roughly 113–120 grams per cup. It behaves heavier in a dough because the bran absorbs far more water than refined flour does.

Q4: How much extra liquid does whole wheat flour need?

A4: Add 10–15% more liquid than the recipe calls for, then let the dough rest 15 minutes after mixing so the bran can hydrate before you judge it. Keep whole wheat to about 30–50% of the total flour unless the recipe was written for it.
