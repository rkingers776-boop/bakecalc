1 Teaspoon of Salt to Grams — Two Salts, Same Spoon, Completely Different Bakes

Table salt = 5.7g per teaspoon. Diamond Crystal kosher = 2.8g. Morton kosher = 5.0g. Swapping them blind is the fastest way to wreck a recipe.

Gram Equivalent:

5.7 grams (5.7 g)

*Table salt: 5.7g/tsp. But Diamond Crystal kosher is only 2.8g/tsp — check your salt type!

There Is No "Teaspoon of Salt." There Are Five Different Teaspoons of Salt.

This is the hill I will die on. A teaspoon is a measure of volume. Salt varies wildly in density depending on the shape and size of the crystals. A teaspoon of fine table salt contains more than twice as much actual salt as a teaspoon of Diamond Crystal kosher salt — 5.7 grams vs 2.8 grams. They're both "1 teaspoon of salt." They will produce completely different results in your dough.

Most American baking recipes written in the last 20 years (from professional sources like King Arthur, Serious Eats, Cook's Illustrated) assume Diamond Crystal kosher salt unless otherwise stated. But older recipes, most non-American recipes, and almost all packaged food recipes assume table salt. If you're working from a recipe that just says "1 tsp salt" and doesn't specify the type, and it's from a blog, a family cookbook, or a recipe card, assume table salt at 5.7g. If it's from a professional test kitchen, assume Diamond Crystal at 2.8g. The difference between getting this right and getting it wrong is often the difference between "this is delicious" and "I think I added too much salt."

The Practical Fix: Weigh Your Salt

Salt costs less than a penny per gram. A basic kitchen scale costs $10-20. If a recipe calls for 6 grams of salt (about 1 teaspoon of table salt) and you use a heaping teaspoon of Diamond Crystal (maybe 4 grams), your bread will be undersalted and taste flat — no amount of butter or jam will fix it. If you use a teaspoon of table salt (5.7g) in a recipe that was developed with Diamond Crystal (2.8g), your cookies will taste salty in a way that's subtle but wrong, like drinking water that's just slightly too mineral-heavy. You might not even identify it as "too much salt" — it'll just taste... off.

The safest move: look at the recipe writer's gram amount. If they provide one, use that and ignore the teaspoon. If they don't provide grams, assume 5.7g per teaspoon of unspecified salt (worst case: it's slightly under-salted, which is fixable). Better yet: find recipes that give salt in grams. The fact that any baking recipe in 2026 still says "1 tsp salt" without specifying the type is honestly a little embarrassing for our industry.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 teaspoon of table salt?

1 US teaspoon of fine table salt = 5.7 grams. Fine sea salt is the same — 5.7g per teaspoon. Both are fine-grain sodium chloride, and their density is nearly identical.

Q2: Why is Diamond Crystal kosher salt so much lighter than Morton kosher salt?

Different manufacturing processes. Diamond Crystal uses a patented evaporation process that creates hollow, pyramid-shaped flakes — like tiny origami structures that trap air. Morton uses a roller-compression method that makes flat, dense flakes. The Diamond Crystal process was actually invented to make salt that dissolves faster and sticks better to food; the low density is a side effect that professional chefs learned to love because it makes seasoning by hand more forgiving (a pinch is less salt, so you're less likely to overshoot).

Q3: Do I really need to weigh salt? It's such a small amount.

Salt is the ingredient where a small absolute error has the largest impact on flavor. 1 gram of salt too much or too little is immediately noticeable in a loaf of bread — that's less than ¼ teaspoon of table salt. And unlike sugar or butter, salt doesn't just affect flavor; it controls yeast activity, strengthens gluten, and extends shelf life. A bread with too little salt will rise too fast and taste bland. A bread with too much salt will rise slowly and taste, well, salty. Weigh it.
