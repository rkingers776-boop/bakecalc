mL to Grams — Water Is Easy. Everything Else Is Complicated.

1 mL of water = 1 gram. But 1 mL of honey = 1.42 grams. And 1 mL of oil = 0.92 grams. The conversion depends entirely on what you're measuring. Here's the full breakdown.

Weight in Grams:

100 g

Based on density of 1.00 g/mL at room temperature (~20°C / 68°F).

Density: The One Science Concept Every Cook Should Know

If you only remember one thing from this page: 1 mL = 1 g is true ONLY for water. For everything else, you need the ingredient's density. Density is just a fancy word for "how much a fixed volume of something weighs." It's why oil floats on water (oil is less dense) and why honey sinks to the bottom of tea (honey is more dense).

When you measure 100 mL of something on a scale expecting 100 g, the error depends entirely on what's in the measuring cup:

  • Water (density 1.00): 100 mL = 100 g. No error. This is the only liquid this works for.
  • Milk (density 1.03): 100 mL = 103 g. 3% error. Negligible in most recipes.
  • Vegetable oil (density 0.92): 100 mL = 92 g. 8% error. Starting to matter, especially in recipes with a lot of oil.
  • Honey (density 1.42): 100 mL = 142 g. 42% error. That's not a rounding error — that's a different recipe entirely.
  • Flour (density ~0.53): 100 mL = 53 g. 47% error. This is why you can't measure flour in a liquid measuring cup and call it a day.

The Oil Problem: Why Your Cake Is Greasy When You Measure by Volume

A common cake recipe calls for "1/2 cup vegetable oil" — that's 118 mL. If you weigh that expecting 118 g, you'll actually get about 109 g (118 × 0.92). The 9-gram difference won't ruin anything.

But here's where it gets tricky: if the recipe developer used the weight method (weighing 118 g of oil into the bowl) and you use the volume method (pouring oil to the 118 mL line), you're actually adding 128 g of oil — because 118 g ÷ 0.92 g/mL = 128 mL. You've added 9 extra grams of oil. That's 8% more fat than the recipe intended.

One 8% error won't ruin a cake. But compounding errors — 8% more oil, 15% more flour (from scooping instead of spooning), 5% more sugar (from a heavy-handed cup measure) — and suddenly your "tried and tested" recipe produces something the original baker wouldn't recognize. This is how five-star recipes get one-star reviews from people who swear they "followed the recipe exactly."

Flour: The Worst-Case Scenario for mL→g Conversion

Flour is the hardest ingredient to convert from volume to weight because its density changes dramatically based on how much it's compacted. Spooned flour: ~0.53 g/mL. Scooped flour: ~0.63 g/mL. Sifted flour: ~0.45 g/mL. The same 250 mL measuring cup can hold anywhere from 113 g to 158 g of flour — a 40% range.

For flour specifically, skip the mL-to-grams conversion entirely and use our cups-to-grams flour converter which uses the standardized spoon-and-level method (120 g per US cup, or 0.51 g/mL). Or better yet: use a recipe that lists flour in grams to begin with. Any recipe developer who cares about your results lists dry ingredients by weight.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 1 mL equal to 1 gram?

Only for pure water at 4°C. For room-temperature water, it's close enough (1.00 g/mL). For milk, oil, honey, and especially dry ingredients, the difference can be 5-50% — use this converter with the correct ingredient selected.

Why is honey heavier than water per mL?

Honey is about 82% sugar dissolved in about 17% water. The sugar molecules pack more mass into the same volume than water molecules alone — that's why honey's density is 1.42 g/mL. The same principle applies to maple syrup, molasses, and corn syrup: the more dissolved sugar, the denser the liquid.

How do I convert mL to grams for flour?

Flour density is highly variable (0.45-0.63 g/mL depending on how it's packed). For reliable results, use a scale and weigh flour in grams directly, or use our dedicated flour cups-to-grams converter which uses the standardized spoon-and-level method.
