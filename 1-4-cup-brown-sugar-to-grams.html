How Many Grams in 1/4 Cup of Brown Sugar?

Brown sugar is the only common baking ingredient where your measuring technique swings the weight by 20% or more. Spoon it in loosely and you get about 45 grams. Pack it firmly with the back of a spoon — the way most American recipes intend — and you get 55 grams per quarter cup. That 10-gram gap is not a rounding error; it is enough extra sugar to change the spread, chew, and color of a batch of chocolate chip cookies. Packed brown sugar weighs about 220 g per cup, or 55 g per quarter cup. The packing matters because brown sugar is coated in molasses, and that thin layer of syrup makes the crystals sticky — they cling together, and pressing them drives out air pockets that would otherwise take up volume. White sugar crystals are dry and slide past each other freely, which is why white sugar weighs the same scooped or spooned. Brown sugar fights you either way: measure it loose and you are probably under-sugaring your recipe; pack it half-heartedly and you are somewhere in the middle with no way to know where.

The Conversion: Two Numbers, One Ingredient

Brown sugar is the rare ingredient that needs two rows on the conversion chart because the measurement method changes the answer:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • Packed (Light or Dark): 0.25 × 220 g = 55 grams
  • Loose / Spooned: 0.25 × 180 g = 45 grams

What 10 Grams of Brown Sugar Actually Changes in Your Bake

Ten grams is roughly two teaspoons of brown sugar — it does not sound like much, but in a quarter-cup quantity it is an 18% swing, and brown sugar punches above its weight class in baking chemistry. Brown sugar is acidic (the molasses brings a pH around 5.5), so it reacts with baking soda to produce lift. More brown sugar = more acid = more CO2, up to a point. It is also more hygroscopic than white sugar because molasses is a humectant — it pulls and holds water from the air and from your dough. An extra 10 grams of brown sugar per quarter cup in a cookie recipe means a chewier, darker, more spread-out cookie that stays soft a day longer. Undershoot by 10 grams and you get a paler, cakier cookie that stales faster. For half-cup conversions the absolute error doubles to 20 grams, but the proportion stays the same. The quarter cup is the foundation — get it right and the scaling follows naturally. Our full-cup brown sugar guide goes deeper on how packing consistency affects large-batch baking.

Light vs. Dark Brown Sugar: Same Weight, Different Personality

Light brown sugar contains about 3.5% molasses by weight; dark brown sugar is around 6.5%. That extra 3% molasses changes the flavor noticeably — dark brown sugar tastes deeper, almost like toffee — but it does not change the density in any measurable way. Both pack to 220 g per cup, both settle to 55 g per quarter cup. The choice between them is a flavor decision, not a weight decision. Dark brown sugar also brings slightly more acid to the party, which means a little more lift from your baking soda, and slightly more moisture retention. If a recipe calls for "brown sugar" without specifying light or dark, it almost always means light — but substituting dark at the same weight will not hurt anything. You will just get a more pronounced molasses note, which is rarely a bad thing in a gingerbread or a barbecue rub.

The Hardened Brown Sugar Problem

Brown sugar turns into a rock when the molasses coating dries out and the crystals fuse together. When your brown sugar is hard as a hockey puck, you cannot pack it properly — the chunks resist compression and you end up with big air gaps in the measuring cup. The result is an under-packed measurement that could be as low as 40 g per quarter cup instead of the intended 55 g. Soften it first. The fastest method: put the hardened sugar in a microwave-safe bowl, drape a damp paper towel over the top, and microwave in 15-second bursts until it loosens up. Alternatively, seal the hardened sugar in a bag with a slice of apple or a piece of bread overnight — the moisture migrates into the sugar and softens it. Or, simplest of all, use a scale and weigh 55 g. The scale does not care how hard your brown sugar is. Tablespoon conversions for brown sugar get even trickier because packing at that small scale is wildly inconsistent — another reason a scale pays for itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is the weight difference between packed and spooned brown sugar?

A1: Packed brown sugar — pressed firmly into the cup until it holds its shape — weighs about 55 g per quarter cup. Loosely spooned brown sugar without any pressing weighs about 45 g. That is a 10-gram difference, or roughly 18%. Most American recipes assume the packed method. If the recipe says "1/4 cup brown sugar, packed," pack it. If it just says "1/4 cup brown sugar" without a qualifier, pack it anyway — that is the default convention in US recipe writing and has been for decades.

Q2: Does hardened brown sugar weigh more than soft brown sugar?

A2: No — a rock-hard lump of brown sugar and a bag of soft, fresh brown sugar both contain the same amount of sugar and molasses per gram. A packed cup of hardened brown sugar and a packed cup of soft brown sugar both weigh about 220 g. The problem with hardened sugar is not the weight, it is that you cannot pack it into a measuring cup properly — the hard chunks leave air gaps, so you end up under-measuring. Soften it first (microwave with a damp paper towel, or seal it overnight with an apple slice), or bypass the problem entirely by using a scale set to 55 g.

Q3: Do light and dark brown sugar weigh the same?

A3: Yes — the density difference is negligible. Light brown sugar (about 3.5% molasses) and dark brown sugar (about 6.5% molasses) both pack to 220 g per cup, or 55 g per quarter cup. The extra few percent of molasses in dark brown sugar changes the flavor, the acidity, and the moisture retention of your bake, but not the weight. Substitute them freely by weight with zero conversion math required.
