400 g Flour to Cups Converter

Convert 400 grams of flour to cups — three and a third, and the batch size where measurement error becomes tolerable.

Cup Equivalent:

Roughly 3 1/3 cups (3.33 cups)

*Calculated using standard US customary baking cups via spoon-and-level method.

400 g of flour in cups — the short answer

Four hundred grams of all-purpose flour is 3.33 cups — three cups plus a third of a cup, measured by the spoon-and-level standard. Unlike most flour weights, this one builds from whole measures without any spoons.

The reason is that 400 divided by 120 gives ten thirds, and a third of a cup is a measure that exists in every set. So the conversion is three scoops, not three scoops and a fiddle.

Why a Bigger Batch Tolerates a Bigger Mistake

The measuring error in a cup of flour does not depend on how much flour you are measuring. Spoon it and you get 120 g; scoop it and you get 145 to 150. That 25 to 30 g spread is a fixed property of the method.

What changes with batch size is how much that spread matters:

  • At 50 g of flour — a 25 g error is 50%. The recipe is effectively a different recipe.
  • At 150 g of flour — a 25 g error is 17%. Enough to notice in a lean dough.
  • At 400 g of flour — a 25 g error is 6%. Usually invisible, and in an enriched dough often undetectable.

This inverts the usual instinct. People assume that a big batch is riskier because more is at stake, and that a small one is safe because a mistake is small in absolute terms. The opposite is true: the absolute mistake stays the same while the thing it is measured against grows.

None of which is an argument for measuring carelessly. It is an argument for knowing where the risk is concentrated — and it sits in the small batches, the single muffins and the half recipes, not in the two-loaf bake.

Where 400 g of Flour Shows Up

  • Two-loaf bakes — two one-pound tins, or one large free-form loaf of around 900 g baked weight.
  • Layer cakes — two nine-inch rounds, enough for a filled cake.
  • Pizza nights — four to five medium bases.
  • Bread books — most home bread formulas are written for 400 to 500 g of flour, because that is the quantity that fits a domestic mixer.

The Water 400 g of Flour Takes

At 70% hydration, 400 g of flour takes 280 g of water. At 75%, it takes 300. Because water weighs one gram per millilitre, those figures convert to millilitres without any arithmetic, which is exactly why bread formulas are written in grams in the first place.

The other ingredient quantities follow the same logic. Salt at 2% is 8 g. Instant yeast at 2% is 8 g, or 4 g if you are fermenting slowly in the fridge. Once the flour weight is fixed, the entire formula is determined, and flour is the only ingredient in the bowl for which that is true.

Where the Neat Multiples Fall

Because a cup is 120 g, exact multiples of a cup land on multiples of 120: 120, 240, 360, 480, 600. Anything else produces a fraction. That is why the round flour weights in American recipes cluster at those numbers while metric recipes cluster at 250, 500 and 1000 — the two systems have different comfortable resting points, and 400 g is one that sits awkwardly between them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 400 g of flour?

A1: Three and a third cups — three cups plus one third cup measure. The exact figure is 3.33 cups, and unlike most flour weights it can be built from whole measures with no spoons involved.

Q2: What does 400 g of flour make?

A2: Two one-pound sandwich loaves, one large 900 g loaf, or two nine-inch cake layers. It is a two-loaf quantity, which is why it appears so often in bread books.

Q3: Does batch size affect how accurate I need to be?

A3: Yes. The measuring error from spooning versus scooping is about 25 grams whether you are measuring 50 grams or 400. Against 400 grams that is 6%; against 50 grams it is 50%. Larger batches dilute the same inaccuracy.
