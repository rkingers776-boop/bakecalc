How Many Grams in 1 Cup of Sugar?

Ask ten bakers what a cup of sugar weighs and you'll get one confident answer: 200 grams. That number is stable in a way flour's is not, because sugar doesn't compact the way flour does — the crystals are hard, uniform, and don't deform when you press on them. Scoop granulated sugar straight from the bag or spoon it in gently and you'll land within a few grams of 200 either way. That reliability is why sugar is one of the few ingredients American recipes can list by volume without much real risk.

The 200-gram figure only covers granulated, though. Change the sugar and the weight changes with it, sometimes dramatically — 1 cup of granulated white sugar is 200 grams, firmly packed brown sugar is 220, and unsifted powdered sugar is only 120. That last gap is the one that ruins frostings: swapping powdered and granulated sugar by volume puts you 80 grams off in a single cup, which is 40% of the sugar the recipe called for.

The Conversion Formula (Cups → Grams)

Same one-line formula as any dry ingredient — cups multiplied by grams per cup:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • Granulated White Sugar: 1 × 200 g = 200 grams
  • Brown Sugar, packed: 1 × 220 g = 220 grams
  • Powdered Sugar, unsifted: 1 × 120 g = 120 grams
  • Caster / Superfine Sugar: 1 × 200 g = 200 grams
  • Turbinado / Raw Sugar: 1 × 180 g = 180 grams

Granulated and caster sugar weigh the same even though their crystals are different sizes, because both pack into the cup with about the same efficiency. Coarse sugars break the pattern: turbinado's larger, irregular crystals leave more air between them, so a cup comes in roughly 20 grams lighter. Powdered sugar, ground to a dust and cut with cornstarch, is lighter still.

Packed, Poured, or Sifted: Why Sugar Types Don't Weigh Alike

Sugar behaves very differently from flour in the measuring cup, and crystal structure explains why. Granulated sugar's crystals are hard and incompressible, so whether you scoop or spoon, the cup fills with the same efficiency. You genuinely cannot pack a cup of granulated sugar the way you can pack a cup of flour — there is no give in it.

Two sugars break that rule, in opposite directions. Brown sugar is coated in molasses, which makes the particles sticky and the mass full of air pockets. Press it with the back of a spoon and it compresses like damp sand, gaining 60 to 70 grams per cup over the loose measure. That is why brown sugar recipes say "firmly packed" and why this converter defaults to the packed figure. Powdered sugar goes the other way: sifting it first fluffs it up and drops the cup weight from about 120 grams to roughly 100. When a recipe says "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted," it means sift first and then measure — not measure first and sift after, which leaves you with noticeably less sugar in the bowl.

A Cup of Every Sugar, Weighed

These are the numbers to reach for before you convert. Most are stable across brands, but the coarse and specialty sugars drift by 10 to 20 grams depending on the mill:

  • Granulated white — 200 g
  • Caster / superfine — 200 g
  • Brown, firmly packed — 220 g
  • Brown, loose (not packed) — 150 g
  • Dark brown, packed — 230 g
  • Powdered, unsifted — 120 g
  • Powdered, sifted — 100 g
  • Turbinado / raw — 180 g
  • Demerara — 220 g
  • Muscovado — 240 g

Sugar Does More Than Sweeten

This is the part that catches anyone who decides to "just cut the sugar" in a recipe. In a batter, sugar is a structural ingredient doing at least five jobs at once:

  • Tenderizing — sugar competes with the flour proteins for water, so the more sugar a batter carries, the less gluten develops and the softer the finished crumb.
  • Holding moisture — sugar is hygroscopic, pulling water from the air and holding onto it. This is why a high-sugar cake stays soft for days while lean bread goes stale overnight.
  • Browning — both caramelization and the Maillard reaction need sugar. Reduce it and the bake stays pale and tastes flat.
  • Aeration during creaming — beating butter and sugar together, the crystals carve tiny air pockets into the fat, and those pockets are what leaven the cake. Coarser sugar means bigger pockets and a coarser crumb.
  • Stabilizing egg foams — sugar dissolved into egg whites raises their viscosity, so the foam holds its structure long enough to be folded into a batter without collapsing.

Cut the sugar by a quarter in a butter cake and you don't simply lose sweetness. You get a drier, paler, denser cake that stales sooner. Reducing sweetness properly means rebalancing fat and liquid at the same time, which is why a tested recipe is worth following before you improvise.

How Much Sugar Error Can a Recipe Absorb?

For granulated sugar, quite a lot — you can't accidentally pack an extra quarter into the cup, so the measurement is naturally forgiving. The trouble starts when you switch sugar types by volume:

  • Powdered where granulated was meant — the classic error. A cup of powdered sugar is 120 g against granulated's 200 g, an 80-gram shortfall per cup. In a cake calling for 400 g of sugar you would be 160 g light: stiff batter, dense crumb, no browning on top.
  • Granulated where powdered was meant — the reverse. Buttercream made with 200 g of granulated in place of 120 g of powdered will be gritty and slack at the same time.
  • Loose brown in place of packed — 150 g instead of 220 g. Cookies lose their chew, spread less in the oven, and taste noticeably less caramel-rich.

In each of these cases the volume measurement reads identically on paper while the weight is off by 30 to 60 percent. A scale settles the question in one step. For the same effect on the other side of the bowl, see 1 cup of flour in grams and the full cups-to-grams guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of granulated sugar?

A1: 200 grams. Granulated sugar is one of the most predictable ingredients in the pantry — the crystals are hard and uniform, so spooning it in and scooping it straight from the bag land within a few grams of each other. Unlike flour, you cannot accidentally pack an extra quarter into the cup.

Q2: Is brown sugar heavier than white sugar per cup?

A2: Yes, once it is packed the way recipes intend. A firmly packed cup of brown sugar weighs about 220 grams against granulated sugar's 200. The extra weight comes from molasses coating the crystals and from the packing itself: leave the same brown sugar loose and the cup comes in closer to 150 grams.

Q3: How does powdered sugar compare to granulated sugar by weight?

A3: A cup of unsifted powdered sugar weighs about 120 grams — 40% lighter than granulated. Sift it before measuring and it drops to roughly 100 grams. This is why the two cannot be swapped by volume: equal cups hold very unequal amounts of sugar.
