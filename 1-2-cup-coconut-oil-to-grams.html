The Ingredient Where State Does Not Matter

1/2 cup of coconut oil weighs 109 grams — solid or melted, it is the same number. This is genuinely unusual in the baking world. Butter changes density when it melts (solid butter has air pockets from the churning process; melted butter doesn't). Flour compresses when scooped vs spooned. Brown sugar packs. Coconut oil, though — coconut oil is 90% saturated fat. Saturated fats have almost no volume change between solid and liquid states. They are molecularly boring, and boring is exactly what you want in a measurement. Whether you scoop it solid from the jar or pour it warm from a measuring cup, you are getting 109 grams per half cup. The precision is built into the ingredient itself.

This property has a practical consequence: coconut oil is arguably the most forgiving fat to measure by cup. Butter? Pack it wrong and you are off by 10%. Flour? Don't even get me started. Coconut oil? Scoop it, melt it, freeze it, whatever — 109g per half cup every single time. The only asterisk is fractionated coconut oil (the kind that stays liquid even in the fridge), which is about 210g per cup, or 105g per half cup. It has had the long-chain fatty acids removed, which changes the density slightly. But most home bakers are using standard refined or virgin coconut oil from a jar, and for that, 109g is your number.

The Conversion Formula

  • Grams = Cups × 218 (standard coconut oil, refined or virgin)
  • 1/2 cup coconut oil = 109 grams (solid or melted)
  • 1/2 cup fractionated/MCT = 105 grams
  • 1 Tbsp coconut oil = 13.6 grams (218 ÷ 16)

The Butter Swap: 109 vs 113 — Close Enough?

For dairy-free baking, coconut oil is the workhorse butter substitute. The numbers line up surprisingly well: 1/2 cup of butter weighs 113 grams. 1/2 cup of coconut oil weighs 109 grams. That is a 4-gram difference — less than a teaspoon of water. For a batch of 24 cookies, 4 grams of fat spread across 24 portions is 0.17 grams per cookie. Your taste buds cannot detect 0.17 grams. So yes, for cookies, muffins, quick breads, and most cakes, a 1:1 cup-for-cup swap works.

But there is a behavioral difference that the weights do not capture. Butter is about 80% fat, 15-17% water, and the rest is milk solids. Coconut oil is 100% fat. When butter bakes, that water turns to steam and creates small pockets of lift — contributing to rise and a slightly lighter crumb. Coconut oil, being pure fat, produces no steam. This means your coconut-oil version will spread a tiny bit more in cookies (no steam lift) and be slightly denser in cakes. The fix is not more coconut oil — it is adding back the missing water. Add about 1 teaspoon of water or non-dairy milk per 1/2 cup of coconut oil when you are swapping for butter in a cake or muffin recipe. It compensates for the missing steam and gets you closer to the original texture. Not perfect — butter is irreplaceable in laminated doughs and anything that depends on water content for flakiness (pie crust, puff pastry) — but for everyday dairy-free baking, this trick works.

Virgin vs Refined: The Flavor Decision at 1/2 Cup Scale

At the half-cup level, the refined-vs-virgin choice becomes noticeable. Half a cup is enough coconut oil that the flavor of virgin — distinctly tropical, unmistakably coconut — will come through in anything you bake. In a vanilla cupcake, it is distracting. In a chocolate brownie, it is confusing. In a coconut cake or a batch of coconut macaroons, it is exactly what you want. The rule: if coconut is in the name of the recipe, use virgin. If it is not, use refined. Refined coconut oil is flavorless, odorless, and melts into the background like any neutral fat. I have seen too many people spend money on expensive virgin coconut oil for a generic muffin recipe, only to end up with muffins that taste vaguely of suntan lotion. Save the good stuff for recipes that celebrate it. For everything else, the $6 jar of refined from the grocery store is the better choice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/2 cup of coconut oil?

A1: 109 grams — solid or melted, the weight is the same. This is unique among baking fats and is due to coconut oil's unusually high saturated fat content (about 90%), which has almost no volume change between solid and liquid states. Fractionated/MCT coconut oil is slightly lighter at 105g per half cup.

Q2: Can I substitute 1/2 cup of coconut oil for 1/2 cup of butter?

A2: Yes — the weights are 109g vs 113g, a trivial 4-gram gap. Coconut oil is 100% fat while butter is ~80% fat with water, so expect slightly more spread in cookies and a slightly denser crumb in cakes. Add 1 teaspoon of water or non-dairy milk per 1/2 cup of coconut oil to compensate for butter's missing water content. And add a pinch of salt — coconut oil has none, and even unsalted butter contains some.

Q3: My kitchen is cold and my coconut oil is rock solid. Does that change the 1/2 cup measurement?

A3: No — 1/2 cup of solid coconut oil still weighs 109g. Coconut oil's melting point is 76°F (24°C), so in cooler kitchens or in winter, the jar will be solid. That is perfectly fine for baking — in fact, solid coconut oil is ideal for pie crusts and biscuits where you want solid fat for flaky layers. Pack it firmly into a dry measuring cup and level the top. Or, skip the cup: scoop chunks onto a scale until you hit 109g.
