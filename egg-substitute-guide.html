🥚 Egg Substitute Guide — 10 Replacements, Honestly Tested

What to use when you're out of eggs (or don't eat them). Exact ratios for every substitute, what each one works for, and where each one fails.

Quick Multiplier: How many eggs does your recipe need?

You Need:

1/2 cup applesauce

Best for: cakes, muffins, quick breads. Your bake will be slightly denser and moister.

The Substitutes, In Detail

Each tested at least once in a real recipe. Where I've noted a failure, I mean it — that one went in the trash.

Applesauce (Unsweetened)

1 egg = 1/4 cup applesauce

The workhorse egg substitute. Works in: cakes, muffins, quick breads, brownies, some cookies. Does not work in: anything that needs to rise dramatically (angel food cake), anything that depends on egg for structure (meringue, macarons), custards.

Applesauce replaces the moisture and some of the binding that eggs provide. It does NOT provide leavening or structure — your bake will be denser and slightly chewier. In muffins and quick breads, this is often an improvement (moister crumb). In layer cakes, the density is noticeable but not unpleasant if you're expecting it.

Pro tip: reduce the other liquid in the recipe by 2 tablespoons per 1/4 cup of applesauce used, or your batter will be too wet. The applesauce itself is mostly water.

★ Best for muffins & quick breads

Flax Egg (Ground Flaxseed + Water)

1 egg = 1 tbsp ground flaxseed + 3 tbsp water, rest 5 minutes

The most versatile egg substitute. Works in: cookies, brownies, pancakes, waffles, muffins, some cakes. Does not work in: light/delicate cakes, meringues, custards, anything where egg is the primary structure.

Ground flaxseed contains soluble fiber that forms a gel when mixed with water — it's remarkably similar to egg white in texture and binding ability. The flavor is nutty and slightly earthy, which complements chocolate, whole grains, and spices but clashes with vanilla and citrus. Use golden flaxseed for lighter-colored bakes; brown flaxseed adds visible dark flecks and a stronger flavor.

Pro tip: grind whole flaxseeds in a coffee grinder right before using. Pre-ground flax meal goes rancid within weeks (those omega-3s oxidize fast). Store flax meal in the freezer.

★ Best all-purpose substitute

Mashed Banana

1 egg = 1/4 cup mashed ripe banana (about 1/2 medium banana)

Works in: pancakes, waffles, banana bread (obviously), pumpkin bread, spice cakes, dense brownies. Does not work in: anything where you don't want banana flavor — which is most things. The banana flavor is NOT subtle, even in small amounts.

Banana adds moisture, binding, and natural sweetness. Bakes made with banana brown faster (more sugar from the fruit) — lower your oven temperature by 25°F and check for doneness 5 minutes early. Texture is denser and gummier than egg-based versions.

Good for pancakes, spice cakes & banana bread

Plain Yogurt (or Greek Yogurt)

1 egg = 1/4 cup plain yogurt (whole milk preferred)

Works in: cakes, muffins, quick breads. Does not work in: cookies (makes them cakey, which you may or may not want), meringues, custards. Greek yogurt is thicker than regular — thin with 1 tbsp milk per 1/4 cup if your batter seems too stiff.

Yogurt adds moisture, tenderness (the acid tenderizes gluten), and a subtle tang. Bakes made with yogurt have a finer, softer crumb — similar to buttermilk-based recipes. This is actually an improvement in many cakes.

Good for cakes & tender crumb

Silken Tofu (Blended)

1 egg = 1/4 cup silken tofu, blended until completely smooth

Works in: quiches, custard-based pies, dense cakes, brownies, cheesecakes. Does not work in: light cakes, cookies, anything that needs to rise. The tofu adds density — it's heavy, and your bake will be too.

This is the best substitute for recipes where egg provides the main structure (quiche, custard). The flavor is neutral — no one will know it's tofu unless you tell them. MUST be blended completely smooth; any lumps become rubbery bits in the final product. A stick blender in a tall cup is the easiest way.

Good for quiches, custards & cheesecakes

Aquafaba (Chickpea Can Liquid)

1 egg = 3 tbsp aquafaba · 1 egg white = 2 tbsp aquafaba + 1/8 tsp cream of tartar

The ONLY substitute that whips like egg whites. Works in: meringues, macarons, mousses, angel food cake (with caveats), cocktails (whiskey sours!). Does not work in: most standard baking — aquafaba is expensive in time and technique for something applesauce can do.

Use the liquid from a can of unsalted chickpeas. Reduce it by simmering until it reaches the consistency of egg whites (unreduced aquafaba is too thin and won't whip properly). Whip with cream of tartar for stability. The meringue will be slightly more fragile than egg-based — bake at a slightly lower temperature and don't open the oven door.

Pro tip: aquafaba from home-cooked chickpeas is inconsistent. Canned is more reliable. Goya and Bush's both work well. Low-sodium cans have thicker liquid (less water added).

★ Only sub for meringues & macarons

Vinegar + Baking Soda

1 egg = 1 tbsp white vinegar (or apple cider vinegar) + 1 tsp baking soda

Works in: cakes that need rise, particularly light/sponge cakes. Does not work in: anything where eggs provide binding or richness (cookies, brownies, custards). This substitute ONLY provides leavening — it replaces the air and lift from eggs, not the binding or richness.

The acid-base reaction produces carbon dioxide bubbles immediately — mix and get it in the oven fast. This is the go-to for vegan vanilla cakes that need to be light and fluffy. It pairs well with yogurt or applesauce (which provide moisture and binding while the vinegar+soda provides the lift).

Good for light cakes & sponge — but only replaces leavening

Buttermilk

1 egg = 1/4 cup buttermilk. Reduce other liquid in recipe by 1/4 cup.

Works in: cakes, pancakes, biscuits — anything where a tender, tangy crumb is welcome. Does not work in: cookies (cakey texture, spreads too much), meringues. Similar to yogurt but thinner — don't reduce other liquid as aggressively.

Buttermilk adds tenderness through acid and moisture. It doesn't bind well on its own — in recipes with 2+ eggs, combine buttermilk with another substitute (flax egg or applesauce) for the eggs beyond the first.

OK for cakes — combine with another sub for multi-egg recipes

Chia Egg (Chia Seeds + Water)

1 egg = 1 tbsp chia seeds + 3 tbsp water, rest 10 minutes

Works in: dense baked goods — muffins, quick breads, veggie burgers. Similar to flax egg but forms a stronger, thicker gel. The gel is almost slimy in texture when raw (this is normal — it's the soluble fiber hydrating). Bakes into a firmer, chewier texture than flax.

Chia seeds stay visible as small dark specks in light-colored bakes. White chia seeds exist but are harder to find. The flavor is milder than flax — more neutral, less nutty. Rest time is longer (10 minutes minimum vs 5 for flax) because chia seeds absorb water more slowly.

OK for dense bakes — stronger gel, visible specks

Oil + Baking Powder (Emergency-Only)

1 egg = use ONLY when replacing 2+ eggs: 2 tbsp oil + 1 tsp baking powder + 1 tbsp water per 2 eggs

Works in: emergency situations where you need something resembling a cake and have no other substitutes. Does not work in: anything you care about the outcome of. This is the "grocery store is closed and I already told people I'm bringing dessert" option.

The oil adds fat, the baking powder adds lift. There's no protein for structure and no emulsifier to hold it together. The result is crumbly, greasy, and structurally dubious. It's better than nothing but worse than literally every other substitute on this page. I include it only for completeness — and because I've been in that situation at 11 PM with no eggs, no flax, no applesauce, and a potluck the next day. The cupcakes held together. Barely. No one complimented them.

✗ Last resort — use any other sub on this page first

The Three Jobs of an Egg (And Why You Need Different Substitutes for Each)

An egg isn't one ingredient — it's three ingredients that happen to come in the same shell. Understanding this is the key to successful substitution:

1. The white provides structure. Egg white is ~90% water and ~10% protein. When heated, the proteins unfold and link together (coagulate), creating a solid network that holds the bake together. This is why cakes without eggs collapse and cookies without eggs crumble. Substitutes that replace structure: flax egg, chia egg, silken tofu, aquafaba (whipped).

2. The yolk provides richness. Egg yolk is ~50% water, ~30% fat, and ~15% protein. The fat tenderizes the crumb, adds flavor, and extends shelf life. The lecithin in yolk is a powerful emulsifier — it helps oil and water mix smoothly, which is why egg-based cake batters are velvety while eggless batters can look curdled. Substitutes that replace richness: oil, yogurt, banana, applesauce (partially).

3. The water in both white and yolk provides steam. About 75% of an egg's weight is water. When heated, this water turns to steam, creating air pockets that lift the batter. This is a secondary leavening mechanism (baking powder/soda is primary). Substitutes that replace steam/leavening: vinegar + baking soda, yogurt + baking soda, any water-containing substitute.

The framework: look at your recipe and figure out what the eggs are primarily doing. Cookies? Binding (use flax). Cake? Leavening + structure (use vinegar + soda + applesauce). Brownies? Richness + binding (use yogurt + flax). Custard? Structure (use silken tofu). Meringue? The white's foaming ability (use aquafaba — it's the only option).

When NOT to Substitute Eggs

Some recipes genuinely need real eggs. Here's when to accept that and make something else:

  • Angel food cake, genoise, chiffon cake: these depend entirely on whipped egg whites for their structure. Aquafaba can work but the result is more fragile and less tall. If you need a guaranteed result, use eggs or pick a different cake.
  • Lemon curd, crème brûlée, flan: eggs are the entire point. Silken tofu custard exists but it's a different dessert. Accept this and move on.
  • Fresh pasta: egg pasta and water pasta are different foods. Both good, but not interchangeable in a recipe that assumes egg dough's richness and color.
  • Recipes with 4+ eggs as the main ingredient: a frittata without eggs isn't a frittata. A quiche without eggs is a vegetable tart — which is fine, but call it that and approach it differently.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best egg substitute for baking?

For most baking: flax egg (cookies, brownies, pancakes) or applesauce (cakes, muffins, quick breads). 1 tbsp ground flax + 3 tbsp water per egg, or 1/4 cup applesauce per egg. See the full guide above for which substitute to use in your specific recipe.

Can I use the same substitute for every type of baking?

No. Eggs serve different functions in different recipes — binding in cookies, leavening in cakes, structure in custards. Match the substitute to the function (see "The Three Jobs of an Egg" section above). Using flax egg for a meringue will not work; using aquafaba for cookies is unnecessarily complicated.

How do commercial egg replacers (Ener-G, Bob's Red Mill) compare?

Commercial egg replacers are mostly potato starch + tapioca starch + leavening agents. They work reasonably well in cakes and muffins but add no richness, flavor, or binding — they're pure leavening. Flax egg and applesauce outperform them in most applications, and they cost 10× more per use. I keep a box of Ener-G for emergencies but almost never reach for it.
