120 g of flour in cups — the short answer

One hundred and twenty grams of all-purpose flour is exactly one US cup. This is the only point in the flour conversion table where the gram figure and the cup figure agree without a fraction appearing on either side, and that is why 120 is the number worth remembering above all the others.

The agreement is not a law of nature. It is what you get when you spoon flour lightly into a cup and level it off, then weigh the result — repeatedly, with real flour, in a real kitchen. Do it the other way, scooping the cup into the bag, and the same cup holds 145 g or more.

Why 120 Is the Pivot of the Whole Table

Every other flour weight on this site is derived from this one figure. Bread flour runs 130 g per cup, cake flour 110, rye 106, almond 96. Those numbers only mean anything relative to a baseline, and 120 is the baseline.

The practical consequence is that once you know 120 g equals a cup, everything else is division:

  • Half a cup — 60 g
  • A third of a cup — 40 g
  • A quarter cup — 30 g
  • A tablespoon — 7.5 g
  • A teaspoon — 2.5 g

That division is exact because 120 happens to be divisible by 2, 3, 4, 8, 16 and 48. Had the measured value come out at 125 — which some charts use — every one of those fractions would produce a decimal, and nobody would remember any of them.

The Equivalence Holds Only for One Measuring Method

120 g equals one cup, provided the cup was filled the way the figure was established: spooned in, then levelled. The same cup filled by dip-and-sweep weighs 145 to 150 g, and a cup of sifted flour weighs less than 120.

So the conversion is not a property of flour. It is a property of flour plus a technique, and dropping the technique drops the accuracy with it. This is the single most important thing to understand about any grams-to-cups conversion for flour, and it is why the same recipe can work for one baker and fail for another using the identical printed quantities.

Where 120 g of Flour Shows Up

  • American recipes written in grams — a recipe that already uses grams but expresses the flour as 120 will be worth following exactly.
  • Small sandwich loaves — a one-pound loaf sits near 300 to 350 g of flour, so 120 g is a third of a loaf.
  • Single cake layers — one six-inch layer is often 100 to 120 g of flour.
  • Conversion checkpoints — when a recipe gives cups and you want grams, 120 per cup is the multiplier.

What One Cup Weighs in Other Flours

  • All-purpose flour — 120 g
  • Whole wheat flour — 120 g
  • Bread flour — 130 g
  • Self-rising flour — 113 g
  • Cake flour — 110 g
  • Rye flour, medium — 106 g

The spread from 96 to 130 is more than a third. Substituting one flour for another cup for cup is therefore never a neutral swap — you are changing the amount of flour in the recipe by up to 35% while believing you kept it the same.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Is 120 g of flour one cup?

A1: Yes, exactly one US cup of all-purpose flour, measured by spooning the flour into the cup and levelling it. It is the one figure in the flour table that converts without rounding — every other weight lands on an awkward fraction.

Q2: Why is a cup of flour 120 grams and not something round?

A2: Because it is a measurement, not a definition. The cup is a fixed volume and flour is a compressible solid, so its weight per cup depends on how it was filled. Testing spooned-and-levelled all-purpose flour repeatedly gives about 120 grams, and that is where the figure comes from.

Q3: Does 120 g of bread flour also equal one cup?

A3: No. Bread flour is denser at about 130 grams per cup, so 120 grams of it is roughly 0.92 cups. The 120 gram figure applies to all-purpose flour and to whole wheat, which happens to weigh the same per cup.
