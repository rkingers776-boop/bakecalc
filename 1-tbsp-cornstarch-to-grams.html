1 tablespoon of cornstarch in grams — the short answer

One tablespoon of cornstarch is 8 grams. A cup of cornstarch weighs about 128 g and holds 16 tablespoons, so the division is exact rather than approximate.

There is one number to take away from this page and it is not the 8. It is the ratio.

The Only Ratio You Need

One tablespoon of cornstarch thickens one cup of liquid into a medium sauce. That is the whole of it, and it scales in both directions:

  • 1 tsp per cup — 2.7 g, a thin sauce or a light glaze
  • 1 tbsp per cup — 8 g, a medium sauce, a gravy, a fruit pie filling
  • 2 tbsp per cup — 16 g, a thick filling or a set custard
  • 4 tbsp per quart — 32 g, the catering version of the same ratio

Because the ratio is volumetric, working in grams requires nothing more than remembering that a cup of liquid is 236 g of water and the starch is 8 g of it. The proportion is tiny in weight terms — under 3.5% — which is part of why cornstarch thickening is so easy to overdo.

Why It Has to Be Mixed Cold

Cornstarch does not dissolve. It suspends. The granules are insoluble in cold water, sitting in it as a milky slurry, and that is exactly what you want.

Heat is the trigger. Around 95 °C the starch granules absorb water, swell enormously, and burst, releasing amylose that tangles into a gel. That process is called gelatinisation, and it is irreversible — which is the source of the classic failure.

Drop dry cornstarch into a hot liquid and the outside of the clump gelatinises on contact, sealing a dry core inside a smooth jacket. No amount of whisking opens it again, and the sauce carries lumps all the way to the table. Mix the same tablespoon into two tablespoons of cold water first, and every particle is already separated before the heat arrives, so the whole slurry sets at once.

So the routine is: 1 tbsp cornstarch, 2 tbsp cold water, whisk to a smooth slurry, then add to the liquid off the heat and bring it up gently.

Gelatinisation, and the Point Where It Stops Working

The gel needs a full boil to develop its maximum thickening power. Bring a cornstarch-thickened sauce to a simmer and it will stay thin and cloudy; take it to a proper boil for a minute and it turns glossy and thick.

But the gel is also fragile. Prolonged boiling shears the amylose chains apart, and the sauce thins again — sometimes dramatically, after five or ten minutes at a hard boil. This is the second classic failure, and it usually appears in a stew that simmered too long.

The practical rule is: boil to activate, then stop. Get it to a boil, hold it for sixty seconds, and drop it to a bare simmer or take it off the heat entirely.

Cornstarch Against Flour

Both thicken, and they are not interchangeable by weight or by volume.

  • 1 tbsp cornstarch — 8 g, roughly the thickening power of 2 tbsp of flour
  • 1 tbsp flour — 8 g, but only about three quarters of it is starch, and its protein and fat dilute the effect further
  • The colour — cornstarch sets to a clear, glossy gel; flour stays opaque and slightly cloudy
  • The flavour — cornstarch is tasteless; flour needs cooking out or it tastes raw
  • The gluten — cornstarch contributes none, which is why it is the thickener of choice for gluten-free sauces

Where flour wins is in anything that cooks for a long time. A roux built from flour holds up to an hour of gentle simmering; a cornstarch gel will not.

Where Cornstarch Thickening Fails

  • High acid plus long cooking — acid hydrolyses the starch chains, so a lemon-heavy sauce thins as it stands. Add the acid at the end instead.
  • Freezing — the gel breaks down when frozen and weeps on thawing. A cornstarch-thickened pie filling comes out spongy unless it is very high in sugar.
  • Over-stirring after setting — mechanical shear breaks the gel the same way boiling does.
  • Enzymes in raw fruit — fresh pineapple, papaya, kiwi and fig carry proteases and enzymes that attack starch. Cook those fruits before thickening.

For a filling that will be frozen or held a long time, tapioca starch or a flour-based roux is the more reliable choice, and arrowroot is the usual substitute where a clear, acid-tolerant gel is wanted.

Measuring 8 g Without a Scale

Cornstarch is a fine, light powder and it compresses, so the tablespoon is not as reliable as the arithmetic suggests. A loosely filled spoon is about 7 g; a packed one can reach 11 g.

  • Dip and level — dip the spoon into the container, sweep the top flat with a knife. This is the closest to 8 g.
  • Fluff first — cornstarch in a settled bag is compacted. Stirring it before measuring makes a measurable difference.
  • Weigh it — 8 g, and since the ratio matters more than the exact gram, a scale makes the whole process repeatable.

One naming note: in the United Kingdom, Ireland and Australia, this product is called cornflour, not cornstarch. In the United States, "corn flour" may mean a finely ground whole cornmeal, which is a completely different ingredient and will not thicken a sauce the same way.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 tablespoon of cornstarch?

A1: 8 grams. A cup of cornstarch is taken as 128 grams and holds 16 tablespoons, so each one is 8 grams exactly.

Q2: How much cornstarch thickens a cup of liquid?

A2: One tablespoon, or 8 grams, gives a medium sauce. Use half that for a thin sauce and up to double for a thick pie filling.

Q3: Why does cornstarch go lumpy in hot liquid?

A3: Because starch gelatinises on contact with heat. Dropped into hot liquid, the outside of each clump sets before the inside has been wetted, sealing it. Mixing it into cold water first coats every particle so the whole slurry thickens at the same time.
