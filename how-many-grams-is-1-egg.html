How Many Grams Is 1 Egg? 🥚

The complete weight guide for bakers — every egg size, every part, and why "1 egg" is a terrible recipe instruction.

The Short Answer

1 large egg (out of shell) = 50 grams. That's the number every American recipe developer assumes when they write "1 egg." The white weighs about 30 grams, the yolk about 18 grams, and the shell accounts for roughly 2 grams you'll never see on your scale. If you bake from British or European recipes, note that UK "large" eggs are slightly bigger (~53–58g out of shell) and Australian "large" eggs are closer to 52g. But for US recipes, the 50-gram baseline is your anchor.

Why "1 Egg" Is the Worst Instruction in Baking

I don't say this lightly. "1 egg" as a unit of measurement is worse than "1 cup of flour" — and that's saying something. Here's why: open any carton of "large" eggs and weigh them individually. I've done this. In a standard dozen labeled "US Large," I've found eggs ranging from 46 grams to 56 grams — out of shell. That's a 10-gram spread within the same carton of the same grade. Now imagine a recipe that calls for 4 eggs. If you get four light ones (46g each), you've added 184 grams of egg. Four heavy ones (56g each) = 224 grams. That's a 40-gram difference — the equivalent of accidentally adding or omitting nearly an entire extra egg. Your cake doesn't know what "large" means. It only knows how much liquid, protein, and fat landed in the bowl. This is why European baking recipes list eggs in grams — and why you should too. Crack your eggs into a bowl on your scale, not directly into the batter.

Egg White vs Egg Yolk: When the Split Matters

Some recipes want just whites (meringues, angel food cake, macarons), others just yolks (custards, ice cream, enriched doughs). Understanding the split helps you scale:

  • 1 large egg white ≈ 30g — about 60% of the total weight. Whites are mostly water (90%) with some protein. They provide structure and leavening but almost no fat.
  • 1 large egg yolk ≈ 18g — about 36% of the total. Yolks are the fat and emulsifier powerhouse. They're roughly 50% water, 30% fat, 17% protein. They tenderize, enrich, and add color.
  • The remaining ~2g is the shell, which doesn't go in your batter.

If a recipe calls for "2 large egg whites" and you're cracking from jumbo eggs, you'll get about 76g of white instead of the expected 60g — a 27% over-measure that can make your macarons hollow. Weigh the whites.

Substituting Egg Sizes: The Actual Math

You have medium eggs but the recipe was written for large. How many do you use? Here's the formula, no guesswork:

  • Target weight = Number of eggs in recipe × weight per large egg (50g)
  • Eggs needed = Target weight ÷ weight per your egg size

Example: Recipe calls for 3 large eggs (3 × 50 = 150g target). You have medium eggs (43g each). 150 ÷ 43 = 3.5 medium eggs. Crack 3 whole medium eggs plus roughly half of a fourth, or just use 3 medium eggs and add a splash of milk (~7g) to make up the liquid shortfall. For 1 or 2 eggs the difference is small enough to ignore; at 3+ eggs, do the math.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 large egg?

A1: One US large egg weighs approximately 50 grams without its shell. The white is about 30g and the yolk is about 18g. This 50g baseline is the standard assumed by virtually all American baking recipes.

Q2: How many grams is 1 egg white?

A2: One large egg white weighs approximately 30 grams. For other sizes: medium white ≈ 26g, extra-large white ≈ 33g, jumbo white ≈ 38g. When precision matters (meringues, macarons), weigh egg whites rather than counting them.

Q3: Why weigh eggs instead of counting them?

A3: Individual eggs within the same "large" carton can vary by 5–8 grams. In a 4-egg recipe, counting can give you anywhere from 188g to 232g — a 44-gram swing. Weighing eliminates this variability and produces consistent results every time.

Q4: What if a recipe calls for eggs at room temperature — does that change the weight?

A4: No. Bringing an egg to room temperature doesn't change its mass — it only changes the temperature. A 50g cold egg is still a 50g room-temperature egg. The reason recipes ask for room-temperature eggs is that cold eggs don't emulsify as well with room-temperature butter, and cold whites whip less voluminously. The weight is unchanged.

Q5: How do I weigh an egg — crack it first or weigh in shell?

A5: Always crack first. Place a small bowl on your scale, tare it to zero, then crack the egg into the bowl. The shell weight (~5–6g for a large egg in shell) is irrelevant to your recipe. If you need separated whites and yolks, crack the egg, fish out the yolk with your hand (clean hands work better than any gadget), and weigh the components in separate bowls.
