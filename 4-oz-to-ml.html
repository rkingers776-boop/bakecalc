4 oz to mL Converter

Convert 4 US fluid ounces to millilitres — the half-cup mark, and the ounce figure that collides most often with butter.

Milliliter Equivalent:

118.29 mL

*Based on standard US Customary fluid ounce calculations (1 fl oz ≈ 29.57353 mL).

4 oz in mL — the short answer

Four US fluid ounces is 118.29 mL — 4 × 29.5735 = 118.2941. Under the imperial fluid ounce it is 113.65 mL. This is one of the most frequently converted figures on the whole site, and the reason is simple: four fluid ounces is one of the few round quantities that lands on a standard measuring cup.

Half a Cup, Exactly

A US cup holds 8 fluid ounces, so 4 fl oz is exactly half a cup, or 118.2941 mL. Half a cup is the single most common liquid measure in American baking — half a cup of milk, of water, of buttermilk, of heavy cream.

The practical consequence is that if you own a half-cup measure, you do not need the millilitre figure at all. Where millilitres matter is on a metric jug, in nutrition maths, and in recipes where the liquid is expressed as a proportion of the flour, as it is in bread.

Why 4 oz Is the Most Useful of the Ounce Marks

Four fluid ounces is one of the few figures that sits on both scales at once. It is exactly half a cup, and it is close to 120 mL — a mark that genuinely exists on most metric jugs. So in a kitchen, 4 oz and 120 mL are treated as interchangeable, with an error of 1.45%.

What does not follow is that 120 mL is 120 g. Outside water, no ingredient has a density of exactly one. Half a cup of honey weighs 168 g despite occupying 118 mL; half a cup of oil weighs about 108 g. Two ingredients, one volume, a 60-gram spread.

Where 4 oz Shows Up

  • Baking — half a cup of liquid: milk, water, buttermilk, cream, brewed coffee.
  • Baby bottles — 4 oz is one of the most common feeding volumes in the first year, and the mark is on every bottle.
  • Ramekins — a standard small ramekin holds about 4 oz, which is why custard and creme brulee recipes are written in 4 oz portions.
  • Large cocktails — the total liquid volume in a number of tiki and punch recipes.

Four Ounces of Butter Is a Different Four Ounces

A US stick of butter is labelled 4 oz, and that is a weight — 113.4 grams. The same stick measures half a cup in volume. Both statements are true, they just rest on different scales, and butter is the one ingredient where everyone collides with the discrepancy.

Here the duality is harmless, and that is worth understanding rather than memorising. A recipe asking for 4 oz of butter and a recipe asking for half a cup of butter want the same stick. You cannot scoop butter into a fluid-ounce jug anyway, so nobody tries; they reach for the half-cup measure and land on the right 113 grams by accident.

The same reasoning fails the moment the ingredient changes. Four ounces of honey by weight is 113 g, which occupies only about a third of a cup. Substitute the fluid-ounce figure there and you are out by 40%. Butter is the exception, not the rule.

Getting the Last Digit Right

Four fluid ounces is one of the conversions where rounding barely costs you anything: 118.29 up to 120 is 1.45%. For an emulsified sauce, a gelatine solution, or a syrup where the liquid balance is structural, use 118. Everywhere else 120 is fine, and it is what most jugs will show you.

One figure worth distrusting is 125 mL. It appears in some chart conversions of 4 fl oz, but 125 mL is actually 4.23 fl oz — a 5.7% overshoot that has outgrown the rounding it came from and become a transcription error.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many mL is 4 oz?

A1: 118.29 mL for 4 US fluid ounces. British and Australian recipes use the imperial fluid ounce, where 4 oz is 113.65 mL — about 4% less.

Q2: Is 4 oz half a cup?

A2: Yes, exactly. A US cup holds 8 fluid ounces, so 4 fluid ounces is half a cup, 8 tablespoons, or 118.29 mL — all the same quantity described three ways.

Q3: Is 4 oz of butter the same as 4 fluid ounces?

A3: No, but on butter the two happen to agree. A US stick of butter is labelled 4 oz by weight — 113.4 grams — and that same stick measures half a cup in volume. Four fluid ounces of a liquid is 118.29 mL. Butter is the rare case where both readings lead to the same piece of fat.
