1/3 Cup Butter to Grams Converter

Convert ⅓ US cup of butter into grams — covering the solid-vs-melted confusion, the stick-marking trick, and why your butter knife is actually a measuring tool.

Gram Equivalent:

≈ 76 g (third cup) (76 g)

*Standard US butter density: 227g per cup. A full stick = 113g (1/2 cup). 1/3 cup = 76g, or 5 Tbsp + 1 tsp using stick wrapper markings. Clarified butter is slightly lighter — milk solids removal reduces density.

How Many Grams in 1/3 Cup of Butter?

One-third cup of butter weighs 76 grams. Unlike flour or sugar, butter has almost no measuring ambiguity — it's sold in standardized sticks (in the US) with tablespoon markings right on the wrapper, and its density is extremely consistent at 227 grams per cup. That consistency is a gift: whether you're using cheap store-brand butter or premium European-style butter with 82%+ butterfat, a cup is still 227 grams. The fat-to-water ratio varies slightly between brands (European butter has less water, more fat), but the total weight per cup remains effectively identical for baking purposes. 5 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon = 1/3 cup = 76g.

The Conversion Formula — Butter Is the Easiest Ingredient

Butter has one of the most reliable cup-to-gram conversions in baking. At 227 grams per cup, the math is clean and the real-world results are predictable:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • Solid or Melted Butter: 0.333 × 227 g = 76 grams
  • Stick Math: 1 stick = 113g = 1/2 cup, so 2/3 stick = 76g = 1/3 cup
  • Tablespoon Math: 1/3 cup = 5 Tbsp + 1 tsp; 1 Tbsp butter = ~14g, so 5.33 × 14 = ~76g

The Melted vs Solid Butter Trap (It's Not About Weight)

Here's where even experienced bakers get tripped up: solid butter and melted butter weigh the same by mass, but they don't occupy the same volume. Melt a stick of butter and it shrinks — the air trapped in the solid structure escapes, and the liquid butter takes up about 15-20% less space. This means ⅓ cup of solid butter (76g) is actually more butter than ⅓ cup of melted butter (about 85-90g if you melted ⅓ cup solid and then measured). The recipe writer's intention matters enormously here. If the recipe says "⅓ cup butter, melted," the standard interpretation is: measure ⅓ cup solid (76g), then melt it. If the recipe says "⅓ cup melted butter," it means: melt butter, then measure ⅓ cup of the liquid (which will be about 85-90g of original solid butter). In brownies and fudgy cookies, this ~12-gram difference in butter weight changes the fat-to-flour ratio enough to shift the texture from chewy to cakey. When in doubt, use a scale — 76 grams is unambiguous regardless of what state the butter is in.

Salted vs Unsalted Butter — Weight Is Identical, Behavior Is Not

Salted and unsalted butter both weigh 227g per cup — the salt content (typically 1.5-2% in salted butter) is too small to measurably change density. But there is a practical difference that matters at the ⅓-cup scale: salted butter contains slightly more water than unsalted. Salt is a preservative, and manufacturers compensate by leaving a touch more water in salted butter. This means 76g of salted butter has roughly 1-2 grams less actual butterfat than 76g of unsalted butter. In a full batch of cookies with a cup of butter, that difference is negligible. In a small-batch recipe with only ⅓ cup of butter (like a six-cookie batch or a single browned-butter sauce for two servings of pasta), that 1-2g fat difference can be the margin between a cookie that spreads nicely and one that pools into a greasy puddle. The solution is simple: bake with unsalted butter so you control the salt and the fat content independently. Where 1/3 Cup Butter Actually Shows Up

A third-cup of butter is the workhorse fat quantity for small-batch baking: the butter in a half-dozen chocolate chip cookies, the fat in a single 8-inch pan of cornbread, or the butter component in a roux for two servings of mac and cheese. It's also the butter amount in many single-layer cake recipes for a 6-inch round pan. In pie crust, ⅓ cup of butter (combined with the same amount of shortening or lard) is the fat for a single-crust 9-inch pie shell — and at that scale, switching from 76g of cold butter to 76g of room-temperature butter changes the crust from flaky to tough. Butter temperature matters as much as butter weight, and the ⅓-cup quantity appears often enough in small recipes that getting both right is the difference between "good enough" and "worth making again."

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/3 cup of butter?

A1: 76 grams. That's the same whether the butter is salted or unsalted. In stick terms, it's two-thirds of a standard US stick (a full stick = 113g = 1/2 cup). In tablespoon terms, it's 5 tablespoons plus 1 teaspoon. If you have a kitchen scale, just weigh 76g directly — it's faster than messing with wrapper markings and far more accurate.

Q2: Does melted butter weigh the same as solid butter?

A2: By mass — yes, 76g of solid butter melted is still 76g. But by volume — no. Melted butter takes up less space because the air pockets in solid butter collapse. This creates a recipe interpretation problem: "⅓ cup melted butter" means measure after melting (about 85-90g of original solid), while "⅓ cup butter, melted" means measure solid first (76g), then melt. The weight difference is roughly 10-12g, which is enough to change brownie or cookie texture. When accuracy matters, weigh the butter — 76 grams is unambiguous. If you're using melted butter in a recipe that calls for solid, you need about 15-20% more solid butter to yield the same melted volume, and the recipe's texture will still be different because melted butter interacts with flour and sugar differently during mixing.

Q3: How do I measure 1/3 cup of butter using stick markings?

A3: A standard US butter stick has tablespoon markings on the wrapper — each line is 1 tablespoon (14g). For 1/3 cup: count 5 full tablespoons, then add 1 teaspoon (one-third of the next tablespoon mark). Cut at the midpoint between the 5th and 6th tablespoon lines, slightly closer to the 5th. Even easier: the wrapper usually has a 1/4 cup (4 Tbsp) line clearly marked — go one tablespoon past that, then add a teaspoon. If your butter doesn't have markings (imported butter, blocks, tubs), the scale method wins hands down: 76g on a digital scale, no wrapper math needed. European butter blocks are often 250g — 76g is just under one-third of the block, which you can approximate by eye and then fine-tune on the scale.
