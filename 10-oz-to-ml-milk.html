10 oz of milk in mL — the short answer

Ten US fluid ounces of milk is 295.74 mL in volume — 10 × 29.5735, the same as any other liquid. Its weight, though, is about 305 grams, because milk is roughly 3% denser than water. Under the imperial fluid ounce the volume is 284.13 mL.

That gap between the volume answer and the weight answer is the whole subject of this page. It is small enough that most recipes survive ignoring it, and large enough that it matters in anything where the milk is a structural proportion of the formula rather than a splash.

Why Milk Is Heavier Than Water

Water is the reference point with a density of 1 g/mL. Milk is water with things dissolved and suspended in it: about 3.25% milkfat, 3.4% protein, 4.8% lactose and 0.7% minerals in whole milk. Those solids are denser than the water they displace, so the mixture comes out slightly heavier — typically 1.03 g/mL for whole milk.

The differences among milk types are smaller than most people expect:

  • Whole milk — about 1.03 g/mL, roughly 305 g per 10 fl oz.
  • Skim milk — about 1.035 g/mL, marginally heavier, because the fat that was removed is the lightest component.
  • Buttermilk — about 1.03 g/mL, essentially the same as whole milk.
  • Evaporated milk — about 1.06 g/mL, noticeably heavier because so much water has been removed.

So if a recipe gives milk by weight and you only have a jug, the safe conversion is 10 fl oz of milk equals about 305 g. If it gives milk by volume and you only have a scale, 305 g is your target.

Where 10 oz of Milk Shows Up

  • Baby bottles — 10 oz is a large feed, and one of the marked sizes.
  • Small cartons — 10 fl oz single-serve milk, common in cafeterias and on room-service trays.
  • Recipes — 1¼ cups is an odd quantity, so a formula asking for 10 oz of milk more often than not is an American recipe that was written in fluid ounces and never converted.
  • Coffee drinks — the milk volume in a large latte hovers around this figure.

Swapping Milk for Water Is Not a One-for-One Trade

Because milk is 87% water, it is tempting to treat the two as interchangeable. Volume for volume they will produce a dough or batter that looks similar, and then behaves differently in three specific ways.

  • Browning — lactose and milk proteins participate in the Maillard reaction. The same bake comes out darker and more evenly coloured. This is usually welcome in bread and unwelcome in a pale cake.
  • Tenderness — milkfat shortens gluten strands, so the crumb is softer. Water gives a chewier result; that is why a lean water-and-flour bread is chewy and an enriched milk bread is soft.
  • Structure — milk proteins add a small amount of extra framework, which is why an all-water brioche does not exist and why milk bread holds a taller shape.

None of those are about the millilitres. They are about the solids that came along with the water, and they are why a recipe that calls for milk is usually not asking for a liquid so much as for those specific ingredients in dissolved form.

When the Density Actually Matters

Three percent sounds like nothing, and in a cake batter it is. It starts to matter in custards and ice cream bases, where the milk or cream is most of the mass and the fat-to-water ratio determines how the mixture sets. It matters more in bread, where hydration is calculated against flour weight and a systematic 3% error in the liquid compounds across every loaf you bake.

The practical fix takes five seconds: pick one unit for your liquids and stay in it. Recipes written in fluid ounces are American, and American recipe writers usually measure milk in cups. Recipes written in grams are the ones worth weighing, and there the scale already gives you the right answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many mL is 10 oz of milk?

A1: 295.74 mL for 10 US fluid ounces, the same volume as any other liquid. Milk weighs more than that, though — about 305 grams, because milk is roughly 3% denser than water.

Q2: How many cups is 10 oz of milk?

A2: One and a quarter cups. A US cup is 8 fluid ounces, so 10 fluid ounces is 1.25 cups, or 20 tablespoons.

Q3: Can I substitute water for milk one for one?

A3: By volume it looks like a fair swap, but it is not. Milk brings about 3.25% fat, 3.4% protein and 4.8% lactose that water does not have. Those solids add colour to the crust, tenderness to the crumb, and a small amount of extra structure — so expect a visibly different result even at the same millilitre figure.
