100 g of butter in cups — the short answer

One hundred grams of butter is 0.44 cups, or 7 level tablespoons. In stick terms it is just under one American stick, which weighs 113.4 g.

The 13 g gap is small enough that many recipes treat 100 g and one stick as the same thing. It is worth knowing they are not, because the reason they differ is a packaging decision rather than a measurement one, and the same decision causes bigger problems higher up the scale.

Two Packaging Systems That Do Not Line Up

American butter is sold in quarter-pound sticks. A stick is therefore 113.4 g, which is exactly 4 ounces, which measures exactly half a cup, which is 8 tablespoons. Every one of those figures is chosen so the wrapper can carry printed tablespoon marks.

European and British butter is sold in 250 g blocks. There is nothing wrong with a 250 g block — it is a round metric quantity — but it does not divide into cups, sticks or tablespoons at all cleanly.

  • US stick — 113.4 g, half a cup, 8 tbsp
  • US stick, doubled — 226.8 g, one cup, 16 tbsp, usually sold as a one-pound pack of four sticks
  • European block — 250 g, 1.1 cups, 17.6 tbsp

So a recipe written for 250 g blocks and a recipe written for quarter-pound sticks are describing quantities that differ by more than 10% per unit. That is the source of a great deal of quiet drift when recipes cross the Atlantic in either direction.

Where 100 g of Butter Shows Up

  • Shortbread — the classic ratio is one part sugar, two parts butter, three parts flour by weight, so a small batch uses 100 g of butter.
  • Cookie dough for two — a half batch, where 100 g of butter is the whole fat content.
  • Buttercream — a small batch for filling a single layer cake.
  • Pie and tart crust — a single 9-inch shell, roughly.

Should You Just Use One Stick Instead?

For most baking, yes. The difference between 100 g and 113.4 g is 13%, and in a cookie or a crumble that is a textural nuance rather than a failure. Butter is fat, and slightly more fat makes a slightly richer, slightly flatter result.

There are two places where the substitution stops being safe. Laminated doughs — croissants, puff pastry, Danish — are built on exact fat-to-flour ratios, and an extra 13% changes how the layers set. And buttercream is an emulsion that can break if the fat proportion shifts, particularly when the butter is already at the edge of its temperature range.

Everywhere else, the wrapper markings will tell you what to do. Cut at the 7 tablespoon line and the problem disappears.

A Note on Melted Butter

Melting butter changes its volume, which is why recipes specify whether the butter should be measured solid or liquid. Melted butter has no air gaps, so the same mass settles into less space — roughly 4% less. One cup of solid butter is 227 g; one cup of melted butter is about 240 g.

At 100 g the difference is tiny, but the direction matters. If a recipe says 100 g of melted butter, weigh it after melting. If it says 100 g of butter, melted, weigh it before. The two instructions produce a small but real difference in the fat in the bowl.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 100 g of butter?

A1: About 0.44 cups, which is a little under half a cup. In tablespoons that is 7 level tablespoons, since a cup of butter is 227 grams and 16 tablespoons.

Q2: Is 100 g of butter the same as one stick?

A2: Almost. A US stick is 113.4 grams, so 100 grams is about 13 grams short — roughly one tablespoon less. Most recipes tolerate the difference; laminated pastry and precise buttercreams do not.

Q3: Why is butter sold in different weights in different countries?

A3: Packaging convention rather than anything technical. The United States settled on the quarter-pound stick, 113.4 grams, sized to measure half a cup. Most of Europe packages butter in 250 gram blocks, and the United Kingdom in 250 gram blocks as well.
