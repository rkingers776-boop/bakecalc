Convert ¼ US cup of powdered (confectioners) sugar into grams — sifted vs. unsifted, why it is so much lighter than granulated, and when those 5 grams make or break a glaze.

Gram Equivalent:

≈ 30 g (quarter cup, unsifted) (30 g)

*Unsifted powdered sugar weighs about 120g per cup (30g per quarter cup). Sifted powdered sugar weighs about 100g per cup (25g per quarter cup) — a 20% difference due to air incorporation. Most recipes assume unsifted unless "sifted" is specified.

How Many Grams in 1/4 Cup of Powdered Sugar?

Powdered sugar — also labeled confectioners sugar or icing sugar — is granulated sugar ground to a fine powder and mixed with about 3% cornstarch to prevent caking. That fine grind changes everything about how it measures. A quarter cup of unsifted powdered sugar weighs about 30 grams (120 grams per cup). Sift that same quarter cup and you lose about 5 grams to air — sifted powdered sugar weighs only 25 grams per quarter cup (100 grams per cup). The 5-gram difference between sifted and unsifted at the quarter-cup scale is a 20% swing, which is enormous in baking terms. For comparison, a quarter cup of granulated sugar weighs 50 grams — nearly double the weight of powdered sugar for the same volume. The explanation is particle size: granulated sugar crystals are relatively large and pack efficiently; powdered sugar particles are 10-20 times smaller and trap far more air between them in the measuring cup. The cornstarch contributes to the fluffiness too. This is why substituting powdered sugar for granulated sugar in a recipe by volume would be catastrophic — you would be adding less than half the sugar the recipe needs, with cornstarch you never accounted for.

The Conversion: Sifted vs. Unsifted — 5 Grams That Matter

The sifted-versus-unsifted distinction is not pedantic — it is a 20% weight difference at the quarter-cup level, and the instruction wording in a recipe tells you exactly which the developer intended:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • Unsifted Powdered Sugar: 0.25 × 120 g = 30 grams
  • Sifted Powdered Sugar: 0.25 × 100 g = 25 grams
  • "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" = sift first, then measure (100g/cup)
  • "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted" = measure first, then sift (120g/cup)

Why Powdered Sugar's Light Weight Makes It Perfect for Frosting (and Terrible for Cookie Dough)

The 30-gram weight of a quarter cup of powdered sugar is not just a number to memorize for conversion purposes — it explains why powdered sugar behaves the way it does in different applications. Frosting and glazes rely on powdered sugar precisely because it is so light and fine: the tiny particles dissolve almost instantly in the small amount of liquid (milk, cream, citrus juice) used in a glaze, producing a smooth, lump-free consistency without the grittiness that granulated sugar would leave behind. The cornstarch in the powdered sugar also acts as a thickener, helping the glaze set to a smooth, matte finish as it dries. But that same lightness makes powdered sugar a poor choice for cookie dough. A cookie recipe that calls for creaming butter and sugar together relies on the sharp edges of granulated sugar crystals cutting into the butter to create air pockets — the mechanical aeration that gives cookies their spread and texture. Powdered sugar particles are too small and too soft to cut into butter effectively; they dissolve into the fat instead of aerating it. Cookies made with powdered sugar spread less, have a denser, more cake-like texture, and develop a tender crumb more like shortbread. When Sifting Powdered Sugar Actually Matters (and When It Does Not)

If you are making an American buttercream — butter, powdered sugar, vanilla, and a splash of cream beaten together — you absolutely should sift the powdered sugar before adding it, because even one tiny lump of compacted sugar-and-cornstarch will resist dissolving and leave a gritty white speck in an otherwise smooth frosting. The sifting is not about weight accuracy in this context; it is about texture. If you are dusting powdered sugar over a finished cake or pastry through a fine-mesh sieve, the sifting is purely cosmetic — you want an even, snow-like dusting with no clumps. But if you are measuring powdered sugar by volume for a recipe and the recipe does not specify sifted versus unsifted, the default assumption in American home-baking recipes is unsifted, scooped-and-leveled powdered sugar at about 120 grams per cup (30g per quarter cup). This is the standard used by most recipe developers and cookbook authors. If the recipe was developed by a pastry chef or a European source, it may assume sifted weight — the only way to know for sure is to check how the recipe performs the first time you make it and adjust on the next attempt. Or just weigh your ingredients. A quarter cup of unsifted powdered sugar at 30 grams will always be 30 grams whether it has been sifted or not — the weight does not change when you sift; only the volume does. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Why does sifted powdered sugar weigh less than unsifted?

A1: Unsifted powdered sugar compacts in the bag over time — the fine particles settle into every available space, producing about 120 grams per cup (30g per quarter cup). Sifting breaks up those compacted clumps, separates the particles, and introduces air, reducing the density to about 100 grams per cup (25g per quarter cup). The same mass of sugar occupies about 20% more volume after sifting. This is why the recipe wording matters: "1 cup sifted powdered sugar" (sift then measure, 100g) is not the same as "1 cup powdered sugar, sifted" (measure then sift, 120g).

Q2: Why is powdered sugar lighter than granulated sugar?

A2: Granulated sugar crystals are relatively large (about 0.5 mm) and roughly cube-shaped, packing efficiently in a measuring cup at about 200 grams per cup. Powdered sugar particles are 10-20 times smaller and irregularly shaped, trapping far more air between particles — the same principle that makes fine sand have more total air space than coarse gravel. The added 3% cornstarch contributes to the lighter, fluffier consistency. At the quarter-cup level: granulated sugar = 50g, powdered sugar (unsifted) = 30g — a 40% weight difference for the same volume.

Q3: Should I sift powdered sugar before measuring for frosting and glazes?

A3: Yes — sift powdered sugar before measuring or weighing for any smooth application like buttercream, glaze, or royal icing. The purpose is lump removal, not weight accuracy. Powdered sugar's cornstarch absorbs ambient moisture and forms hard little lumps that will not dissolve in a frosting and will leave visible white specks. Sifting breaks those lumps apart. If measuring by volume, sift first, then lightly spoon into the cup without packing. If measuring by weight, weigh after sifting if the recipe calls for sifted sugar, or sift after weighing if the recipe calls for unsifted. At the quarter-cup level, the 5-gram difference between sifted (25g) and unsifted (30g) is enough to affect the consistency of a small-batch glaze.
