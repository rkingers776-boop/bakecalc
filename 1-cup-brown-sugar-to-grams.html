1 Cup Brown Sugar to Grams — and Why Your Cookies Need You to Read This

Packed, loose, light, dark, homemade — every brown sugar measurement variable, explained by someone who has ruined enough cookie batches to know exactly which mistakes matter.

Gram Equivalent:

200 grams (200 g)

*1 cup packed light brown sugar = 200g. Dark brown ≈ 210g/cup (more molasses = denser). Loose/not packed ≈ only 150g/cup — always pack unless the recipe says otherwise.

The Cookie Batch That Taught Me What "Packed" Actually Means

I was 19, making chocolate chip cookies from the Toll House bag for maybe the fifth time in my life. The recipe said "¾ cup packed brown sugar." I scooped brown sugar into a measuring cup — loosely, the way you'd scoop flour — leveled the top with a knife, and dumped it in. The dough looked pale. The cookies came out of the oven pale too: uniformly beige, puffed up like little pillows, tasting vaguely of butter and vanilla but missing that deep caramel chew that makes a chocolate chip cookie worth eating.

I had used maybe 110 grams of brown sugar. The recipe expected 150 grams (¾ of a packed cup at 200g/cup). I shorted the recipe by nearly 30% of its brown sugar without changing a single number. The missing molasses meant less moisture, less acidity (so less reaction with the baking soda for spread), and less caramelization. The cookies were basically butter-sugar-flour pucks. Edible, sure. But not what anyone means when they say "homemade chocolate chip cookie."

Here's the thing no recipe card tells you: brown sugar is the only common baking ingredient where the recipe wants you to pack it — to physically crush the air out. Flour? Spoon and level, never pack. Granulated sugar? Scoop and sweep. Cocoa powder? Spoon gently. Brown sugar? Press it in like you mean it. Why? Because brown sugar is moist from molasses and naturally clumpy — it traps giant air pockets when scooped loosely. A loosely filled cup might be 40% air. The same cup, packed, is near-zero air. That's the 50-60 gram difference between "I followed the recipe" and "I actually followed the recipe."

Light Brown vs Dark Brown: What 10 Grams and Double the Molasses Gets You

Light brown sugar (packed) = 200 grams per cup. Dark brown sugar (packed) = 210 grams per cup. The 10-gram difference comes from molasses content: light brown is about 3.5% molasses by weight; dark brown is about 6.5% — nearly double. That extra molasses adds density, moisture, acidity, and flavor.

I ran a side-by-side test: two batches of the same cookie dough, identical weights, one with light brown (200g/cup) and one with dark (210g/cup). After baking at 350°F (180°C) for 11 minutes:

  • Light brown cookies: Golden-brown edges, soft centers, classic butterscotch note. The cookie most people picture when they think "chocolate chip." Spread was moderate — about 3 inches across.
  • Dark brown cookies: Deeper amber color, noticeably chewier texture, a flavor that reads as "caramel" more than "butterscotch." Slightly less spread (more acid = faster structure set), slightly denser crumb. Tasted more "homemade" and less "slice-and-bake."

When does it matter which you use? For chocolate chip cookies, either works — the choice is stylistic. For gingerbread, molasses cookies, or anything where "deep and spicy" is the goal, dark brown is the right call; light brown will taste washed out. For delicate things like brown sugar meringue or a brown sugar simple syrup for cocktails, use light — dark's molasses punch overpowers subtle flavors. And if a recipe specifically calls for dark brown, it's usually because the molasses is doing chemical work (reacting with baking soda for leavening) — substituting light brown means less rise and a different texture. Make Brown Sugar From Scratch (It Takes 30 Seconds and Tastes Better)

If you bake regularly, stop buying brown sugar. Here's why: commercial brown sugar is just white sugar with molasses added back in — they refine the molasses out of raw sugar, then spray a measured amount back on. You're paying a premium for someone to un-mix and re-mix sugar. You can do it yourself in less time than it takes to drive to the store.

DIY Light Brown Sugar: 1 cup (200g) granulated white sugar + 1 tablespoon (15g) unsulphured molasses. Mix with a fork until the molasses is evenly distributed — the sugar will darken uniformly. If you have a food processor, pulse for 20-30 seconds for the fluffiest, most even result.

DIY Dark Brown Sugar: Same as above, but 2 tablespoons (30g) of molasses per cup of sugar. That's it.

By weight: DIY light brown ≈ 205g per packed cup (the added molasses contributes ~5g). DIY dark brown ≈ 210g per packed cup. These are close enough to commercial weights that no recipe will notice the difference. The homemade version is actually better — the molasses is fresher, the aroma is stronger, and you control the ratio. Want extra-dark? Add an extra teaspoon of molasses. Want something between light and dark? Use 1½ tablespoons. You can't do that with a bag from the store.

One catch: use unsulphured molasses (like Grandma's or Brer Rabbit). Sulphured molasses is treated with sulfur dioxide as a preservative and has a harsher, more chemical taste. It'll work in a pinch, but unsulphured is the standard for baking. Blackstrap molasses is a different product entirely — very bitter, very mineral — and I don't recommend it for brown sugar unless you know exactly what you're doing.

Brown Sugar Isn't Just for Cookies

Most people associate brown sugar exclusively with baking, but it earns its pantry space in savory cooking too. Brown sugar in a dry rub for ribs or pork shoulder melts into the meat as it cooks, balancing the salt and smoke with a molasses sweetness that granulated sugar can't match. A tablespoon of brown sugar in tomato sauce cuts acidity more smoothly than white sugar — the molasses adds a roundness that plain sucrose doesn't have. Stirred into hot coffee with a pinch of cinnamon, it's a poor man's latte flavoring that costs approximately nothing. Mixed 50/50 with softened butter and spread on toast under the broiler for 60 seconds, it's the topping from a bakery cinnamon roll without the dough work. Sticky Situations: Storing, Softening, and Saving Brown Sugar

Brown sugar has two enemies: air and time. Left exposed, the molasses dehydrates and the sugar turns into a rock you could use as a doorstop. Store it in an airtight container — the original plastic bag, folded over and clipped shut, is fine for a few weeks; a sealed glass jar or a heavy-duty zipper bag with the air squeezed out is better for longer.

If it's already a brick: put the hardened sugar in a bowl, lay a damp (not wet) paper towel over the top without touching the sugar, cover tightly, and microwave in 15-second bursts. The steam rehydrates the molasses. It usually takes 30-45 seconds total for a cup of sugar. Fluff with a fork and it's as good as new. Alternatively, toss a slice of fresh bread or a marshmallow into the container overnight — the sugar draws moisture from it and softens. The bread trick sounds like an old wives' tale but I've done it; it works.

For those who bake with brown sugar maybe twice a year: just make it from scratch when you need it. A jar of molasses lasts essentially forever in the pantry, and your granulated sugar is always fresh. No more chiseling apart a fossilized lump of brown sugar at 10 p.m. because you promised cookies for the school bake sale tomorrow. Questions About Brown Sugar That Come Up in Real Kitchens

How many grams in 1 cup of brown sugar?

200 grams for packed light brown sugar. 210 grams for packed dark brown. 140-160 grams if you scoop loosely (don't do that unless the recipe specifically says "loosely packed"). This is one of the largest measurement variances in baking — the same measuring cup can deliver anywhere from 140g to 210g depending solely on how you fill it. Weighing eliminates the problem entirely: 200g on a scale is 200g regardless of packing technique. For other sugars that have their own measurement quirks, powdered sugar cups to grams has its own set of rules (hint: sifting order matters).

Is brown sugar heavier than white sugar?

By volume, they're effectively the same when measured correctly: packed brown sugar = 200g/cup, granulated white sugar = 200g/cup. But they arrive at that number by completely different paths. White sugar is dense because the uniform crystals pack efficiently on their own. Brown sugar is dense because you physically press the air out. Loose brown sugar (unpacked) is only ~150g/cup — dramatically lighter. So the real answer: properly measured brown sugar equals white sugar in weight, but improperly measured brown sugar is 25% lighter. This is why recipes that use brown sugar almost always say "packed" and recipes that use white sugar never do — white sugar doesn't need the instruction; it packs itself.

Can I use brown sugar instead of white sugar in a cake?

You can, but the cake will be different in three ways: it'll be moister (molasses is hygroscopic — it holds onto water), darker in color, and have a subtle caramel/molasses flavor. For a vanilla cake, this might be a downgrade (the molasses mutes the vanilla). For a spice cake, carrot cake, or banana bread, it's an upgrade — the molasses complements those flavors perfectly. If substituting 1:1 by weight, you may also need a tiny pinch more baking soda, since the extra acidity from molasses will react with it. For the full acid-base leavening picture, baking powder vs baking soda explains when the swap matters and when it doesn't.

What's the difference between brown sugar and raw sugar (turbinado)?

Brown sugar is refined white sugar with molasses added back in. Turbinado (raw sugar) is partially refined — it retains some of its natural molasses from the cane but in much smaller amounts than brown sugar, and the crystals are larger and coarser. Raw sugar weighs about 180-190g per cup (lighter than both white and brown) because the large, irregular crystals don't pack as tightly. You can't swap raw sugar for brown sugar 1:1 and expect the same result — the moisture content, acidity, and dissolution rate are all different. If a recipe relies on brown sugar's moisture for texture (like chewy cookies), raw sugar won't deliver.
