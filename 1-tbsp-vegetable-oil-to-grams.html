1 tablespoon of vegetable oil in grams — the short answer

One tablespoon of vegetable oil is 13.6 grams. A US tablespoon holds 14.79 millilitres, oil runs at about 0.92 grams per millilitre, and the product is 13.6 g.

The interesting comparison is not with water but with butter, because those are the two fats a recipe is most likely to be choosing between.

A Tablespoon of Oil Against a Tablespoon of Butter

Put them side by side and the weights look close: 13.6 g of oil against 14.2 g of butter. The butter is actually heavier. But weight is not what a recipe is buying when it asks for fat, and the two spoons are not equivalent.

  • 1 tbsp vegetable oil — 13.6 g, of which 13.6 g is fat
  • 1 tbsp butter — 14.2 g, of which about 11.4 g is fat, 2.4 g is water, and 0.4 g is milk solids

So the oil delivers roughly 19% more fat than the butter does, in a spoon whose weight is only 4% lighter. That is the whole point of the distinction: butter is a fat plus water, and the water is not doing the fat's job.

It follows that the swaps run in opposite directions:

  • Replacing 1 tbsp of oil needs about 1 1/4 tbsp of butter, or 17 g.
  • Replacing 1 tbsp of butter needs about 2 1/2 tsp of oil, or 11.4 g.

The Water You Remove and Forget to Add Back

Swapping a tablespoon of butter for two and a half teaspoons of oil balances the fat, but it removes 2.4 g of water from the recipe. On a single tablespoon that is nothing.

Scaled up it stops being nothing. A recipe using eight tablespoons of butter — half a cup — carries about 20 g of water. Replace all of it with oil and the batter loses nearly three tablespoons of liquid, which is enough to change the consistency visibly and to reduce the steam that was helping the bake. Any competent butter-to-oil conversion adds that liquid back.

Where Small Measures Get Dangerous

A tablespoon is 13.6 g, which is about as small as a kitchen scale handles gracefully. Most home scales resolve to 1 g, so the reading is either 13 or 14, and the true value sits between them. That is a 7% uncertainty before you have even considered the oil left on the spoon.

The usual advice — measure oil by volume because it is incompressible and therefore reliable — is right, and here it is the better option rather than a compromise. A tablespoon measure filled to the brim and poured out is a more consistent 13.6 g than a scale reading of "13 or 14".

What a Tablespoon of Oil Does in a Recipe

  • Finishes a pan sauce — a tablespoon whisked in off the heat, where it rounds the acidity and adds body.
  • Greases a pan — a tablespoon is enough for an 8 inch round; more just pools.
  • Lets bread dough be kneaded — a tablespoon worked into a stiff dough softens it and stops it tearing.
  • Keeps a lean cake moist — in a low-fat sponge, a single tablespoon of oil noticeably delays staling.
  • Stops a sauce splitting — a tablespoon whisked into a sauce at the end helps emulsify it.

Notice that none of these is a structural job. At 13.6 g the fat is too small a fraction of any batter to control its texture, so this is the quantity where exactness matters least and where a half-gram of drift is genuinely invisible.

What the Spoon Keeps

At this volume the oil left on the spoon is proportionally larger than anything left in a cup. A tablespoon measure retains perhaps 0.2 to 0.3 g of oil as a film, which is around 2% of the contents.

It is a real loss and not worth chasing, because the recipe has the same tolerance. Where it does matter is in a sequence: measuring three separate tablespoons and leaving film on each shortchanges the recipe by about a gram, which is 7% of a single spoonful. Scrape the spoon with a small spatula, or measure once and pour.

Why Oil Is the Fat You Can Measure Loosely

Oil has no air in it, so it cannot be packed. Scoop it, pour it, let it settle — the density does not change. That makes it unlike flour, cocoa, or powdered sugar, all of which can vary by 20% or more depending on how the cup was filled.

The one thing volume measurement of oil gets wrong is the meniscus and the reading angle, and both are solved by setting the measure on the counter and reading at eye level rather than holding the spoon up to the light.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 tablespoon of vegetable oil?

A1: 13.6 grams. A US tablespoon is 14.79 millilitres and oil has a density of about 0.92 grams per millilitre, which gives 13.6 grams.

Q2: Is a tablespoon of oil the same as a tablespoon of butter?

A2: No. A tablespoon of butter weighs 14.2 grams but is only about 80% fat, so it contains roughly 11.4 grams of fat against the oil's 13.6. The oil carries about a fifth more fat in the same spoon.

Q3: How do I replace 1 tablespoon of butter with oil?

A3: Use about 2 1/2 teaspoons of oil, or 11.4 grams. That matches the butter's fat content without adding the water and milk solids the butter brought along.
