300 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

Three hundred grams of granulated sugar is exactly 1.5 cups — one cup plus a half cup. It divides cleanly at 200 g per cup, and it also happens to be three measures of 100 g, which makes it one of the easiest large quantities to build by either route.

If you are weighing, 300 g on the scale. If you are measuring, one cup and a half cup. Both land on the same sugar, because granulated sugar is one of the few ingredients where the cup measure genuinely holds up.

Why the Sugar-to-Flour Ratio Matters More Than Either Figure

American cake recipes express sugar and flour in the same unit, which suggests they are comparable quantities. They are not. A cup of sugar is 200 g and a cup of flour is 120 g, so a recipe calling for one cup of each is using two thirds more sugar than flour by weight while appearing to use equal amounts.

That is why recipes written in grams are easier to reason about. With 300 g of sugar in hand, the flour weight tells you immediately what kind of cake you are making:

  • Flour equal to sugar — 300 g each. A rich, tender, close-crumbed cake, close to a pound cake in proportion.
  • Flour 25% above sugar — around 375 g. The standard butter cake ratio, still tender but with enough structure to hold a filling.
  • Flour double the sugar — 600 g. A lean, sturdy crumb, closer to a sweet bread than a cake.

Those three cakes differ in texture more than in sweetness, and the difference comes entirely from a ratio that the cup system conceals. Reading a cup recipe, all three look like reasonable proportions; reading the weights, the structure of each is obvious.

Where 300 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • Two-layer cakes — a pair of nine-inch rounds typically takes 300 to 400 g of sugar in total.
  • Large batches of cookies — two to three dozen, depending on size.
  • Swiss meringue buttercream — the sugar is cooked with egg whites, and 300 g makes enough to fill and coat a two-layer cake.
  • Breads and rolls — a large enriched dough, at roughly 15% sugar against the flour.

Building 1.5 Cups From What You Own

One cup plus a half cup, both levelled. Nothing partial is needed, which puts 300 g in the small group of weights that build exactly from standard measures.

If you prefer to think in spoons, 1.5 cups of sugar is 24 level tablespoons. If you prefer to think in thirds — which is how most people divide a recipe — 300 g is three 100 g portions, each of which is half a cup. So half a batch of 600 g, or a third of 900 g, both fall out without arithmetic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 300 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly one and a half cups of granulated sugar — one cup plus a level half cup. Sugar measures 200 grams per cup, and 300 is one and a half times that.

Q2: How much flour goes with 300 g of sugar?

A2: For a standard layer cake, roughly 360 to 400 grams of flour. That is a sugar-to-flour ratio of about 0.8 to 1 by weight, which is the middle of the range for butter cakes. Pushing the sugar higher makes a tenderer, more fragile crumb; lowering it makes a sturdier, drier one.

Q3: Is 300 g of sugar too much for a cake?

A3: For a two-layer cake, no — it is typical. For a single layer, it would be very sweet and structurally weak. The right question is not how much sugar, but how much sugar relative to the flour and eggs, which is the ratio that actually determines the result.
