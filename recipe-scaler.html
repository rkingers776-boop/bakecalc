I Doubled the Cake and It Was Raw in the Middle

The first time I tried to turn my mom's 8-inch vanilla cake into a 10-inch cake for my son's birthday party, I multiplied everything by 1.25. Because 10 is 25% bigger than 8, right?

Wrong. The batter overflowed the pan. The middle was raw soup at the 35-minute mark. At 55 minutes, the edges were burnt and the center was still jiggly. I served a grocery-store backup cake at my own kid's party.

The problem wasn't the recipe. It wasn't my oven. It was my math. I'd used the ratio of diameters (10/8 = 1.25) when I should have used the ratio of areas (π × 5² / π × 4² = 1.56). I'd underscaled the recipe by 20% and overbaked it trying to compensate. If you've been there, this tool is for you.

The One Number That Changes Everything

Recipe scaling comes down to one number: the scale factor. It's simply:

Scale Factor = Target Yield ÷ Original Yield

Original recipe makes 12 cupcakes, you need 30? Factor is 30/12 = 2.5×. Multiply every ingredient by 2.5. That's the easy part. The hard part is knowing when that simple multiplication breaks down — and what to do about it.

Why You Can't Just Double Everything

For most ingredients — flour, sugar, butter, milk, oil — yes, just multiply by the scale factor. But three categories need a lighter touch:

1. Leaveners (baking powder & baking soda): When scaling up more than 2×, use about 1.75× the leavener instead of a full 2×. Extra leavening produces too much gas too fast — the batter rises dramatically, then collapses before the structure sets. You get a cake with a crater in the middle. (Yes, I've done this. Multiple times.)

2. Spices and salt: Taste is logarithmic, not linear. Doubling cinnamon doesn't double the perception of cinnamon — it can go from "warm spice note" to "this tastes like a candle." For strongly-flavored ingredients, scale by 1.5× first, taste the batter if it's safe, and adjust.

3. Baking time: A doubled batch baked in two separate pans of the same size takes roughly the same time. A doubled batch baked in one larger/deeper pan takes longer — but not 2× longer. Start checking at about 1.3× the original time and trust the toothpick test over the clock.

The Pan Size Trap: Diameter vs. Area

This is the mistake almost every baker makes the first time. Pan capacity doesn't scale with diameter — it scales with area. And area scales with radius squared (π × r²).

An 8-inch round pan: radius = 4 inches, area = π × 4² ≈ 50.3 sq in.
A 10-inch round pan: radius = 5 inches, area = π × 5² ≈ 78.5 sq in.
Ratio: 78.5 ÷ 50.3 = 1.56× — not 10/8 = 1.25×.

If you'd used 1.25×, you'd have 20% less batter than you need. Your cake would be thin, dry, and overbaked. The table below has the correct factors for every common pan swap.

Eggs: The Half-Egg Problem (Solved)

Scaling recipes inevitably produces fractional eggs. Your original cake uses 3 eggs and you need 1.5× — that's 4.5 eggs. What's half an egg?

The right answer: weigh it. Crack the eggs into a bowl, beat with a fork until the white and yolk are fully combined (10 seconds of light beating), then pour out the weight you need. One large egg out of the shell weighs approximately 50 grams. So 4.5 eggs = 225g of beaten egg.

This is more accurate than trying to eyeball "half an egg" and it avoids waste — whatever you don't use goes into scrambled eggs for the baker. A digital kitchen scale that reads to 1 gram costs about $12 and it's the single best investment you can make as a baker. I use mine every single day.

How This Relates to Baker's Percentage

If you've read our guide to baker's percentage, you know that professional bakers express every ingredient as a percentage of the flour weight. Here's exactly how the math works for a real recipe:

Original (8-inch, 2 layers, ~12 servings):

  • 2½ cups all-purpose flour
  • 1½ cups sugar
  • ¾ cup butter
  • 3 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tsp vanilla
  • 2½ tsp baking powder
  • ½ tsp salt

Scaled (10-inch, 2 layers):

  • 3¾ cups + 2 tbsp flour  (2.5 × 1.56 = 3.9 cups)
  • 2⅓ cups sugar  (1.5 × 1.56 = 2.34)
  • 1 cup + 3 tbsp butter  (0.75 × 1.56 = 1.17)

  • 1½ cups + 1 tbsp milk  (1 × 1.56 = 1.56)
  • 3 tsp vanilla
  • 3½ tsp baking powder  (scaled slightly under 1.56×)

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I scale a baking recipe up or down?

Divide your target yield by the original yield to get the scale factor, then multiply every ingredient amount by that factor. Example: original serves 12, you need 18 — factor is 18/12 = 1.5×. Multiply 2 cups flour by 1.5 = 3 cups. Most ingredients scale linearly, but leaveners, spices, and baking time need adjustments (see the sections above).

Can I just double all the ingredients in a recipe?

For most ingredients — yes. For leaveners (baking powder/soda), use about 1.75× instead of 2× when doubling to avoid over-rising and collapse. For spices and salt, start at 1.5× and taste. For baking time, do NOT double it — a doubled recipe in the same pan size (two pans) takes roughly the same time; in one larger pan, start checking at ~1.3× the original time.

How do I convert a cake recipe for a different pan size?

Compare the areas, not the diameters. For round pans: area = π × radius². An 8-inch pan (radius 4) has an area of ~50 sq in. A 9-inch pan (radius 4.5) has ~64 sq in. The scale factor is 64/50 = 1.28×. For rectangular pans: area = length × width. The tool above has built-in presets for all common pan swaps.

How do I handle half an egg when scaling?

Beat the eggs together, then weigh out what you need. One large egg out of the shell = ~50g. Half an egg = 25g beaten egg. This is far more accurate than trying to eyeball it. See our complete egg weight guide for all sizes (medium through jumbo).

Does baking time change when I scale a recipe?

It depends on whether you're baking in the same pan size (multiple pans) or a larger single pan. Multiple pans of the same thickness: same time. One larger/deeper pan: longer time. Rule of thumb — for every 50% increase in batter depth, add ~25% more time. Always use the toothpick test: insert in the center, it should come out clean or with a few moist crumbs.

What's the difference between scaling by servings and scaling by pan size?

Scaling by servings is linear: 8 servings → 12 servings = 1.5×. Scaling by pan size uses area: 8-inch round → 10-inch round = 1.56× (not 1.25×). The diameter ratio gives the wrong answer because pan capacity depends on area (radius²). When a recipe says "makes one 8-inch cake," you're scaling by pan size, not by servings — use the pan presets in the tool above.
