How Sourdough Hydration Works (and Why Your Number Is Probably Wrong)

Here's the thing most beginner sourdough bakers get wrong: they calculate hydration as water ÷ flour and call it a day. 500g flour + 350g water = 70%, easy. But that 100g of starter you added? It's not just "starter." It's 50g of flour and 50g of water (if you feed at 100% hydration, which almost everyone does). So your real total flour is 550g and your real total water is 400g — giving you a final dough hydration of 72.7%, not 70%. That 2.7 percentage point difference changes how your dough behaves. It's stickier than you expected because it's actually wetter than you thought.

This calculator does that math — including multi-flour blends, non-standard starter hydrations, and salt suggestion — instantly. No spreadsheets, no scratching numbers on a flour-dusted notepad.

What the Numbers Actually Mean for Your Bread

Hydration isn't just a number to brag about on Instagram. It directly controls three things about your bread:

  • Crumb structure. Lower hydration (60-65%) = tight, even crumb — good for sandwiches that need to hold fillings. Higher hydration (75-82%) = open, irregular crumb with those dramatic holes everyone posts photos of. The holes aren't just for show — an open crumb means the dough had enough water for the gluten to stretch into thin, translucent membranes around gas pockets.
  • Dough handling. Every percentage point above 70% makes the dough noticeably stickier and harder to shape. At 80%+ you're not really "shaping" in the traditional sense — you're coaxing. A bench scraper and wet hands stop being optional somewhere around 72%.
  • Crust and keeping quality. Higher hydration doughs produce bread that stays fresh longer — the extra water creates more steam during baking, which gelatinizes more starch in the crust. That's why a 78% hydration sourdough boule is still edible on day 4 while a 60% hydration sandwich loaf is getting stale on day 2.

Starter Hydration: Why 100% Is Standard (and What Happens If It's Not)

Almost every home sourdough recipe assumes your starter is fed at 100% hydration — equal weights of flour and water. That's the default for good reason: it's easy to remember, easy to mix, and the math is simple (half flour, half water). But if you maintain a stiffer starter — say 60% hydration for a lievito madre-style culture — or a liquid one at 125%+, the contribution to your final dough changes. A stiff starter brings more flour and less water to the party. That 100g of 60% hydration starter contributes roughly 62.5g flour and 37.5g water, not 50/50. If your recipe was formulated for 100% hydration starter and you use a stiff one without adjusting, your final dough will be drier — potentially by enough to notice. Our calculator handles any starter hydration you throw at it.

Flour Blends Matter Too

Not all flour drinks water at the same rate. Whole wheat flour absorbs about 10-15% more water than white bread flour. Rye absorbs even more — up to 20% more. So a dough at 75% hydration made with 100% bread flour handles differently than one at 75% hydration with 30% whole wheat. The whole wheat version feels drier and firmer because the bran is soaking up water that would otherwise be free in the dough. This calculator tracks your flour blend percentages so you can see exactly how each flour contributes to the total — useful if you're trying to dial in a specific blend or troubleshoot why a new formula feels different from an old one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What hydration should I start with as a beginner?

65-68%. At this level the dough is cohesive enough to shape without a fight but wet enough to produce decent oven spring and a respectable crumb. Start here, bake a few loaves until they're consistent, then bump to 70%. Jumping straight to 78% as a first-timer is a recipe for a floury countertop, a sticky blob, and a pancake loaf that makes you want to quit. Work your way up gradually — your hands need to learn the feel of wet dough as much as your brain needs to understand the theory.

Does salt count toward hydration?

No. Salt is typically 2% of flour weight (baker's percentage) and doesn't meaningfully affect hydration. The calculator suggests 2% salt based on your total flour — you can adjust it. Salt is included in total dough weight but not in the hydration calculation.

What if I add oil, honey, or other liquids?

Oil and fat don't count toward hydration in the traditional baker's percentage system — hydration specifically means water relative to flour. Honey, milk, and other water-containing liquids do contribute water. Milk is about 87% water; honey is about 17% water. If you're using significant amounts of these, you'd need to calculate their water contribution separately. For most home sourdough recipes (flour, water, starter, salt), this isn't an issue — it's just flour, water, starter, and salt.

My dough feels way wetter than the hydration % suggests. Why?

Two possibilities. One: your flour is low-protein (AP flour at 10% protein absorbs less water than bread flour at 13%). Two: you're using volume measurements instead of a scale — a "cup" of flour can vary by 30% in weight. Switch to a scale and make sure you're using bread flour (12-14% protein) for sourdough. If you're already doing both and it's still too wet, drop your hydration by 3-5% — climate matters. A humid kitchen in Florida at 75% hydration handles completely differently from a dry kitchen in Colorado at the same number.
