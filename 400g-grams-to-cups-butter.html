400 g of butter in cups — the short answer

Four hundred grams of butter is 1.76 cups — one and three quarter cups — which is 28 level tablespoons or three and a half US sticks.

At this quantity the interesting content is not the conversion but the reason the number is large. Four hundred grams is the scale a laminated dough is built on, and in lamination butter plays a structural role that no other baking technique asks of it.

Butter as a Sheet, Not an Ingredient

In most baking butter is mixed into a batter or rubbed into flour. In laminated pastry it is neither. The butter is beaten into a flat rectangle — the butter block, or beurrage — and folded inside a sheet of dough. The package is rolled out and folded repeatedly, and each fold multiplies the number of alternating layers of dough and fat.

Three folds take the count to 27 layers; a fourth takes it past 80. In the oven the water in each butter layer turns to steam, and the steam pushes the layers apart. The flaky structure of a croissant or a puff pastry is not a texture that emerges from a formula — it is manufactured, layer by layer, out of butter.

Which is why the quantity is large. A croissant dough takes a butter block of 250 to 400 g in addition to whatever butter went into the dough itself, and the ratio between the block and the dough is deliberately kept close to one to one. Cut the butter back to save calories and the layers simply do not separate.

Getting the Butter to the Right Temperature

Lamination is a temperature exercise more than a mixing one. The butter block must be cold enough to stay distinct from the dough and warm enough to bend without cracking. The working range is roughly 13 to 16 °C, and it is narrower than most recipes admit.

  • Block too cold — it fractures into shards as the dough is rolled, and those shards tear the layers apart. The pastry comes out greasy and uneven.
  • Block too warm — it squashes into the dough and the two merge, leaving a bready texture with no distinct layers at all.
  • Block just right — it bends under its own weight and holds a clean edge when cut.

The practical method is to keep everything cold and rest the dough in the fridge between folds. If the butter starts to soften at any point, stop rolling and chill — the dough will not suffer from waiting, but it cannot recover from warm butter.

Where 400 g of Butter Shows Up

  • Croissants — a full batch of dough, with the block folded in.
  • Puff pastry — a rough puff for one large tart or a batch of turnovers.
  • Danish and babka — enriched laminated doughs, where the butter is layered rather than creamed.
  • Large celebration bakes — a tiered cake plus its frosting, taken together.

Should the Butter Be American or European?

For lamination, higher fat is better. American butter is at least 80% butterfat; European-style butter runs 82 to 85%. The extra fat and correspondingly lower water content mean the layers are richer and hold their shape more cleanly, and the pastry rises higher before it sets.

The difference is real but not dramatic, and it is worth knowing where it comes from. Water in the butter becomes steam, and steam is what lifts the layers — so more water sounds like more lift. In practice, the extra water also makes the dough stickier and weakens the gluten, and the net effect of a higher-fat butter is a cleaner, crisper result. For a first attempt at croissants, standard butter will work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 400 g of butter?

A1: About 1.76 cups — one and three quarter cups, or 28 level tablespoons. In US stick terms it is three and a half sticks.

Q2: Why do croissants need so much butter?

A2: Because the butter is not an ingredient mixed into the dough, it is a sheet folded between layers of dough. Each fold multiplies the number of fat layers, and steam from the butter separates them during baking. A batch of croissant dough uses a butter block of 250 to 400 grams, separate from any butter in the dough itself.

Q3: Does the butter need to be cold for lamination?

A3: Cold but plastic — around 13 to 16 °C. Too hard and it cracks into shards inside the dough and tears the layers; too soft and it squashes into the dough and disappears. The test is that it bends without breaking.
