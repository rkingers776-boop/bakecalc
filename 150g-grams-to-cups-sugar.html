150 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

One hundred and fifty grams of granulated sugar is exactly three quarters of a cup, or 12 level tablespoons. At 200 g per cup, the arithmetic divides to a clean fraction that almost lands on a standard measure.

Almost, because three quarters of a cup is the fraction most measuring sets leave out. This is the same problem that 6 fluid ounces runs into on the liquid side of the site, and it has the same solution: build it from the measures you already own.

Building Three Quarters From a Half and a Quarter

A level half cup is 100 g of sugar. A level quarter cup is 50 g. Together they are 150 g, exactly, with no partial fill and no estimating:

  • 1/2 cup measure — 100 g of granulated sugar
  • 1/4 cup measure — 50 g of granulated sugar
  • Combined — 150 g, exactly three quarters of a cup

The alternative is 12 level tablespoons, which is slower but needs only one implement. Either route works because sugar measures so consistently — the reason this fraction is more reliable in sugar than it would be in flour is that the scoops themselves are not the variable.

Where 150 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • Shortbread and butter cookies — a classic ratio is one part sugar to two parts butter to three parts flour by weight, so the sugar figure sits near 150 g for a standard batch.
  • Single cake layers — a nine-inch round usually takes 150 to 200 g of sugar.
  • Meringue — a four-egg meringue takes roughly 200 g; three eggs brings it near 150 g.
  • Jam and preserves — small batches, where sugar is roughly equal to fruit by weight.

The Sugar to Flour Ratio Is the Important Number

Sugar and flour are both measured in cups in American recipes, which hides the fact that a cup of each weighs very different amounts. A cup of sugar is 200 g and a cup of flour is 120 g, so a recipe calling for one cup of each is using 67% more sugar than flour by weight.

That is a very different formula from one calling for 150 g of sugar and 300 g of flour, which is a 1 to 2 ratio by weight. Both are reasonable cakes, and they behave nothing alike:

  • Sugar exceeding flour by weight — a high-ratio cake, tender, moist, prone to collapsing if underbaked.
  • Sugar roughly half the flour by weight — a leaner, sturdier crumb that holds its shape well.
  • Equal weights — the middle ground most layer cakes occupy.

Because cups hide this ratio, a recipe written in cups can be scaled into trouble by someone converting roughly. Writing the weights down makes the proportions visible, and at 150 g of sugar the comparison against the flour is easy to make at a glance.

That is more than double, and it is entirely a property of what the sugar is: coarse moist crystals, fine dry powder, or something in between. Any recipe that swaps sugars by cup rather than by weight is unknowingly changing the quantity by up to 50%.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 150 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly three quarters of a cup of granulated sugar, which is 12 level tablespoons. Sugar runs 200 grams per cup, so 150 is three quarters of that with no rounding.

Q2: How do I measure 3/4 cup if my set has no 3/4 measure?

A2: Add a level half cup to a level quarter cup. Half of a cup is 100 grams and a quarter cup is 50 grams, so the pair gives exactly 150 grams. Twelve level tablespoons also works, but takes longer.

Q3: Is 3/4 cup of sugar a lot?

A3: It is a moderate amount — enough to sweeten a single cake layer or a batch of shortbread, and about a third of what a full two-layer cake recipe calls for. In a recipe using 150 grams of flour, 150 grams of sugar would be a very sweet cake, since sugar outweighs flour at that ratio.
