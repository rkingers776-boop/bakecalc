1 Tbsp Sugar to Grams Converter

Convert one tablespoon of sugar into grams — twelve and a half, and the reason the spoon works better here than it does with flour.

Gram Equivalent:

12.5 grams (12.5 g)

*Granulated sugar: 1 cup = 200g, 1 tbsp = 1/16 cup. Packed brown sugar ≈ 13.8g/tbsp. Powdered sugar ≈ 7.5g/tbsp.

1 tablespoon of sugar in grams — the short answer

One level tablespoon of granulated sugar is 12.5 grams. A cup is 200 g and holds 16 tablespoons, so the division is exact, and each teaspoon comes out at a little over 4 g.

What makes this figure more useful than the equivalent for flour is not the number but the reliability. Sugar crystals resist compaction, so a level tablespoon of sugar weighs 12.5 g whether it was spooned into the measure or pressed in from a full bag. A level tablespoon of flour can vary by 20% between the same two approaches.

Why the Spoon Works Better for Sugar

A tablespoon is a small volume, and small volumes are unforgiving when the ingredient is compressible. Flour heaps and packs, and the difference between a level spoon and a heaped one can be nearly double. Sugar has the same heaping problem but not the packing one: a heap of sugar crystals is loose and predictable, and levelling it gives the same weight every time.

The practical upshot is that a recipe can reasonably ask for tablespoons of sugar and mean it. The same recipe would be better off giving flour in grams.

  • 1 tsp of sugar — about 4.2 g
  • 1 tbsp of sugar — 12.5 g
  • 2 tbsp of sugar — 25 g, or 1/8 cup
  • 4 tbsp of sugar — 50 g, or 1/4 cup
  • 8 tbsp of sugar — 100 g, or 1/2 cup

Where a Tablespoon of Sugar Shows Up

  • Whipping cream — a tablespoon per cup is the usual sweetening, added once the cream begins to thicken.
  • Meringue — sugar is added a spoonful at a time so it dissolves before the next addition, and each spoonful is about one tablespoon.
  • Yeast activation — a spoonful of sugar in the warm water feeds the yeast and proves it is alive.
  • Finishing — a spoonful sprinkled over a tart or stirred into a sauce at the end, where the quantity is a matter of taste.

In all four cases the sugar is added for a reason other than bulk sweetness, and in all four the spoon is the natural tool. That is worth noticing: tablespoons of sugar appear in recipes where the sugar is acting on something else — stabilising a foam, feeding a microorganism, balancing an acidity.

Tablespoons to Grams for Every Sugar

  • Granulated white sugar — 12.5 g
  • Brown sugar, packed — 13.8 g
  • Powdered sugar, unsifted — 7.5 g
  • Powdered sugar, sifted — 6.3 g
  • Coconut sugar — 11.3 g
  • Turbinado or raw sugar — 12.5 g

Powdered sugar at a tablespoon is worth pausing on, because it weighs only about 60% of what granulated does. A recipe that swaps them by spoon will end up with less sugar and more cornstarch than intended, and the cornstarch will thicken any liquid it meets.

When a Tablespoon Is Not a Tablespoon

The figure of 12.5 g assumes a US tablespoon, which is 14.79 mL. Three other tablespoons are in circulation and the sugar weight follows the volume:

  • US customary tablespoon — 14.79 mL, 12.5 g of sugar
  • US nutrition-label tablespoon — 15 mL, 12.7 g
  • Australian tablespoon — 20 mL, 16.9 g, because it is four teaspoons rather than three
  • Metric tablespoon — 15 mL in most European cookbooks, though older ones use 20 mL

The Australian difference is the one that actually bites. A recipe written in Australia that calls for a tablespoon of sugar means a third more than a US cook would measure, and scaled across a batch of eight spoons that is 35 grams of missing sugar. It rarely ruins anything, but it explains why an Australian recipe can taste noticeably sweeter when made elsewhere.

The Difference Between a Spoonful and a Pinch

Sugar measurements at this scale are unusual in that they are almost never structural. A tablespoon of sugar does not hold a cake together or set a custard; it sweetens, feeds or stabilises. That means the tolerance is wide, and a gram or two either way is invisible.

Which is the opposite of how sugar behaves in bulk. At 200 g a systematic error in the sugar weight shifts the ratio against flour and changes the crumb. At 12.5 g the same percentage error is 1.5 g and changes nothing. The quantity at which sugar starts to matter structurally is somewhere around the half-cup mark — which is exactly where recipes stop giving it in spoons and start giving it in cups.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 tablespoon of sugar?

A1: 12.5 grams of granulated sugar, level. A cup holds 200 grams and 16 tablespoons, so each tablespoon is one sixteenth of that.

Q2: How many teaspoons is a tablespoon of sugar?

A2: Three teaspoons, so a teaspoon of sugar is about 4.2 grams. That makes a tablespoon roughly 12.5 grams and a teaspoon roughly a quarter of that.

Q3: Is a tablespoon of sugar a lot?

A3: It is about 50 calories and three times a teaspoon, the spoon you would stir into a cup of tea. In baking terms it is a small quantity, added for finishing or for what it does to a mixture rather than for bulk sweetness.
