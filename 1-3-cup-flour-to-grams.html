How Many Grams in 1/3 Cup of Flour?

One-third cup is the unloved middle child of baking measurements. Most dry measuring cup sets skip it entirely — you get 1/4, 1/3 (sometimes, in liquid sets), 1/2, and 1 cup. The 1/3 line on a liquid measuring cup is calibrated for water, not flour, so using it for dry ingredients adds another layer of imprecision to an already fussy measurement. ⅓ cup of all-purpose flour = 40 grams using the spoon-and-level method. Bread flour comes in at ~43 g, cake flour at ~37 g. That 6-gram range across flour types is proportionally huge — 15% of the total weight — and your measuring technique can eat up another 5-8 grams on top of that.

The Conversion Formula (and Why 0.333 Isn't 1/3)

One-third as a decimal is 0.333 repeating — no calculator will ever give you a perfectly clean number. But for baking purposes, 0.333 × 120 = 39.96, which anyone with a kitchen scale calls 40 grams:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • All-Purpose Flour: 0.333 × 120 g = 40 grams
  • Bread Flour: 0.333 × 130 g = 43 grams
  • Cake Flour: 0.333 × 110 g = 37 grams

The Scoop-vs-Spoon Problem Gets Worse at 1/3 Cup

At a full cup, the dip-and-sweep vs spoon-and-level debate might swing your flour weight by 15-20 grams — annoying, but manageable in a recipe with 3-4 cups of flour total. At half a cup, that error is proportionally doubled. At a third of a cup, it's tripled. If you jam your measuring cup into the flour bag, scoop, and level — congratulations, you've probably packed in 45-48 grams when the recipe expects 40. That's a 20% over-measurement. In a roux, where ⅓ cup of flour is the entire thickener for a small batch of gravy, 20% extra flour means paste instead of sauce. In a small-batch cookie recipe with only ⅓ cup of flour total, it means dense, tough cookies. The fix is boring but effective: spoon flour into your measuring cup, don't scoop, and level with the flat back of a knife. Or better yet, use a scale — 40 grams is 40 grams regardless of how you filled the cup. See our flour measuring guide for the full breakdown with side-by-side weight comparisons.

Where 1/3 Cup Flour Actually Shows Up

Recipes don't call for ⅓ cup of flour as often as they do halves and quarters, but when they do, it tends to be in places where precision actually matters more: thickening a small roux for two servings of mac and cheese, dusting a work surface for a single pie crust, or the flour component in a small-batch cookie recipe meant to yield six cookies instead of three dozen. It also shows up in gluten-free flour blends, where the total flour weight is split among three or four different flours (almond, rice, tapioca, sorghum) and each gets a third-cup portion. In those blends, a 5-gram error per flour times four flours is a 20-gram total error — enough to throw off the starch-to-protein ratio that gluten-free baking depends on.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/3 cup of all-purpose flour?

A1: 40 grams for all-purpose flour measured by spoon-and-level. Bread flour is ~43 g, cake flour ~37 g. Scooping directly from the bag can pack in 5-8 extra grams — at this small volume, that error is proportionally huge.

Q2: My measuring cup set doesn't have a 1/3 cup line. What do I do?

A2: Use a kitchen scale and weigh 40 g directly — it's actually more accurate than any measuring cup. If you must use cups, spoon flour into a 1-cup dry measure until it reads roughly one-third full by eye, then level with a straight edge. But honestly, a $15 digital scale solves this problem permanently and makes every recipe more consistent.

Q3: Why does the flour type change the weight for the same 1/3 cup?

A3: Different flours pack differently based on protein content and grind fineness. Bread flour is higher-protein and denser — about 43 g per ⅓ cup. Cake flour is finely milled, low-protein, and much fluffier — about 37 g. All-purpose splits the difference at 40 g. If you swap bread flour for cake flour by volume without adjusting, you've changed the flour weight by roughly 15%.
