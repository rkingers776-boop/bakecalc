10 oz of oil in mL — the short answer

Ten US fluid ounces of oil is 295.74 mL — 10 × 29.5735, exactly the same volume as ten fluid ounces of anything else. Its weight is the part that surprises people: about 272 grams, because cooking oil is roughly 8% less dense than water.

Under the imperial fluid ounce the volume is 284.13 mL. The density figure varies a little by oil — olive oil runs about 0.918 g/mL, canola and vegetable oil about 0.92, coconut oil fractionated for liquid use around 0.92 as well — but they are all close enough to treat as one number.

Oil Is the One Common Liquid Lighter Than Water

Milk is denser than water, cream sits almost exactly at water density, honey is 40% heavier. Oil goes the other way, and it is the only everyday kitchen liquid that does. That is not a subtle property; it is why oil floats, why a vinaigrette separates, and why the numbers on this page run in the opposite direction from the rest of the site.

  • 10 fl oz of water — 295.74 mL, weighing 295.74 g
  • 10 fl oz of milk — 295.74 mL, weighing about 305 g
  • 10 fl oz of oil — 295.74 mL, weighing about 272 g
  • 10 fl oz of honey — 295.74 mL, weighing about 420 g

One volume, four weights spanning more than 150 grams. Any conversion table that gives a single gram figure for a fluid ounce is describing water and nothing else.

Where the Difference Shows Up in Baking

A recipe that gives oil in cups and you weigh in grams is where the 8% becomes a real number. One cup of oil is 236.59 mL and weighs about 218 g, not the 237 g a cup of water would weigh. Use 237 g and you have added nearly 9% more fat than intended, which in a cake shows up as a greasy crumb and a collapsed centre.

The reverse substitution is worse. Replacing oil with butter gram for gram is a common mistake: butter is about 80% fat and 20% water, so 100 g of butter carries only 80 g of fat. To match the fat in 218 g of oil you need roughly 270 g of butter, and that butter also brings water and milk solids that change the outcome again.

Where 10 oz of Oil Shows Up

  • Bottles — 10 fl oz is a common small bottle of speciality oil such as sesame or truffle.
  • Frying — smaller quantities for shallow frying or a single batch of doughnuts.
  • Dressings — a vinaigrette for a large salad lands near this volume.
  • Cake recipes — oil-based cakes often specify 1¼ cups, which is the same 10 fl oz stated a different way.

Why Oil Cakes Use Oil at All

Oil is liquid at room temperature, which means it coats flour proteins immediately and blocks gluten development before it starts. The result is a cake that stays tender for days, where a butter cake firms up as the butter resolidifies. That is the whole trade: oil cakes are softer and keep longer, butter cakes taste better and hold their shape.

Because oil interferes with gluten, it cannot be swapped into a bread formula the way a cook might expect. Bread needs gluten, and the fat in an enriched bread is kept low for exactly this reason. The same property that makes oil excellent in a muffin makes it unwelcome in a baguette.

Measuring Oil Without the Mess

Oil clings to measuring cups, which is why the volume in the jug is rarely quite the volume in the bowl. Two habits fix it. Weigh the oil directly into the mixing bowl instead of measuring it separately, which removes both the cling and the washing up. Or, if you are using a spoon, coat it with a little of the oil first so the rest slides off cleanly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many mL is 10 oz of oil?

A1: 295.74 mL for 10 US fluid ounces, the same volume as any other liquid. It weighs only about 272 grams, though, because cooking oil is roughly 8% less dense than water.

Q2: How many cups is 10 oz of oil?

A2: One and a quarter cups, or 20 tablespoons. A US cup is 8 fluid ounces, so 10 fluid ounces is 1.25 cups regardless of which liquid is in the jug.

Q3: Does oil weigh less than water?

A3: Yes, by about 8%. Oil floats on water, which is the everyday proof. In practice that means one cup of oil, 236.59 mL, weighs about 218 grams rather than the 237 grams a cup of water would weigh — a difference worth knowing when a recipe gives oil in grams.
