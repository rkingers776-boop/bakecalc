Why Use a Baking Cost Calculator?

If you sell baked goods — whether at a farmers market, through a cottage food operation, or via online orders — knowing your exact costs is the single most important step toward profitability. Many home bakers underprice their products because they only count ingredient costs, forgetting labor, packaging, overhead (electricity, water, equipment wear), and profit margin.

Our calculator walks you through every cost component, gives you a suggested retail price, and even generates a printable recipe card you can share with customers or keep for your records.

How to Use This Tool

  1. Name your recipe and set the number of servings.
  2. Add each ingredient — choose from our preset list or type your own. Enter the quantity and the price you paid per unit (e.g., if flour costs $2/kg, the unit price is $0.002/g).
  3. Set your labor rate — how much you want to pay yourself per hour, and how long the recipe takes.
  4. Add packaging costs — boxes, bags, labels, stickers.
  5. Set your overhead and profit margin — typical overhead is 5–15%; typical profit margin is 20–50%.
  6. Read your results and print a clean recipe card.

Pricing Tips for Cottage Food Bakers

A common rule of thumb: your retail price should be roughly 3× ingredient cost to cover labor, overhead, and profit. But every product is different — high-labor items like decorated cookies may need a 4× multiplier, while simple bread loaves may work at 2.5×. Use this tool to find the number that works for your specific recipe.

How Many Servings Does Your Cake Actually Yield?

A cake does not have one fixed number of servings. It has a number of servings per cut, and the same 8-inch round gives 20 pieces or 24 depending on which cut you use. That single detail causes more disagreements between home bakers and their customers than any other, and it is why two bakers can quote the same cake at very different prices per serving.

The generous party cut — roughly 1½ × 2 inches — is what you would plate at a birthday, a slice big enough to be dessert on its own. The thinner wedding cut, about 1 × 2 inches, is the formal portion handed out to a long guest list or served alongside other desserts. Neither is wrong. They simply feed different numbers of people from the same pan.

Two things surprise people who have not costed a cake before. First, layers do not change the count. A two-layer cake is twice as tall, not twice as wide, and you still cut one wedge through the whole stack. Servings come from the area of the pan's top surface, not from its height — a 4-inch-tall 10-inch round feeds exactly as many people as a 6-inch-tall one cut the same way. Only when a cake drops below 3 inches tall does the serving count fall, and then it roughly halves, because the slices are too short to plate. Second, square pans beat round pans at every size, because there is no curved edge left behind to waste. A 10-inch square yields 30 party servings against a 10-inch round's 28, and the gap widens as pans get larger.

If you price by the serving, settle the cut before you quote and write it on the order. Our pricing guide walks through that calculation with worked examples.
