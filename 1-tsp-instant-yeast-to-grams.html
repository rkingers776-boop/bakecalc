1 teaspoon of instant yeast in grams — the short answer

One teaspoon of instant yeast is 3.1 grams. A standard sachet is 7 g, which is 2 1/4 teaspoons, and that is where the familiar recipe quantity comes from.

Three grams is the smallest weight in the baking pantry. It is also the only one that is alive.

Why the Packet Beats the Spoon

Yeast is sold in pre-measured 7 g sachets, and the temptation is to treat them as a convenience rather than as the more accurate option. They are the more accurate option.

The arithmetic makes the case. A teaspoon is 3.1 g, so a 7 g sachet is 2.25 teaspoons — an awkward fractional number to measure. And 3.1 g is below the comfortable resolution of most kitchen scales, which read to the nearest gram. On a scale that says "3" when the true value is 3.1, the yeast could equally be 2.6 or 3.5, which is a spread of a third of the dose.

  • One sachet — 7 g, no measurement at all, sized to a standard loaf
  • One teaspoon — 3.1 g, half a sachet, and the practical unit for smaller batches
  • 2 1/4 teaspoons — 7 g, exactly one sachet

If you buy yeast in a jar or a block rather than in sachets, a scale that reads to 0.1 g is the one piece of equipment worth having. Otherwise, measure by teaspoon and accept that the spoon is more consistent than a coarse scale.

Instant, Active Dry, and Fresh Cake Yeast

Three products, three different weights for the same leavening power.

  • Instant yeast — 3.1 g per teaspoon. Milled finer and dried more gently than active dry, so it hydrates on contact and can be mixed straight into the dry ingredients. Also sold as rapid-rise or bread machine yeast.
  • Active dry yeast — also about 3.1 g per teaspoon, but the granules are larger and coated with dead cells, so it dissolves more slowly and is roughly 25% weaker by volume. Use 1 1/4 teaspoons where a recipe calls for one of instant, and proof it in warm water first.
  • Fresh cake yeast — 9.3 g per teaspoon, about three times the weight. It is a live, pressed cake with roughly 70% moisture, it is highly perishable, and it is the standard in professional bakeries for its clean flavour. To convert, divide the fresh weight by three.

The practical direction of the swap: instant to fresh multiplies by three; fresh to instant divides by three; active dry to instant divides the volume by 1.25.

Where a Fraction of a Gram Actually Matters

Yeast is forgiving in the middle of its range and unforgiving at the edges, and it is worth being clear about which is which.

Within reasonable limits, changing the yeast quantity changes the speed of the proof rather than the final result. A dough with 20% more yeast will be ready sooner; a dough with 20% less will take longer. Both will produce good bread if you watch the dough rather than the clock, which is the single most useful habit in bread baking.

Where it stops being forgiving:

  • Too little — the dough stalls, and in a cool kitchen it may simply not rise in the time you have.
  • Far too much — the dough proofs before you are ready for it, and past a point the excess yeast contributes a distinctly yeasty, almost solvent-like flavour, because fermentation outpaces the flavour development.
  • Scaled down batches — halving a recipe means 1.55 g, which almost no kitchen scale resolves. Measure half a teaspoon and accept the approximation, or halve the packet by eye.

Temperature and Time Are the Real Levers

A tenth of a gram of yeast is a small lever. Temperature is a large one.

Yeast metabolism follows the usual rule for biological rates: in the range from about 4 °C to 35 °C, the rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C of warmth. A dough at 24 °C might double in about 90 minutes; the same dough at 27 °C takes closer to an hour.

Which is why the same recipe behaves differently in a hot kitchen and a cold one, and why professional bakers talk about dough temperature rather than room temperature. It is also why a slow, cold proof is so effective. A dough retarded overnight in the refrigerator ferments slowly enough to build flavour compounds that a fast warm proof never gets time to develop — which is why one teaspoon in a retarded dough can taste richer than two teaspoons proofed in an hour.

The upper limit is real. Above roughly 50 °C yeast dies, which is why the water for proofing should feel comfortably warm rather than hot. If you can hold your finger in it without flinching, it is about right.

Salt, Sugar, and Fat

  • Salt — slows fermentation by drawing water out of the yeast cells. It is also what makes bread taste like bread, so it cannot be left out. Keep the two apart until mixing so the salt does not make direct contact with concentrated yeast.
  • Sugar — feeds the yeast at low levels and inhibits it at high ones, through the osmotic pressure of a concentrated solution. Above roughly 10% of the flour weight, enriched doughs need more yeast or a longer proof. That is what osmotolerant yeasts such as SAF Gold are formulated for.
  • Fat — coats the flour proteins and the yeast cells, slowing both gluten development and fermentation. An enriched dough rises more slowly than a lean one and needs more patience rather than more yeast.

Testing and Storing Yeast

Yeast is a living organism and it dies quietly. The test takes ten minutes: stir 1 teaspoon into 1/4 cup of warm water with a pinch of sugar and wait. Foam and a beery smell means it is alive; nothing means buy more.

Store it sealed and dry. Humidity is what kills it — a jar kept in a steamy kitchen loses potency over months, and an opened sachet on the counter is worse. The refrigerator is fine for sachets, and the freezer is better for a large block, as long as it is wrapped so it does not pick up moisture or odours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 teaspoon of instant yeast?

A1: 3.1 grams. A standard 7 gram sachet therefore holds two and a quarter teaspoons, which is why so many recipes are written around that amount.

Q2: How much active dry yeast replaces 1 teaspoon of instant?

A2: About 1 1/4 teaspoons. Instant yeast is the more concentrated of the two by volume, and active dry is roughly 25% weaker, so the volume has to go up to deliver the same leavening.

Q3: How does fresh yeast convert to instant yeast?

A3: Fresh cake yeast weighs about 9.3 grams per teaspoon, roughly three times what instant yeast weighs for the same leavening. Divide a fresh yeast weight by three to get the instant equivalent.
