1 teaspoon of baking powder in grams — the short answer

One teaspoon of baking powder is 4.6 grams. It is the standard figure, and it is the same for baking soda — the two are close enough in bulk density that a teaspoon of each comes to 4.6 g.

Four and a half grams is a tiny quantity, and this is the ingredient where that tiny quantity matters most.

The Smallest Weight With the Largest Consequence

Consider what a gram means here. Most kitchen scales resolve to 1 g, so 4.6 g reads as either 4 or 5 — an error of up to 11% before anything has gone wrong. And unlike a gram of flour in a 500 g batch, a gram of baking powder is a substantial share of the total.

The comparison that makes the point:

  • 1 g out of 500 g of flour — 0.2% of the batch
  • 1 g out of 4.6 g of baking powder — 22% of the leavening

Leavening does not scale linearly with the size of a cake, either. The gas a given weight of baking powder produces is roughly proportional to the powder, but the batter it has to lift scales with the pan and the flour. So a 20% shortfall in leavening is not a 20% shorter cake — it can be a cake that fails to crown at all, or one that crowns and then collapses on cooling.

This is the one ingredient in the pantry where weighing to a tenth of a gram, or measuring by level teaspoon and trusting the spoon, genuinely beats a coarse scale.

What Is Actually Inside a Teaspoon

Baking powder is a mixture, not a compound, and the proportions explain both its behaviour and its shelf life.

  • Sodium bicarbonate — the active ingredient, roughly 28 to 30% by weight. In a teaspoon that is about 1.4 g.
  • Acid salts — one or two of them, making up around 35 to 40%. These are what the bicarbonate reacts with, and which ones are used determines when the reaction happens.
  • Cornstarch — around 25 to 30%, present to absorb moisture and keep the two reactive components from meeting prematurely in the tin.

So of the 4.6 g in the spoon, only about 1.4 g is doing the leavening. The rest is the acid that activates it and the starch that protects it.

Double-Acting Means Two Rises From One Spoon

The acid salts chosen determine when the gas is released, and the common formulations release it twice.

  • First action, in the bowl — a fast-acting acid dissolves as soon as the powder gets wet and reacts at room temperature. This is why a batter visibly begins to bubble while you are still folding it.
  • Second action, in the oven — a slower acid stays inert until the batter passes roughly 60 °C, at which point it reacts and delivers a second lift during the bake, when the structure is setting and the gas is most useful.

Single-acting powders release everything at once. They are common in the United Kingdom and parts of Europe, and they require a batter to go into the oven promptly, because the gas that has already escaped cannot be recovered.

The practical instruction for both types is the same: mix the batter and bake it. Double-acting powder buys you some minutes of slack, not an hour.

Replacing It, and Being Replaced

Baking powder and baking soda are not interchangeable, but there is a workable conversion.

  • Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate and needs an external acid — buttermilk, yoghurt, lemon, vinegar, molasses, natural cocoa — to react with.
  • Baking powder is bicarbonate that already carries its acid, so it needs nothing from the recipe.
  • To replace baking powder with soda — use a quarter of the volume of baking soda plus half the volume of cream of tartar. One teaspoon of powder becomes 1/4 tsp of soda and 1/2 tsp of cream of tartar.
  • To replace baking soda with powder — you need roughly three to four times the volume, and the result will be less alkaline, which means less browning and a paler crust.

As a starting point for a new recipe, the usual guidance is about 1 to 1 1/4 teaspoons of baking powder per cup of flour. Much more than that and the cake tastes soapy and rises too fast, collapsing in the middle.

Testing Whether Yours Still Works

Baking powder is hygroscopic — it pulls moisture out of the air — and once it has absorbed enough, part of its first-action acid has already reacted in the tin. The powder is not spoiled, but it is weaker, and you have no way of knowing by how much.

The test takes a minute. Stir 1 teaspoon of baking powder into 1/4 cup of hot water. Fresh powder fizzes vigorously and continuously for several seconds. A few lazy bubbles, or none, means replace it.

Store it sealed and dry, away from the kettle and the dishwasher. An opened tin keeps well for six to twelve months; a tin that has been sitting open above the stove is a different matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

A1: 4.6 grams. That is the standard figure used on nutrition labels and it is the one this converter applies. Baking soda comes in at the same 4.6 grams per teaspoon.

Q2: How much baking soda is in a teaspoon of baking powder?

A2: About 1.4 grams. Baking powder is roughly 30% sodium bicarbonate by weight, with the rest made up of acid salts and a little cornstarch.

Q3: How do I replace baking powder with baking soda?
