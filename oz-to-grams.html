Ounces to Grams — No More Guessing at the Scale

Convert weight ounces to grams. Not fluid ounces — those are completely different. (Yes, it's confusing. I'll explain.)

Weight in Grams:

28 g

1 oz = 28.35 grams. Rounded to nearest gram for kitchen use.

I Ruined a British Christmas Cake Because of This Conversion

December 2019. My wife's colleague had emailed her a family Christmas cake recipe from Yorkshire — all in grams. My wife handed me a bag of dried fruit and said "we need 12 ounces of this." I looked at the recipe: 340g currants, 225g raisins, 110g mixed peel. I had no idea what 12 ounces was in grams. I guessed. I guessed wrong. The cake was dense enough to use as a doorstop, and I spent the next two hours learning everything about ounce-to-gram conversion out of pure spite.

The number you need: 1 ounce = 28.35 grams. That's it. That's the whole conversion. But knowing the number isn't the same as knowing when to use it — and more importantly, when not to.

Weight Ounces vs. Fluid Ounces — The Thing Nobody Explains

Here's what tripped me up for years: the word "ounce" means two completely different things depending on context.

Weight ounce (oz) — how heavy something is. 16 weight ounces = 1 pound. Used for flour, butter, meat, cheese, chocolate, produce. This page converts weight ounces.

Fluid ounce (fl oz) — how much space a liquid takes up. 8 fluid ounces = 1 cup. Used for water, milk, oil, vanilla extract. Our fluid ounce to mL converter handles that.

The confusion gets worse: 8 fluid ounces of water happens to weigh about 8.3 weight ounces (236g ÷ 28.35 = 8.3). So for water, the numbers are almost the same. For flour? 8 fluid ounces (1 cup) of flour weighs about 4.2 weight ounces (120g). The numbers aren't even close. Every American who's tried to bake from a British cookbook has learned this the hard way.

Rule of thumb: if the recipe just says "oz" without "fl" before it, it's probably weight. If it's an American recipe from before 1980, it might be fluid ounces for liquids only. European recipes don't use ounces at all. When in doubt: weigh it.

28 grams, 28.35 grams, or 30 grams?

Different contexts use different rounding:

  • Baking: use 28.35g/oz exactly, or 28g for quick mental math. The difference is 1.25% — negligible for a single ounce, but it adds up: at 16 oz (1 pound), 28g/oz gives you 448g when the real answer is 454g. That 6-gram difference matters in bread and delicate pastry.
  • Nutrition labels (US): the FDA rounds to 28g/oz on the standard conversion. A "4 oz (112g)" serving of chicken on a label is the FDA doing its thing.
  • Cannabis / precious metals / postage: these use troy ounces (31.1g) — a completely different ounce from the kitchen one. If a recipe says "oz," it never means troy. That's a separate rabbit hole.
  • Restaurant portions: often round to 30g/oz for easy math. A "6 oz burger" is roughly 170-180g raw weight.

Why I Weigh Everything Now

After that Christmas cake incident, I bought a $12 digital scale. It's the single best kitchen purchase I've made. Here's what changed:

  • No more washing measuring cups between dry and wet ingredients.
  • Recipes come out the same every time — 120g of flour is 120g of flour. A cup of flour can be anything from 110g to 150g depending on how you scoop.
  • I can use any recipe from any country. British, French, Japanese — grams are grams everywhere.
  • Fewer bowls. Put the mixing bowl on the scale, tare between ingredients, pour directly. One bowl for most batters and doughs.

If you're on the fence about buying a kitchen scale: get one. Even the cheapest one that reads to 1 gram will improve your baking more than any other gadget under $50.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many grams in 1 ounce?

Exactly 28.3495 grams. Most kitchens round to 28g for quick math or 28.35g for precision. Use 28.35g/oz when converting ingredient weights for baking.

Is 1 oz the same as 28g or 30g?

28g is a slight undercount (1.2% low). 30g is a slight overcount (5.8% high). Neither will ruin a stew, but in baking — especially bread — use 28g for small amounts (1-2 oz) and 28.35g for larger amounts (4+ oz) to keep the error from compounding.

Why can't I use a liquid measuring cup for ounces of flour?

Because "fluid ounces" (volume) and "weight ounces" (mass) are different units that share a name. One cup = 8 fluid ounces. One cup of flour = about 4.2 weight ounces. If a recipe says "8 oz flour," it means weigh out 227g on a scale — not fill a 1-cup liquid measure. American recipes are especially bad at distinguishing these; assume "oz" means weight for dry ingredients and fluid ounces only for liquids explicitly labeled "fl oz."
