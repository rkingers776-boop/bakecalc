500 g of butter in cups — the short answer

Five hundred grams of butter is 2.2 cups — two full cups and a fifth more — which is 35 level tablespoons or four and a half US sticks.

Half a kilogram is a commercial quantity rather than a home one, and at this size the conversion stops being the main question. Nobody buys 500 g of butter to use in one cake; they buy it because that is how it is packaged, and the useful knowledge becomes how to store it and what to do with what is left.

Storing Half a Kilo

Butter is mostly fat, and fat oxidises. The enemies are air, light and warmth, in that order. Handled well, half a kilo will last long enough to be used rather than thrown away.

  • Refrigerator, sealed — one to three months for salted, about a month for unsalted. Salt is a mild preservative, which is the historical reason salted butter exists at all.
  • Freezer, wrapped tightly — six to nine months with no meaningful loss of quality. Butter freezes very well because it is almost entirely fat.
  • Counter, in a butter dish — fine for a few days for spreadable butter, but not for a block this size.

The practical approach with 500 g is to divide it before storing rather than after. Cut the block into the quantities your recipes actually use — 113 g for a stick equivalent, or 250 g for a standard half block — wrap each portion separately, and freeze what will not be used within a month. Rewrapping once, at the start, is easier than chipping at a frozen block later.

What Clarifying Does to 500 g

Clarified butter is butter with the water and milk solids removed. Since American butter is about 80% fat, 17 to 18% water and a couple of percent milk solids, clarifying half a kilo yields roughly 375 to 400 g of pure butterfat. The missing 100 to 125 g is not lost to error — it is the water evaporating and the solids being skimmed off.

What you gain is heat tolerance. Whole butter burns at around 150 °C because the milk solids scorch; clarified butterfat holds to roughly 250 °C. That is the entire reason ghee and clarified butter are used for frying and for high-heat Indian cooking, and it is why the yield is worth knowing before you start — 500 g going in and 380 g coming out is not a mistake.

Where 500 g of Butter Shows Up

  • A standard metric block pack — sold as two 250 g blocks in most of the world, and as four US sticks plus a little in American one-pound packs.
  • Bulk baking — a very large batch of cookies, or a celebration cake with frosting taken together.
  • Clarifying — the quantity people buy when they intend to make ghee.
  • Laminated pastry, large batch — several batches of croissant dough over a weekend.

The One Conversion Worth Memorising

Of all the figures on this page, the one with the widest use is that one US stick is 113.4 g, half a cup and 8 tablespoons. Every other butter quantity on this site is built from it, and once it is fixed in memory the wrapper markings do the rest — no cups, no scale, and no arithmetic beyond counting to eight.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 500 g of butter?

A1: About 2.2 cups — two full cups plus a fifth of a cup. In tablespoons that is 35, and in US sticks it is four and a half.

Q2: How long does butter keep?

A2: Salted butter keeps about one to three months in the fridge and six to nine months frozen. Unsalted butter has a shorter fridge life, around one month, because salt is a mild preservative. Both are fine well beyond that if they have been kept sealed and cold, though the flavour fades.

Q3: How much clarified butter does 500 g of butter make?

A3: Roughly 375 to 400 grams. Clarifying removes the water, which is about 17 to 18% of the weight, along with the milk solids. What remains is pure butterfat with a much higher smoke point, which is why it is used for frying.
