How Many Grams in 1 Cup of Flour?

If you've hit an American recipe calling for "1 cup of flour" while your scale only speaks grams, the short answer is 120 grams. That's the figure most professional recipe developers build their formulas around, and it's what this converter assumes. The honest long answer is that a cup of flour has no fixed weight at all — it has whatever weight your hand gave it between the bag and the measuring cup. Spoon the flour in gently and level it off, and you get 120 g. Plunge the cup straight into the bag and sweep it level, and the same cup holds 145 g or more. That is a twenty-percent swing in the single most structurally important ingredient in your bake, decided by technique rather than by the recipe.

So the useful question isn't "how many grams is a cup of flour." It's "how many grams is a cup of flour the way I filled it" — and then filling it that way every single time. The rest of this page covers where 120 comes from, how far each filling method moves it, what every common flour actually weighs, and when a five-gram difference is enough to ruin a cake.

The Conversion Formula (Cups → Grams)

The math is one multiplication — the number of cups times that flour's grams-per-cup density:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • All-Purpose Flour: 1 × 120 g = 120 grams
  • Bread Flour: 1 × 130 g = 130 grams
  • Cake Flour: 1 × 110 g = 110 grams
  • Whole Wheat Flour: 1 × 120 g = 120 grams
  • Rye Flour: 1 × 106 g = 106 grams

The density figure is the only part you have to look up, and it moves with the grain, the grind, and how much bran or germ the miller left in. A cup of rye is noticeably lighter than a cup of all-purpose, because rye kernels mill into fine slippery particles that resist packing. Almond flour, with no gluten and a high oil content, is lighter still at around 96 g. Cornstarch and cocoa powder run the other direction — both are milled so fine and settle so tightly that a cup of either weighs more than a cup of flour.

How You Fill the Cup Changes Everything

Flour compacts. That one property is why American cup recipes and gram weights argue with each other so often. There are three ways people fill a dry measuring cup, and each produces a different weight from the same bag:

  • Dip and sweep — the cup goes straight into the bag, takes a full scoop, then gets leveled with the back of a knife. The cup's own walls press the flour against itself as it fills. This is how most people measure by default, and it produces the heaviest cup of the three.
  • Spoon and level — flour is spooned lightly into the cup until it mounds above the rim, then leveled with a straight edge. Almost no compaction happens. This is the 120-gram standard.
  • Sift, then spoon and level — flour is sifted first to break up clumps and work in air, then spooned and leveled. Maximum aeration, and the lightest cup of the three.

Between the heaviest filling method and the lightest there is roughly a third of a cup of flour in play. Drop that into a pound cake and you have moved the liquid-to-flour ratio by more than a quarter — which is exactly how a cake that came out beautifully last month comes out dry and tight this month from the same printed recipe. Recipe developers assume spoon-and-level when they publish gram weights, and that assumption is baked into every number on this page.

A Cup of Every Common Flour, Weighed

Because flours get substituted for one another so casually, here is the per-cup weight for the ones most likely to be in your cupboard. Find the right row before you convert, or the formula quietly hands you the wrong answer:

  • Bread flour — 130 g
  • Cake flour — 110 g
  • Pastry flour — 106 g
  • Whole wheat flour — 120 g
  • Rye flour, medium — 106 g
  • Self-rising flour — 113 g
  • Oat flour — 92 g
  • Almond flour — 96 g
  • Coconut flour — 128 g
  • Cornstarch — 128 g
  • Cocoa powder — 100 g

One warning that catches people out: whole-grain flours absorb more water than white flours at the same weight. It isn't a density problem — bran and germ particles are simply thirsty. Swap whole wheat for all-purpose gram for gram and expect to add liquid, not to get an identical dough.

Why Charts Disagree About the Same Cup

Search the conversion and you'll find 120, 125, and 130 all presented as settled fact. Each is defensible, because each was arrived at differently. Some charts average many measurements of one brand's flour — King Arthur's own chart puts all-purpose at 120 g. Others come from government nutrient databases, which list a generic enriched all-purpose flour nearer 125 g, a sample that includes denser commodity flours. Still others work from bulk density measured in a lab rather than from a real cup in a real kitchen.

None of them describes your bag. Flour weight drifts with wheat variety, growing year, milling fineness, and how long the bag sat in a humid pantry. Which is why the number matters less than the consistency: pick one figure, write it into your own recipes, and use it every time. Trouble only starts when a recipe's internal ratios assume one density and your measuring cup delivers another.

When a 25-Gram Error Actually Matters

Not every recipe cares. In a batch of drop cookies, a 25-gram flour error is a textural nuisance — slightly cakier, slightly chewier, gone by the second day. As flour's share of a recipe rises and the liquid's share falls, though, the same error stops being a nuisance and becomes structural:

  • Bread — hydration is expressed as a percentage of flour weight. Get the flour twenty percent heavy and a 75% hydration dough is suddenly sitting at 62%. The loaf bakes tight and dense, short on oven spring.
  • Butter cakes — these depend on a precise fat-to-flour-to-liquid balance. Extra flour pulls moisture out of the crumb, giving you a dry cake with a domed top and tunnels around the edge.
  • Pastry and pie dough — flakiness is set by the flour-to-butter ratio. More flour means less butter per unit of dough, and the distinct layers never form.
  • Small batches — a single-layer cake or a half batch magnifies everything, because the absolute amount of every other ingredient is small.

This is the entire case for a scale. It doesn't care how humid the kitchen is, whether the flour settled in the bag, or how firmly you pressed. For flours that behave differently from the 120 g baseline, see bread flour, cake flour, whole wheat, or almond flour. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of all-purpose flour?

A1: 120 grams when you spoon the flour into the cup and level it off — the method most recipe developers use when they test. Scoop the cup straight into the bag instead and the same cup can hold 145 grams or more, because the walls of the cup compress the flour against itself. That 25-gram spread is the largest single source of measurement error in American home baking.

Q2: Does the type of flour change the 1-cup-to-grams conversion?

A2: Yes. At one cup, all-purpose and whole wheat both land near 120 grams, bread flour runs heavier at about 130, cake flour lighter at about 110, rye around 106, and almond flour as light as 96. Cornstarch and cocoa powder go the other way — finely milled and highly packable, at roughly 128 and 100 grams per cup.

Q3: Why do professional bakers prefer grams over cups for flour?

A3: Because flour is compressible and a cup measures volume, not mass. A dip-and-sweep cup can hold 150 grams while a spoon-and-level cup holds 120 — the same cup, the same flour, a 25% difference. A scale removes the variable entirely: 120 grams is 120 grams whether the kitchen is humid or the bag has settled.
