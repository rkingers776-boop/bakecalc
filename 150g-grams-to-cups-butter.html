150 g of butter in cups — the short answer

One hundred and fifty grams of butter is 0.66 cups — exactly two thirds — which is 10 and a half tablespoons, or one US stick plus a third of another.

The figure is awkward in runs of whole sticks but clean in thirds, and that points at the most practical way to measure it: forget sticks and think in tablespoons, because the wrapper is already divided into them.

Cutting 150 g From a Standard Pack

A US stick is 8 tablespoons, and the wrapper carries a mark for each. Getting to 10.5 tablespoons takes two cuts:

  • First stick — cut whole. That is 8 tablespoons, or 113.4 g.
  • Second stick — cut at the 2.5 tablespoon mark. That is the remaining 36.6 g.
  • Combined — 10.5 tablespoons, or 150 g

The half tablespoon at the end is the fiddly part. It is 7 g of butter, and in all but the most exacting recipes it can be ignored — cut at the third mark instead of the half mark and you have 154 g rather than 150. The 2.7% difference will not be visible in anything.

Where 150 g of Butter Shows Up

  • Layer cakes — one nine-inch round takes roughly 115 to 150 g of butter in a standard butter cake.
  • Frosting for a single layer — enough buttercream to fill and cover a small cake.
  • Shortcrust pastry — a double-crust 9-inch pie, at the half-fat-to-flour ratio.
  • Enriched bread — a brioche or babka for one small loaf, where the butter is beaten in after the dough has developed.

Why Butter Comes in Sticks at All

The American stick exists to make this kind of measurement unnecessary. A quarter-pound stick is a fixed 4 ounces, and because 4 ounces of butter measures half a cup, the wrapper can be printed with eight tablespoon divisions and the cook never needs a scale or a cup.

It is a packaging solution to a measurement problem, and it is unusually effective. The equivalent problem in Europe is solved differently: butter comes in 250 g blocks and recipes are written in grams, so nobody needs to convert at all.

The friction only appears when a recipe crosses systems, which is exactly what happens to anyone converting 150 g into spoons. The number is clean in grams and awkward in tablespoons, and there is no way around that except to use the grams.

Solid, Softened and Melted Are Three Different Things

Butter's weight does not change with temperature, but its volume does, and its behaviour in a batter changes far more. A recipe that says 150 g of butter assumes one of three states, and using the wrong one is a common cause of cakes that fail without an obvious reason.

  • Cold and firm — for cutting into flour. Makes flaky pastry because the fat stays in distinct pieces.
  • Softened, around 18 to 21 °C — for creaming with sugar. Yields to the beater without collapsing, and holds the air it traps.
  • Melted and cooled — for batters where the butter is a liquid ingredient. Incorporates evenly but provides no aeration, so those recipes rely entirely on chemical leaveners.

All three weigh 150 g. Only one of them is right for any given recipe, and the instruction is usually a single word — softened, cold, or melted — carrying a great deal of the outcome.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 150 g of butter?

A1: About 0.66 cups, or two thirds of a cup. In tablespoons that is 10 and a half, and in US stick terms it is one stick plus a third of a second.

Q2: How do I measure 150 g of butter without a scale?

A2: Cut one full stick, which is 8 tablespoons, then cut a second stick two thirds of the way along. Read the wrapper markings rather than estimating — a stick is printed with eight tablespoons, so 10.5 in total is straightforward to locate.

Q3: Is 150 g of butter the same as two thirds of a cup?

A3: Yes, 150 grams of butter is 0.66 cups, which is exactly two thirds. Since a cup of butter is 227 grams, one third is 75.7 grams and two thirds is 151.3 — close enough that 150 grams and two thirds of a cup are used interchangeably.
