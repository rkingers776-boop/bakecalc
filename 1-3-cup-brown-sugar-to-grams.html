1/3 Cup Brown Sugar to Grams Converter

Convert ⅓ US cup of brown sugar into grams — the only dry ingredient where "pack it down" isn't bad advice, it's the entire point of the measurement.

How Many Grams in 1/3 Cup of Brown Sugar?

One-third cup of packed brown sugar weighs 73 grams. This is true for both light and dark brown sugar — the molasses percentage differs (about 3.5% vs 6.5%), but the weight impact is less than 1 gram per ⅓ cup. The key word in that sentence is "packed." Brown sugar is unique among dry baking ingredients: its molasses coating makes the granules tacky, so they cling together and create unpredictable air gaps if you simply scoop. Packing eliminates those gaps and gives consistent results. Loosely scooped brown sugar weighs only about 53g per ⅓ cup — that 20-gram, 27% difference is the largest measuring-method gap in common baking ingredients, larger even than the scoop-vs-spoon debate for flour.

The Conversion Formula — Why "Packed" Is Part of the Math

Brown sugar is the rare ingredient where the measuring instruction is baked into the density number itself. The standard conversion of 220g per cup assumes packing — without it, you're working with a completely different ingredient weight:

  • Grams = Cups × Grams-per-Cup
  • Packed Brown Sugar (Light or Dark): 0.333 × 220 g = 73 grams
  • Loosely Scooped Brown Sugar: 0.333 × 160 g = 53 grams
  • Compare to Granulated White Sugar: 0.333 × 200 g = 67 grams

Light vs Dark Brown Sugar — Same Weight, Different Job

Light and dark brown sugar are interchangeable by weight, but not by effect. Dark brown sugar has roughly twice the molasses content of light (6.5% vs 3.5%), which means it's more acidic, more hygroscopic (water-attracting), and more intensely flavored. In recipes using only ⅓ cup of brown sugar — like a small batch of oatmeal cookies or the sugar component in a dry rub for ribs — the choice between light and dark is genuinely noticeable:

  • Light brown sugar (73g per ⅓ cup packed): Subtle butterscotch notes, gentle moisture boost. Best for cookies where brown sugar is a supporting player, not the star. Use when the recipe doesn't specify — light is the default "brown sugar" in most American cookbooks.
  • Dark brown sugar (73g per ⅓ cup packed): Pronounced molasses flavor, deeper color, more acidity (which activates baking soda more aggressively). Best for gingerbread, barbecue sauces, and anything where you want people to taste the brown sugar specifically. The extra acidity means dark brown sugar recipes may need slightly more baking soda to neutralize, though at ⅓-cup quantities the difference is small.

The weight is the same, so you can swap freely. But the flavor and chemistry shift is real — in a recipe with only ⅓ cup total, the choice between light and dark brown sugar is proportionally more significant than in a recipe with 2 cups. How to Pack Brown Sugar Correctly (There's Actually a Technique)

Most recipe writers assume you know how to pack brown sugar. Many home bakers don't, and the range of "packed" interpretations — from a gentle pat to a full-force press — spans about 15 grams per cup. Here is the standard method used by test kitchens: spoon brown sugar into a dry measuring cup, then press it down firmly with the back of a spoon (or your fingers) until it's compacted. Add more sugar, press again. Repeat until the cup is full and the sugar holds the shape of the cup when you turn it out onto a plate. A properly packed ⅓ cup of brown sugar should come out as a single solid brick that holds its shape. If it crumbles apart when turned out, you didn't pack hard enough — add more and press again. If it feels rock-hard and you needed a chisel to get it out of the bag, your brown sugar has dried out (see our guide to softening hardened brown sugar with a slice of bread or a damp paper towel trick).

Where 1/3 Cup Brown Sugar Actually Shows Up

A third-cup of brown sugar appears in exactly the kinds of recipes where its moisture and acidity matter most: the brown sugar in a six-cookie batch of chocolate chip cookies (paired with ⅓ cup granulated sugar for the classic chewy-crisp balance), the sweetener in a small pan of candied pecans for a salad topping, or the sugar in a dry rub for a single rack of ribs. It's also the amount called for in many small-batch banana bread and muffin recipes meant to use up two overripe bananas. In those recipes, the moisture from brown sugar is structurally important — using 53g of loose brown sugar instead of 73g packed means your banana bread will be drier, less sweet, and paler. The molasses in brown sugar is hygroscopic (it pulls moisture from the air and holds onto it), so every gram of brown sugar contributes to both sweetness and shelf-life softness.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/3 cup of brown sugar when packed?

A1: 73 grams for packed brown sugar — same number for both light and dark varieties. Packing means pressing the sugar firmly into the measuring cup with the back of a spoon until no air gaps remain and the sugar holds the cup's shape when turned out. If you scoop loosely without packing, you'll get only about 53g — a 20-gram shortfall that reduces both sweetness and moisture in your recipe.

Q2: Light vs dark brown sugar — do they weigh the same?

A2: Yes. Both weigh 220g per packed cup (73g per ⅓ cup). Dark brown sugar has about 6.5% molasses vs 3.5% for light, but that 3% difference in molasses content changes the weight by less than 1 gram per ⅓ cup. The molasses does matter for flavor (dark is stronger, more bitter, more acidic) and moisture (dark holds slightly more water), so they're not functionally identical — but for weight-based measuring, treat them as the same. If a recipe just says "brown sugar" without specifying light or dark, it means light brown sugar in American recipes.

Q3: What happens if I don't pack brown sugar when the recipe says to?

A3: You'll use about 27% less sugar by weight — roughly 53g instead of 73g for a ⅓-cup measure. The consequences cascade through your bake: less sweetness (obviously), less moisture (brown sugar's molasses is hygroscopic, so less sugar = drier result), less spread (sugar liquefies in the oven and promotes cookie spread), reduced browning (less sugar = less caramelization and Maillard reaction), and altered acidity (brown sugar is slightly acidic and activates baking soda — less sugar means less acid to react with, potentially leaving a soapy baking-soda taste). In barbecue rubs, packing is equally important — loose brown sugar means your bark won't caramelize properly. The packing instruction is one of the few measuring conventions where the difference between "doing it right" and "not bothering" is dramatic enough that you can see it, taste it, and feel it in the finished product.
