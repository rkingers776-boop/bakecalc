10 oz of cream in mL — the short answer

Ten US fluid ounces of cream is 295.74 mL — 10 × 29.5735, the same as any liquid. It weighs about 298 grams, which makes cream one of the closest liquids to water in density: the fat is lighter than water and the non-fat solids are heavier, and in heavy cream the two effects nearly cancel.

Under the imperial fluid ounce the volume is 284.13 mL. But the more important question with cream is not the millilitres — it is which cream the recipe means, because that choice changes the fat content by a factor of three.

Cream Is Four Different Ingredients

In British and American usage the single word covers a range of products that behave nothing like each other. Since the fat is what does the work in a recipe, choosing wrongly is not a small error:

  • Half and half — 10 to 18% fat. Will not whip. Thickens a sauce barely. Essentially milk with a little cream in it.
  • Light cream — around 20% fat, sometimes called table cream. Pours well, whips poorly or not at all.
  • Whipping cream — 30 to 36% fat. Whips into a soft, unstable foam that will not hold for long.
  • Heavy cream or heavy whipping cream — at least 36% fat. Whips to firm peaks and holds them. This is what American recipes mean by cream unless they say otherwise.

When a British recipe says double cream it means roughly 48% fat, which is heavier than anything sold under that name in the United States. Substituting American heavy cream for double cream works for whipping and gives a slightly less rich result in a sauce.

What 10 oz of Cream Whips Into

Cream roughly doubles in volume when whipped, because the fat globules are being beaten into a network that traps air. Ten fluid ounces is 1.25 cups of liquid, which becomes about 2.5 cups of finished whipped cream — a useful number to have before you start, since most recipes specify the whipped volume and leave you to work out how much cream to buy.

Two things change the yield. Cold cream whips to a greater volume than cream at room temperature, and cream that is under-whipped occupies less space than cream taken to stiff peaks. If a recipe is specific about the finished volume, buy a little more than the arithmetic suggests.

Where 10 oz of Cream Shows Up

  • Small cartons — 10 fl oz is a common single-serving size for coffee cream.
  • Sauce and soup recipes — quantities around this figure are typical for finishing a pan sauce for four.
  • Ice cream bases — a home batch often uses between 10 and 16 fl oz of cream.
  • Ganache — heavy cream at roughly equal weight to chocolate is the standard ratio, so 10 oz of cream makes about 300 g of ganache before any additions.

Why Cream Behaves Differently From Milk in Heat

Fat is what makes cream useful and also what makes it fragile. In a sauce, cream thickens because fat globules and proteins interact as the mixture heats. Push it too far, or add acid or a lot of salt at the wrong moment, and the globules coalesce — the sauce breaks and you are left with grease and watery liquid.

Milk does not have enough fat to break that way, which is why it can be simmered more patiently. Half and half sits in between and is the most common source of disappointing pan sauces: it looks like cream, pours like cream, and then fails to thicken as expected because 10% fat is not enough for the reaction to build.

Storing and Using the Rest of the Carton

Heavy cream is sold in sizes that rarely match what a recipe wants, so the leftovers matter. It keeps for about a week in the coldest part of the fridge, and it does not freeze well as liquid — the emulsion separates and it will never whip again, though it remains fine for cooking where it is being heated anyway. If you need to use it up, whip it with a little sugar and freeze the result; whipped cream freezes far better than the liquid it came from.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many mL is 10 oz of cream?

A1: 295.74 mL for 10 US fluid ounces of cream, which weighs about 298 grams. Heavy cream has a density close to water because its fat and its water fraction nearly balance each other out.

Q2: How much whipped cream does 10 oz of liquid cream make?

A2: About two and a half cups. Heavy cream roughly doubles in volume when whipped, so 1.25 cups of liquid becomes 2.5 cups of finished whipped cream. The exact figure depends on how cold the cream is and how far you take it.

Q3: Can I use half and half instead of heavy cream?

A3: Not usually. Half and half is 10 to 18% fat, against 36% for heavy cream. It will not whip to a stable foam at all, and in a sauce it thickens far less and breaks more easily under heat. It is a cream for coffee, not a cream for whipping.
