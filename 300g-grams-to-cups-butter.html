300 g of butter in cups — the short answer

Three hundred grams of butter is 1.32 cups — one cup plus about a third — which is 21 tablespoons or two and two thirds US sticks.

It is a quantity that appears most often in pastry rather than cake, and pastry is where butter's real function becomes visible. At 300 g the fat is no longer flavour or moisture; it is the mechanism that determines the texture of the finished thing.

What Shortening Actually Means

The word shortening predates the brand that took it over. It describes what fat does in a dough: it coats the flour proteins with a waterproof layer, and those proteins cannot then link to each other to form gluten. Gluten strands are long and elastic, and they are what give bread its chew. Cut them short and the texture turns tender and crumbly instead.

The mechanism explains the entire texture map of baking:

  • Almost no fat — bread. Long gluten strands, chewy crumb, structure that traps gas.
  • Moderate fat — cake. Enough gluten to hold a crumb, enough shortening to keep it soft.
  • A lot of fat — shortbread and pastry. Minimal gluten, crumbly and delicate, structurally weak by design.

The proportion matters as much as the presence. There is no amount of butter that can be added to a bread dough without eventually turning it into a cake, and there is no way to remove the butter from a shortbread without turning it into something closer to a cracker.

Why Butter and Oil Do Not Shorten the Same Way

Butter is solid at room temperature; oil is liquid. That physical difference changes when the fat coats the flour. Liquid oil spreads over the particles the moment it touches them, coating thoroughly and evenly. Solid butter stays in discrete pieces until it melts in the oven, so the coating is patchier and some gluten manages to form before the fat gets in the way.

The result is that oil produces a consistently tender crumb, and butter produces a more varied one — flaky in pastry, structured in cake. Neither is better; they are different tools, and substituting one for the other by volume rather than by fat content changes both the texture and the amount of water in the dough.

Where 300 g of Butter Shows Up

  • Shortbread — a large batch, at roughly two parts butter to three parts flour by weight.
  • Double-crust pies — pastry for two 9-inch shells, or one deep-dish pie with a lattice.
  • Enriched celebration cakes — a large two-layer cake with a high butter ratio.
  • Buttercream for a celebration cake — a generous batch with enough for piping.

Cold, Soft or Melted — Three Different Shortenings

The temperature of the butter changes which version of shortening you get, and at 300 g the effect on the finished texture is substantial. Cold butter cut into flour stays in distinct flakes, and when those flakes melt in the oven the water inside them turns to steam — that steam is what separates the layers in a flaky pie crust. Softened butter creams with sugar and holds air instead, which lifts a batter. Melted butter blends evenly and does neither, giving a dense, sandy result in the same pastry recipe.

Three hundred grams of butter is 300 g whichever state it is in. What varies is what it was asked to do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 300 g of butter?

A1: About 1.32 cups — one full cup plus roughly a third of a cup. In tablespoons that is 21, and in US sticks it is two and two thirds.

Q2: What does shortening mean in baking?

A2: Shortening is the old term for any fat used in baking, and it describes what fat does: it coats flour proteins so they cannot link into gluten, which shortens the strands and gives a tender, crumbly texture instead of a chewy one.

Q3: Does more butter always make a better bake?

A3: No. Up to a point fat tenderises and enriches; past that point the structure weakens and the bake cannot hold its shape. A shortbread uses a very high proportion of butter deliberately. A cake built on the same ratio would collapse.
