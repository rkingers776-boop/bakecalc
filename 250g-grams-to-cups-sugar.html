250 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

Two hundred and fifty grams of granulated sugar is 1.25 cups — one cup plus a quarter cup measure. It is also a quarter of a kilogram, which is why metric recipes reach for it so readily.

The straightforward answer assumes granulated sugar, and that assumption is doing more work than it looks. Sugar is not one ingredient but a family of them, and the volume that 250 g occupies depends entirely on which member of the family is in the bag.

The Same 250 g in Five Different Sugars

Weight is the fixed quantity. Volume is what moves, and it moves a long way:

  • Granulated white sugar — 200 g per cup, so 250 g fills 1.25 cups
  • Brown sugar, packed — 220 g per cup, so 250 g fills 1.14 cups
  • Coconut sugar — about 180 g per cup, so 250 g fills 1.39 cups
  • Powdered sugar, unsifted — 120 g per cup, so 250 g fills 2.08 cups
  • Powdered sugar, sifted — 100 g per cup, so 250 g fills 2.5 cups

From 1.14 cups to 2.5 cups for the same 250 g of sugar. That is more than double, and it is entirely a consequence of crystal size, moisture and how much air the particles leave between them.

The practical warning is for anyone substituting one sugar for another in a recipe written in cups. Measure 250 g of powdered sugar into the cup the recipe intended for granulated and you will overfill the bowl by an entire cup while believing you followed the instruction. Recipes that specify weight sidestep this; recipes in cups cannot.

Where 250 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • European cake recipes — 250 g of sugar against 250 g of butter is the classic pound cake starting point.
  • Frosting — a batch of buttercream for a single layer cake sits near this quantity, though it will be powdered rather than granulated.
  • Preserves — 250 g of sugar to 250 g of fruit is a 1 to 1 ratio, which is the traditional minimum for a set.
  • Breads and enriched doughs — sweet doughs use between 10 and 20 percent sugar against the flour, which puts a 1.5 kg batch near 250 g.

When the Sugar Choice Matters More Than the Amount

Swapping sugars is rarely a neutral trade, because each one brings something besides sweetness. Brown sugar carries molasses, which is acidic — and acid changes how baking soda behaves, so a recipe built on brown sugar may be relying on that acidity. Powdered sugar carries cornstarch, usually about 3%, which thickens liquids slightly and is why it makes a glaze rather than a syrup. Coconut sugar is less sweet by weight and absorbs differently.

At 250 g those differences are large enough to change a result. A cake made with 250 g of brown sugar instead of granulated will be darker, moister and slightly more acidic, and the leavening may not be tuned for it. None of that is a problem if it is deliberate. It becomes one when the swap was made only because one bag was closer to hand, and the recipe had no way to know.

What Happens to 250 g of Sugar in Liquid

Dissolved sugar takes up far less room than granulated sugar does. A cup of sugar stirred into a cup of water does not produce two cups of liquid — it produces about one and a quarter cups, because the sugar molecules slot into the spaces between water molecules instead of adding their own volume on top.

The effect shows up in three common places:

  • Simple syrup — equal volumes of sugar and water give more syrup than the water alone, but nothing approaching double.
  • Jam — the sugar dissolves into the water released by the fruit, so the pan holds roughly what it started with until the mixture reduces.
  • Custards and ice cream bases — sugar is dissolved before churning, so the final volume is set by the dairy rather than by the sugar.

The practical consequence: 250 g of sugar tells you nothing about the volume of the syrup it will make. Weigh the sugar going in if you want to know what it is doing, and measure the liquid coming out if you want to know what you have.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 250 g of sugar?

A1: One and a quarter cups of granulated sugar — one cup plus a level quarter cup measure. Sugar runs 200 grams per cup, and 250 is one and a quarter times that.

Q2: Does 250 g of powdered sugar measure the same?

A2: No, and the gap is large. Powdered sugar weighs about 120 grams per cup unsifted, so 250 grams fills 2.08 cups — nearly double the granulated figure. The two sugars are the same weight and very different volumes.

Q3: Why do different sugars weigh different amounts per cup?

A3: Because the air between the particles varies. Granulated crystals settle tightly at 200 grams per cup; the fine powder of powdered sugar traps far more air at 120; packed brown sugar compresses under moisture to 220. Same cup, same weight of sugar, three different volumes.
