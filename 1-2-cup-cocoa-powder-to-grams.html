1/2 cup of cocoa powder in grams — the short answer

Half a cup of cocoa powder is 50 grams when spooned and levelled, or 8 level tablespoons. Cocoa is taken as 100 g per cup, so the half cup divides it evenly — one of the few genuinely clean conversions in the cocoa family.

Half a cup is also the most common amount of cocoa in home baking, and the reason is not arithmetic.

Why Half a Cup Is the Common Amount

Half a cup of cocoa is enough to make a single-layer chocolate cake properly chocolate, enough to flavour a batch of brownies in a 9 by 13 pan, and not so much that the batter turns bitter or the starch load overwhelms the flour. Above about a cup, most recipes start blending cocoa with melted chocolate, because cocoa alone cannot supply enough fat to keep the crumb tender.

So 50 g is the sweet spot, and it is worth knowing the landmarks around it:

  • 1/4 cup — 25 g, enough to tint a batter brown
  • 1/3 cup — 33 g
  • 1/2 cup — 50 g, a proper chocolate cake
  • 3/4 cup — 75 g, a deep, dark result
  • 1 cup — 100 g, usually paired with melted chocolate

The Bloom: 50 g of Cocoa and Half a Cup of Boiling Water

Blooming is the step where cocoa is whisked into a hot liquid before it meets the rest of the batter, and it is the single change that most improves a homemade chocolate cake.

The reason is that cocoa particles are not soluble. They are tiny fragments of roasted cocoa solids suspended in liquid, and when they are merely stirred into a cold batter they stay partly dry at the centre, which reads as a chalky, dull chocolate flavour. Hot liquid hydrates the particles fully, dissolves the clumped starch, and volatilises the aromatic compounds that carry the chocolate aroma — which is exactly what you want, because those compounds are the flavour.

The standard ratio is volumetric and easy to remember: 1/2 cup of cocoa to 1/2 cup of boiling water. That is 50 g of cocoa to 118 g of water. The mixture forms a thick paste that loosens as it sits, and it goes into the batter warm.

How Much Liquid 50 g of Cocoa Drinks

Cocoa absorbs aggressively. Fifty grams will take up around 50 g of liquid before the mixture stops being a paste, and it will continue to thicken a batter as it stands. Two consequences follow.

  • A batter with cocoa needs more liquid than the plain version. Chocolate cakes routinely carry an extra quarter cup of milk or water over their vanilla counterparts, and it is not there to adjust the consistency — it is there to rehydrate the cocoa.
  • Blooming water counts as batter liquid. If you bloom 50 g of cocoa in half a cup of water, that 118 g is part of the recipe's hydration. A recipe that blooms and also adds the full quantity of milk has double-counted, and the result will be a slack, sunken cake.

Natural or Dutch at 50 g

Same weight, same volume, and quite different results in the oven.

  • Natural cocoa — acidic, so it works alongside baking soda and produces the reddish-brown colour people associate with old-fashioned chocolate cake.
  • Dutch-process cocoa — alkalised, so it needs baking powder rather than soda, and it delivers a darker, smoother, less sharp chocolate flavour.
  • Black cocoa — heavily alkalised and only about 95 g per cup, so half a cup is 47.5 g. It gives an almost-black colour and a slightly bitter, low-acid taste, and most recipes use it as a portion of the cocoa rather than all of it.

Whichever you use, bloom it the same way. Dutch-process cocoa actually disperses a little more readily in hot liquid, because the alkalising treatment changes the surface of the particles so they wet more evenly.

Measuring 50 g Without a Scale

  • Spoon and level — eight level tablespoons, spooned from the tin rather than scooped, is the reliable route. A scooped half cup can be 60 g.
  • Two level quarter cups — 4 tablespoons each, which is the same thing counted differently and easier to keep consistent.
  • Weigh it — 50 g, and the compressibility problem disappears entirely.

If you are blooming anyway, weighing is the obvious choice, because you are already at the counter with a bowl and a whisk.

What 50 g of Cocoa Makes

  • One 8 inch chocolate layer — 50 g of cocoa against 200 g of flour is a standard, well-balanced ratio.
  • A 9 by 13 pan of brownies — 50 g of cocoa plus 115 g of melted butter and 200 g of sugar.
  • A batch of chocolate cookies — 50 g is generous; most recipes use 30 to 40 g.
  • Half a litre of drinking chocolate — 50 g of cocoa whisked into 500 ml of hot milk, sweetened to taste, is a rich cup.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/2 cup of cocoa powder?

A1: 50 grams when spooned into the cup and levelled. Cocoa is taken as 100 grams per cup, and the half cup divides that evenly.

Q2: How much boiling water do I use to bloom 50g of cocoa?

A2: Half a cup, or 118 millilitres. The bloom ratio is a volume ratio rather than a weight one, so the water weighs about 118 grams against the cocoa's 50.

Q3: Do I have to bloom cocoa before baking with it?

A3: Not always. Blooming improves the flavour and colour of any recipe where the cocoa is a leading ingredient, but a batter with plenty of hot liquid and a long mixing time will hydrate it reasonably well on its own.
