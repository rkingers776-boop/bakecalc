500 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

Five hundred grams of granulated sugar is exactly 2.5 cups — two cups plus a level half cup. It is also half a kilogram, which makes it the natural unit for jam making and for large batches of anything.

At this quantity the interesting question stops being how to measure it and becomes what sugar is actually for. Half a kilo is enough sugar to preserve fruit, and that use has nothing to do with sweetness.

Sugar Preserves by Withholding Water

Microorganisms need water to grow, and not just water in the bowl — water they can actually draw on. Dissolved sugar binds water molecules and makes them unavailable, a property food scientists call water activity. Push enough sugar into a preserve and the mould and bacteria that would otherwise colonise it simply cannot get a foothold.

That is the entire principle behind jam, marmalade, candied fruit and fruit preserves. It is also why the sugar cannot be casually reduced:

  • A 1 to 1 ratio by weight — 500 g of sugar to 500 g of fruit. The traditional minimum, and the point at which the preserve keeps at room temperature.
  • Below 1 to 1 — the water activity is too high, and the preserve needs refrigeration and a shorter shelf life.
  • Well above 1 to 1 — candied fruit territory, where the sugar is high enough that the fruit becomes shelf stable on its own.

This is why a low-sugar jam recipe has to say so and has to specify refrigeration. It is not a lighter version of the same product; it is a different preservation strategy.

Why Sugar Itself Never Spoils

Dry crystalline sugar is one of the few foods with effectively unlimited shelf life. Bacteria, mould and yeast all need water, and sugar in its crystalline form has almost none available — the crystal structure locks it in. Sealed properly, a bag of sugar will still be sugar in a decade.

What does happen is caking. Sugar is hygroscopic, meaning it draws moisture from the air, and when the surface of the crystals dissolves and then dries again the crystals fuse into a solid block. The sugar is unchanged chemically and perfectly usable; it simply needs breaking up. A slice of bread or a terracotta disc in the container keeps the humidity steady enough to prevent it, and a brief spell in a food processor rescues a block that has already formed.

Where 500 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • Jam and preserves — 500 g of sugar to 500 g of fruit is the standard small-batch ratio, producing three to four jars.
  • Simple syrup — equal parts sugar and water by volume, so 500 g of sugar takes 2.5 cups of water.
  • Large cakes and batches — celebration cakes, sheet cakes and big cookie batches.
  • Half of a kilogram bag — the practical reason of all, since sugar is sold in one and two kilogram bags in metric countries.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 500 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly two and a half cups of granulated sugar, which is also half a kilogram. Two cups plus a level half cup measure reaches it with no partial filling.

Q2: Does sugar go bad?

A2: No. In its dry crystalline form sugar has no moisture for microorganisms to use, so it does not spoil. It can absorb moisture from the air and turn into a solid block, but that is a texture problem rather than a food safety one, and the sugar is still fine to use.

Q3: How much sugar does jam need to be safe?

A3: The traditional minimum is equal weights of sugar and fruit — a 1 to 1 ratio, which means 500 g of sugar to 500 g of prepared fruit. Below that the water activity is high enough for mould to grow, and the preserve needs refrigeration or another preservation method.
