300 g Flour to Cups Converter

2 1/2 cups (2.50 cups)

*Calculated using standard US customary baking cups via spoon-and-level method.

300 g of flour in cups — the short answer

Three hundred grams of all-purpose flour is exactly 2.5 cups — two cups and a half cup, with no partial spoon required. It is one of the cleanest conversions in the entire flour table.

That cleanliness is not an accident of the flour but of the arithmetic. Three hundred divides by 120 to give two and a half, and two and a half is a fraction every measuring cup set can build in two scoops. Had the recipe called for 290 g, you would be counting tablespoons.

Why 300 g Is the Loaf Default

Most sandwich loaf recipes in the United States are written around two and a half to three cups of flour, which is 300 to 360 g. Scale that up or down and you change the tin the dough fits, so 300 g has become the de facto standard for a one-pound loaf pan.

The rest of a typical 300 g flour formula follows from the baker's percentage convention, where the flour is always 100%:

  • Flour — 300 g, the reference
  • Water at 65% — 195 g, or 195 mL
  • Instant yeast — about 6 g, or 2%
  • Salt — about 6 g, or 2%
  • Butter, if enriched — 30 g, or 10%

Every figure in that list is a percentage of the flour. That is the structural reason flour conversion matters more than any other: it is not one ingredient among many, it is the number all the others are calculated from.

Where 300 g of Flour Shows Up

  • Sandwich loaves — a one-pound tin takes roughly 300 to 350 g of flour, yielding a baked loaf near 450 g.
  • Layer cakes — one nine-inch round is usually 250 to 300 g of flour.
  • Cookies — about twelve to fifteen drop cookies, depending on size.
  • Dumplings and noodles — a large batch for a family meal.

Baking With 300 g and No Scale

Two cups plus a half cup, both spooned and levelled. That is the whole method; no estimating is involved, which makes 300 g one of the few weights where the cup route is as reliable as the scale.

The one caveat is that this holds only if you spoon. Scoop two and a half cups from the bag and you will be holding 360 to 375 g of flour, because each scooped cup is heavier. The conversion is exact for one measuring technique and wrong by 25% for another, which is the single most important fact about any flour conversion.

The Same 300 g in a Different Flour

The two and a half cups figure belongs to all-purpose flour at 120 g per cup. Change the flour and the cup count changes with it, because each flour weighs differently for the same volume:

  • All-purpose flour — 120 g per cup, so 300 g is 2.5 cups
  • Bread flour — 130 g per cup, so 300 g is 2.31 cups, about two and a third
  • Cake flour — 110 g per cup, so 300 g is 2.73 cups, approaching two and three quarters
  • Whole wheat flour — 120 g per cup, so 300 g is 2.5 cups
  • Almond flour — 96 g per cup, so 300 g is 3.13 cups, just over three

Almond flour makes the point better than any explanation could. Three hundred grams is three hundred grams on any scale, but it fills between two and a third cups and three and a quarter cups depending on what is in the bag. Converting a weight into cups is only a meaningful operation once you know which flour is being converted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 300 g of flour?

A1: Exactly two and a half cups, using the 120 grams per cup standard for spooned and levelled all-purpose flour. No partial measure is needed — two cups plus one half cup measure lands on it precisely.

Q2: What can I bake with 300 g of flour?

A2: It makes one standard sandwich loaf, a single 9-inch round layer cake, or a batch of about a dozen cookies. It is the quantity most American loaf recipes are written around.

Q3: Why does 300 g work out so neatly?

A3: Because 300 divides by 120 without a remainder, giving two and a half. The same applies at 240 g, which is exactly two cups, and 360 g, which is exactly three. The awkward weights are the ones in between.
