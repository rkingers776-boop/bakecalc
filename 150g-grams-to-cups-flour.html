150 g of flour in cups — the short answer

One hundred and fifty grams of all-purpose flour is 1.25 cups — one full cup plus a quarter cup — measured by spooning the flour in and levelling it, at 120 g per cup.

That is the straightforward answer. The interesting one is that 150 g is also, almost exactly, what a single cup of flour weighs when the cup is plunged into the bag and swept level. Same number, half the volume, and no way to tell from the recipe which one the writer meant.

The Most Dangerous Round Number in Flour

Most conversion figures are dangerous in one direction. 150 g is dangerous in both, because it sits precisely where the two filling methods cross.

  • Spooned and levelled, a cup weighs 120 g. So 150 g is one and a quarter cups.
  • Scooped straight from the bag, a cup weighs 145 to 150 g. So 150 g is one cup.

Put those side by side and the problem is plain. A recipe that says 1 cup and a recipe that says 150 g may be asking for the same flour, or may be 30 grams apart. There is no way to resolve it from the text, which means the number alone is not enough information.

The practical rule is to follow the unit the recipe used. If it gives grams, weigh. If it gives cups, spoon and level — and accept that the writer's cup may have been heavier than yours. Recipes written in grams by someone who owns a scale are internally consistent, and that consistency is worth more than matching your flour to theirs exactly.

Where 150 g of Flour Shows Up

  • Freezer-friendly breads — 150 g of flour makes a very small loaf, about 250 g baked. This is a common size for testing a new formula.
  • Small tins — a six-inch round or a small loaf pan takes roughly this much flour.
  • European recipes — metric recipes reach for 150 g where an American one would say one and a quarter cups.
  • Pizza — a single 12-inch pizza dough often sits near 150 to 180 g of flour, depending on thickness.

Working in the Other Direction

If a recipe says 1.25 cups and you are converting to grams, 150 g is the answer at the spoon-and-level standard. If it says 1.5 cups, that is 180 g. And if it says 2 cups, that is 240 g — a figure worth memorising, since two cups is the most common flour quantity in American home baking.

The reverse conversions are where a scale pays for itself. Converting grams to cups requires deciding which filling method the original used; converting cups to grams does not, because you are simply measuring what you have. A scale removes the ambiguity from one direction entirely, which is half the problem solved.

How the Filling Method Changes 150 g

The three ways people fill a dry measuring cup produce three different weights from the same bag of flour, and at 150 g the spread is large enough to be the difference between a good bake and a dense one:

  • Sift, then spoon and level — the lightest cup, and 150 g becomes nearly one and a third cups.
  • Spoon and level — the standard. 150 g is 1.25 cups.
  • Dip and sweep — the heaviest. 150 g is one cup, and the flour has been compacted to get there.

Between the lightest and heaviest readings there is roughly a quarter cup of flour in play. In a lean bread dough, that is the difference between a workable dough and a stiff one, and it all comes down to a habit rather than a measurement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 150 g of flour?

A1: One and a quarter cups, by the spoon-and-level standard of 120 grams per cup. That is one full cup plus a quarter cup measure.

Q2: Why does 150 g of flour sometimes measure as one cup?

A2: Because a single cup filled by dip-and-sweep, plunging the measure into the bag, weighs 145 to 150 grams. So one cup and 150 grams describe the same scoop — but only when the cup was packed by that method, which is the one you should avoid.

Q3: Is a recipe that says 1 cup the same as a recipe that says 150 g?

A3: Only if the cup was scooped from the bag. A spooned and levelled cup is 120 grams, so the two recipes differ by 30 grams of flour — 25% — despite both describing a single cup. When a recipe gives grams, follow the grams.
