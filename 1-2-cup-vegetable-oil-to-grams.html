If you've baked muffins, quick bread, or a box mix, you've measured ½ cup of oil. It's 109 grams.

½ Cup Oil = 109 Grams. The Amount You've Measured a Hundred Times.

Flip through any muffin cookbook and count how many recipes call for ½ cup of oil. It's easily half of them. A standard dozen muffins uses about 240g of flour and 109g (½ cup) of oil — roughly a 2.2:1 flour-to-oil ratio by weight. Quick breads (banana, zucchini, pumpkin) use the same ratio. Boxed cake mixes almost universally want ½ cup of oil. It's the default amount, and now you know it's 109 grams.

The reason ½ cup became the standard isn't some culinary institute decree — it's that 109 grams of fat per standard batch produces a crumb that's moist without being wet, tender without falling apart. Less oil and you get a dry, crumbly texture after 24 hours. More oil and the muffin wrapper turns translucent with grease.

Brownies vs Muffins — Why Oil Amounts Differ

Brownie recipes almost never call for ½ cup of oil. They use butter — typically 113g (1 stick, ½ cup of butter). That's 113 grams of butterfat vs 109 grams of oil for the same volume — but butter also brings water and milk solids, which change the texture completely. An oil-based brownie will be fudgier; a butter-based one will have more structure. If you're converting a butter brownie recipe to oil (maybe for dairy-free), use 100g of oil to replace 113g of butter — roughly a 12% reduction — plus a tablespoon of water or dairy-free milk to mimic the water content you're losing. Your brownies will be slightly denser and fudgier than the original. Most people consider that a feature, not a bug.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is ½ cup of vegetable oil?

½ US cup of vegetable oil = 109 grams (218g/cup ÷ 2). For olive oil: 108g (216g/cup ÷ 2). The 1-gram difference between the two is within the margin of error for most kitchen scales.

Q2: My recipe says ½ cup oil but I only have a scale. Do I use 109g?

Yes — zero your scale, pour to 109g, done. Weighing oil is actually faster than measuring it in a cup because oil clings to the measuring cup and you lose some to the sides. With a scale, you pour directly into the bowl and every gram ends up in the batter. No scraping, no waste, no washing an oily measuring cup.
