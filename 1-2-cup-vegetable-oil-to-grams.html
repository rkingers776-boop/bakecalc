1/2 cup of vegetable oil in grams — the short answer

Half a cup of vegetable oil is 109 grams, or 8 level tablespoons. A full cup is 218 g, so the half cup is a clean division, and the arithmetic is the same density calculation scaled down.

Half a cup is also the most commonly written oil amount in home baking, which makes it the one worth knowing without looking it up.

Why Half a Cup Is the Recipe Standard

Oil quantities in recipes cluster around half a cup because that is the amount that suits the pans most people own.

  • A 9 by 13 sheet cake — half a cup of oil, sometimes a little more.
  • An 8 by 8 brownie pan — half a cup of oil is the classic amount, and it is what gives brownies their fudgy centre.
  • A dozen muffins — half a cup of oil against about 200 g of flour produces the tender, slightly greasy crumb muffins are supposed to have.
  • A single-layer carrot or banana cake — half a cup, plus the moisture the fruit brings.
  • A boxed cake mix — most of them are written around half a cup of oil, which is a large part of why they taste the way they do.

109 g Against 109 g of Butter

The trap with oil is that it looks interchangeable with butter in a cup, and it is not. On the fat side they are different products.

  • 109 g of oil — 109 g of fat, no water.
  • 113 g of butter — the weight of half a cup — of which about 90 g is fat and about 20 g is water.

So substituting half a cup of oil for half a cup of butter, by the same volume, removes about 19 g of fat and adds about 20 g of water. In a muffin that reads as a softer, wetter crumb that browns less. In a cookie it is closer to a failure, because the fat in a cookie is what limits spread and shapes the texture.

Working by weight fixes it. To replace half a cup of oil with butter you want about 136 g, since 136 g of butter at 80% fat delivers the 109 g of fat you started with. In the other direction, half a cup of butter — 113 g — is replaced by about 90 g of oil, which is 6 1/2 tablespoons rather than a half cup.

What the Water Does When You Swap

The 20 g of water in butter is easy to overlook and it is not trivial. It hydrates the flour, it turns to steam in the oven and contributes lift, and it is part of why butter cakes can have a lighter crumb than oil cakes despite containing less fat.

So when a recipe is converted from butter to oil, the missing water usually has to be added back — a tablespoon or two of milk or water. A converter that only swaps the fat and ignores the water produces a batter that is visibly stiffer than the recipe intended.

Do Not Cream Half a Cup of Oil

Oil cannot trap air. Whipping it with sugar produces a shiny, sandy paste, not the pale, fluffy mass that creamed butter becomes, and the air that butter would have trapped has to come from somewhere else.

Most oil-based recipes handle this by increasing the chemical leavening and by relying on the eggs, which are whipped or at least beaten hard. If you take a butter cake recipe and swap in half a cup of oil without changing the leavening, the cake will be flatter and denser than the recipe you started from.

What you get in return is shelf life. Oil remains liquid at room temperature, so an oil cake does not firm up as it cools and stays soft for days. Butter cakes go firm in the refrigerator and taste stale sooner.

Half a Cup in Tablespoons

  • 8 tablespoons — 109 g
  • 4 tablespoons — 54.5 g, or a quarter cup
  • 2 tablespoons — 27.3 g
  • 1 tablespoon — 13.6 g
  • 1 teaspoon — 4.54 g

The tablespoon figure is the useful one here, because a half cup of oil measured in tablespoons has no film to lose: each spoonful is poured out and scraped, and the losses vanish.

The Oil the Cup Keeps

Pouring a half cup of oil out of a dry measuring cup leaves roughly a gram of it on the walls. That is about 1% of the fat, which is negligible in a brownie and relevant in nothing at this volume.

It becomes worth thinking about only if you are measuring many small quantities in sequence. Four tablespoons poured separately accumulate three or four grams of loss — enough to matter in a recipe built on 27 g of fat, not enough to matter in one built on 109 g.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1/2 cup of vegetable oil?

A1: 109 grams, or 8 level tablespoons. The full cup is 218 grams, and the half cup divides it evenly.

Q2: How much butter replaces 1/2 cup of oil?

A2: About 136 grams of butter. Oil is pure fat while butter is roughly 80% fat and 20% water, so you need a fifth more butter by weight to deliver the same fat.

Q3: Can I swap 1/2 cup of oil for 1/2 cup of butter?

A3: You can, and it will work, but it will not be the same cake. Half a cup of butter is 113 grams — only about 90 grams of actual fat — so the swap quietly removes fat and adds water, giving a firmer, slightly drier crumb.
