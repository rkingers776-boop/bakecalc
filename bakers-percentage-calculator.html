The first time I tried to scale a sourdough recipe from a 500g-flour boule to an 800g-flour batard, I multiplied every ingredient by 1.6 on my phone calculator, wrote the numbers on a flour-dusted Post-it, and promptly misread "12.8g salt" as "18g." The bread was inedible. Three hours of work, a puddle of starter down the drain, and a loaf so salty my wife asked if I'd used seawater.

The problem wasn't my math. It was my system. I was treating every ingredient as a separate number to scale, when professional bakers treat them as ratios anchored to flour. Once I switched to baker's percentage, I never did scaling math again. I just decide how much flour I'm using, and every other ingredient falls out automatically. How Baker's Percentage Actually Works (In Plain English)

There's exactly one rule: flour always equals 100%. Every other ingredient is a percentage of the flour weight — not the total dough weight. Not the bowl weight. The flour weight.

So if you're using 500g of flour and 350g of water, the water is 350 ÷ 500 × 100 = 70%. That 70% is your hydration. It's the single most important number in bread baking — it tells you how wet your dough is. Change nothing else about a recipe except hydration, and you'll get a completely different loaf: 60% gives you a tight sandwich crumb, 75% gives you those big irregular holes everyone chases on Instagram.

Salt, yeast, starter, oil, sugar — they all work the same way. 10g salt with 500g flour = 2%. 3g instant yeast with 500g flour = 0.6%. Once you see a recipe this way, you can compare any two bread recipes at a glance, regardless of batch size.

The Four Numbers That Define Every Bread

Pick up any bread book, and the recipes reduce to four percentages:

1. Hydration (water %) — 55-60% for bagels and sandwich loaves, 65% for baguettes, 70-75% for sourdough boules, 80%+ for ciabatta and focaccia. Higher hydration = more open crumb, crispier crust, faster staling (counterintuitive but true — wetter doughs go stale faster once baked). Lower hydration = tighter crumb, softer crust, better for sandwiches.

2. Salt % — Almost always 1.8% to 2.2%. Below 1.5% and your bread tastes flat and the gluten is slack (salt strengthens gluten). Above 2.5% and it's inedible. This tiny window — less than one percentage point — is why weighing salt matters. A "teaspoon" of table salt weighs about 6g; a teaspoon of kosher salt weighs about 3g. Same volume, double the saltiness. Use grams.

3. Yeast or starter % — Commercial yeast: 0.3% to 1.5%. Lower for long cold ferments (pizza dough, artisan bread), higher for same-day bakes. Sourdough starter: 15% to 30% of flour weight. More starter = faster fermentation, tangier flavor. Less starter = slower, more complex flavor development.

4. Fat and sugar % — Enriched doughs (brioche, challah, milk bread) add butter, eggs, sugar, and milk. A lean dough has 0% fat; brioche can hit 50% butter (that's half the flour weight in butter — now you know why it tastes so good). Sugar ranges from 0% (baguette) to 10-12% (brioche, cinnamon rolls).

Converting a Family Recipe: A Worked Example

My aunt's oatmeal bread recipe — written on a card in 1987 — says:

  • 6 cups bread flour
  • 2 cups warm water
  • 1 cup rolled oats
  • 3 tablespoons honey
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2¼ teaspoons instant yeast

Step one: weigh everything once. Six cups of my flour, spooned and leveled, came to 780g. Two cups of water is 472g. One cup of oats: 100g. Three tablespoons honey: 63g. Two teaspoons of my salt: 12g. 2¼ teaspoons yeast: 7g.

Step two: identify the flour. Bread flour (780g) + rolled oats (100g) = 880g total flour. That's our 100%.

Step three: divide. Water: 472 ÷ 880 = 53.6%. Salt: 12 ÷ 880 = 1.4%. Yeast: 7 ÷ 880 = 0.8%. Honey: 63 ÷ 880 = 7.2%. Oats: 100 ÷ 880 = 11.4% (part of the flour blend).

Now I can make this recipe at any size. Want a 500g-flour version? Multiply each percentage by 5 (since 500g ÷ 100 = 5). Water: 53.6 × 5 = 268g. Done. No Post-it notes, no phone calculator, no ruined bread.

Why "Just Double It" Doesn't Work on Bread Recipes

A cake recipe that says "2 cups flour, 1 cup sugar, ½ cup butter" — you can double it. The ratios stay the same because all the units are volume-based and linear. But bread recipes have time as a hidden ingredient. A 500g-flour dough ferments differently than a 1000g-flour dough at the same yeast percentage. The larger mass holds heat better, ferments faster, and needs less yeast per gram of flour.

That's why commercial bakeries drop the yeast percentage as batch size increases. A 0.8% yeast recipe at 500g flour might only need 0.5% at 5kg flour to ferment in the same time. Baker's percentage makes this adjustment visible and precise. You can't do that with cups.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is baker's percentage?

Baker's percentage expresses every ingredient as a percentage of the total flour weight. Flour = 100%, always. If you have 500g flour and 350g water, the water is 70%. This lets you compare, scale, and troubleshoot recipes at a glance regardless of batch size. It's the universal language of professional bakers worldwide.

How do I convert my recipe to baker's percentages?

Weigh everything in grams. Add up all flour ingredients (they're your 100%). For each other ingredient: divide its weight by total flour weight, multiply by 100. Or use the calculator on this page — switch to "Recipe → Percentages" mode, enter your amounts, and check the Flour box for flour ingredients.

What hydration should my bread be?

60-65% for sandwich bread and easy-handling doughs. 65-70% for artisan loaves with some open crumb. 70-78% for sourdough boules and ciabatta with large irregular holes. Start low (60-65%) if you're learning — the dough is easier to shape and still makes great bread.

Can I use baker's percentage for recipes that aren't bread?

Yes. Cakes, cookies, pastry — anything weighed in grams works. The numbers look different (cake sugar might be 80-120% of flour weight, vs 0-10% for bread) but the math is identical. The calculator handles any recipe — just check which ingredients are your flour base.

Why does salt percentage matter so much?

Salt isn't just flavor — it strengthens gluten and controls fermentation. Too little (< 1.5%) and your dough is slack and bland. Too much (> 2.5%) and it's inedible and the yeast struggles. The window is narrow — about 1.8-2.2% — which is why weighing salt in grams matters more than any other ingredient.

What if my recipe uses multiple flours?

Add them together. 400g bread flour + 100g whole wheat = 500g total flour = 100%. The individual flours are sub-percentages of the flour blend (80% bread flour, 20% whole wheat). The calculator handles this — just check the Flour box for every flour ingredient and the math works automatically.
