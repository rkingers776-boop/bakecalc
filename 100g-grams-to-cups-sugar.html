100 g of sugar in cups — the short answer

One hundred grams of granulated sugar is exactly half a cup, or 8 level tablespoons. The conversion is exact because sugar measures 200 g per cup and 100 is half of that.

This is a rare thing among dry ingredients. Compare the flour page in this series: 100 g of flour is 0.83 cups, an awkward fraction that needs two measures to assemble. Sugar simply halves. The difference is not in the numbers, it is in what the ingredient is made of.

Why Sugar Holds Its Volume

Granulated sugar is crystalline. Each grain is a hard, roughly cubic solid of similar size, and a pile of them will not compress under any force a measuring cup can produce. Press down on a cup of sugar and nothing happens; the crystals are already resting on each other as tightly as their shape allows.

Flour is the opposite. Its particles are soft, flat and irregular, and they slide past each other and lock when pressed. That is why the filling method changes a cup of flour by 25 grams and a cup of sugar by 2 or 3.

The consequence is a difference in how much a conversion is worth trusting:

  • A cup of sugar — reliable to about 1.5%, whichever way you fill it
  • A cup of flour — reliable to about 20%, depending entirely on method

So when this site warns that cup measures are imprecise, the warning is really about flour. Sugar, salt and cornstarch are all close enough to incompressible that a cup measure is a decent instrument for them.

Where 100 g of Sugar Shows Up

  • Whipped cream and meringue — half a cup sweetens two cups of cream, or makes a small pavlova.
  • Coffee cakes and small sponges — half batches, where 100 g of sugar is half the sweetener.
  • Caramel — half a cup is the standard starting quantity for a small batch.
  • Simple syrup — equal parts sugar and water by volume, so 100 g of sugar pairs with half a cup of water.

Sugar as Structure, Not Just Sweetness

In a cake batter, sugar competes with flour proteins for the available water. The more sugar present, the less water the proteins get, and the less gluten develops — which is why high-ratio cakes are tender and why simply cutting the sugar from a recipe makes it tough and dry rather than merely less sweet.

Sugar also raises the temperature at which the batter sets. More sugar means a longer window in which the leavening gases can expand the crumb before the structure locks. And it holds moisture after baking, which is why a sugar-rich cake is still soft on day three while a lean bread is stale on day one.

None of those effects are matters of taste, and none of them can be adjusted by changing the amount of sugar without changing the rest of the formula. Reducing sugar in an existing recipe is a structural edit, not a preference.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 100 g of sugar?

A1: Exactly half a cup of granulated sugar, which is 8 level tablespoons. Sugar measures 200 grams per cup, so the conversion divides cleanly with no partial measure required.

Q2: Is a cup of sugar twice as heavy as a cup of flour?

A2: Nearly. A cup of granulated sugar is 200 grams; a cup of spooned all-purpose flour is 120 grams. The same volume of sugar carries about two thirds more weight, because sugar crystals are dense and settle tightly while flour is mostly air.

Q3: Does it matter whether I scoop or spoon sugar?

A3: Far less than it does with flour. Hard sugar crystals resist compaction, so a scooped cup and a spooned cup land within a few grams of each other. Flour can differ by 25 grams between the same two methods, which is why sugar is more forgiving of technique.
