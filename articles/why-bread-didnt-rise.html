Why Your Bread Didn't Rise — 6 Common Causes & How to Fix Each One

You followed the recipe. You waited two hours. You opened the towel and found a dough brick. Here's exactly what went wrong — and how to never let it happen again.

The first time I tried to bake a loaf of sandwich bread from scratch, I set the dough on the counter, covered it with a clean dish towel, and waited. The recipe said "1 to 1.5 hours, or until doubled." Two hours later, the dough looked exactly the same. Maybe a millimeter taller. I poked it. It didn't poke back. It just sat there, dense and cold, like it had decided not to participate.

I baked it anyway. The result was a brick. Not "dense bread." A brick. I could have built a wall with it.

Since then I've killed dough in every way possible. Water too hot — dead yeast. Water too cold — alive but hibernating. Forgot the salt. Forgot the yeast. Left it to rise in a 62°F kitchen in January and came back three hours later to a mildly puffed hockey puck.

Here's what I learned the hard way. If your dough didn't rise, it's one of these six things. Probably the first one.

1. You killed the yeast — water was too hot

Yeast is a living organism. It eats sugar, produces carbon dioxide, and dies at around 130–140°F (54–60°C). Not "gets sluggish." Dies. Permanently. No coming back.

The recipe says "warm water" — but warm to whose hand? My tap water comes out at about 120°F in the winter. That's "warm" to me. It's also 10 degrees shy of killing every yeast cell in the bowl. I learned this when I temped my "warm" tap water with a kitchen thermometer and realized I'd been nuking my yeast for months.

The real temperature range yeast wants: 100–110°F (38–43°C) for active dry yeast when you're blooming it. For instant yeast mixed directly into flour, aim for the final dough temperature to land around 75–80°F (24–27°C) — that's the sweet spot where yeast produces gas steadily without burning out.

The fix: Use a thermometer. Not your wrist, not the "baby bottle test." A $10 instant-read takes the guesswork out. If your water is 115°F or above, let it cool. If you killed a batch, don't try to save it by adding more yeast — the dead yeast cells release glutathione, which weakens gluten. Start over. For instant yeast, our yeast tsp-to-grams converter gives you an exact weight — 1 teaspoon = 3.1g — so at least the quantity is right before you worry about the water.

2. Your kitchen was too cold — and the recipe didn't account for it

Most bread recipes are written by people baking in 70–75°F kitchens. If your kitchen is 62°F in January — or 58°F overnight — the dough will still rise. Eventually. But "eventually" might mean 4 hours instead of 1, and most home bakers give up at the 2-hour mark and assume something went wrong.

A few winters ago I was testing a sourdough recipe that called for a 4-hour bulk ferment at "room temperature." My room temperature that week was 64°F. At 64°F, yeast activity drops by roughly half compared to 75°F. That 4-hour ferment should have been 8. I didn't know that. I shaped it on schedule anyway. The loaf came out flat as a focaccia.

What's happening mechanically: Yeast is an enzyme-driven organism. Enzyme activity doubles for roughly every 18°F (10°C) increase in temperature — and halves for every equivalent drop. A dough fermenting at 65°F takes twice as long as one at 75°F. It's not broken. It's just slow.

The fix — pick one:

  • Oven with the light on: Turn the oven light on, leave the door closed. Most ovens with just the light on stabilize around 75–85°F — perfect. Put the dough in a covered bowl on the middle rack. This is my go-to method in winter.
  • Microwave trick: Boil a cup of water in the microwave for 2 minutes. Push it to the back corner. Put your covered dough bowl in the front. Close the door. The residual steam keeps the cavity warm for about an hour.
  • Bottom-of-fridge-top trick: Many fridges vent warm air from the top. Put the dough bowl on top of the fridge, near the back. Check with a thermometer — some fridges vent at 80°F, some at 95°F (too warm). Measure first.

Whichever method you pick, don't guess. Check the actual temperature with a thermometer the first time. I once "proofed" dough on top of my fridge and discovered the vent was blowing 98°F air — nearly killed another batch.

3. Not enough kneading — the gluten never developed

Gluten is what traps the gas yeast produces. Without gluten, the CO₂ bubbles rise to the surface and escape instead of stretching the dough. No trapped gas = no rise. It's like trying to blow up a balloon with a hole in it.

Under-kneaded dough feels slack and tears easily when you pull it. It doesn't spring back when poked. It sticks to everything — the bowl, your hands, the counter — not because it's too wet, but because the gluten strands that give dough structure haven't formed yet.

The windowpane test tells you in 15 seconds whether you've kneaded enough. Pull off a golf-ball-sized piece of dough. Stretch it gently between your fingers, pulling outward in all directions. If it stretches into a thin, translucent membrane without tearing — like a windowpane — the gluten is developed. If it tears immediately, keep kneading. We have a full guide to the windowpane test here.

How long? By hand, 10–12 minutes of steady kneading. With a stand mixer and dough hook, 6–8 minutes on speed 2–3. Both assume you're working with bread flour (12–14% protein). If you're using all-purpose flour (10–12% protein), add 2–3 minutes — there's less gluten-forming protein to work with, so you need more mechanical development to compensate.

And if you're using the wrong flour entirely — cake flour for bread, say — the protein isn't there to develop in the first place. Check your bag. For bread, you want at least 12% protein on the label. Measure precisely with our flour cups-to-grams converter — bread flour weighs about 130g per cup, slightly more than all-purpose at 120–125g, because the higher protein absorbs more moisture.

4. Under-proofed — you didn't wait long enough, but it looked right

Proofing is the final rise after shaping — the last chance for the yeast to puff the dough before it hits the oven. Under-proofed dough goes into the oven tight and tense. The yeast hasn't produced enough gas yet. The oven heat kills the yeast before it gets a chance to finish the job, and the loaf comes out dense with a tight, tunneled crumb — small isolated holes surrounded by heavy dough, not the open airy structure you wanted.

This one is sneaky because under-proofed dough looks risen. It's taller than when you shaped it. The surface feels dry and smooth. But it hasn't relaxed — the gluten is still tight from shaping, and when you poke it with a floured finger, the indentation springs back immediately, like pressing on a balloon. That's the sign. If the dough springs back fast, it's not ready.

The poke test — the most reliable way to tell: Flour your fingertip. Press gently about ½ inch into the dough.

  • Springs back immediately, no dent left: under-proofed. Give it 15–20 more minutes and test again.
  • Slowly springs back but leaves a slight indent: ready. This is your window.
  • Dent stays, dough deflates slightly: over-proofed (see cause #5).

Time is a guideline, not a rule. "Proof for 45 minutes" means 45 minutes in the recipe developer's kitchen. Your kitchen, your flour, your humidity — all different. Use the poke test.

5. Over-proofed — the structure gave out

Over-proofing is the opposite mistake: you let the dough rise too long, the gluten network stretched past its limit, and the whole thing collapsed. The yeast exhausted the available sugars and started dying off. The gas that had built up escaped through micro-tears in the overstretched gluten. The dough deflates when you score it, or collapses in the oven within the first 5 minutes.

I over-proofed a batch of dinner rolls once because I got distracted. Came back two hours later to find the dough had risen, domed beautifully, and then sunk back down in the middle like a deflated balloon. I baked them anyway — they came out flat, pale, and tasted faintly alcoholic. That's another sign: over-proofed dough smells like beer. The yeast has run through the sugars and started producing ethanol as a byproduct.

The fix (before it's too late): If you catch it early — the dough has over-risen but hasn't collapsed yet — gently punch it down, reshape it, and let it proof again. The second rise will be faster because the yeast is still active and the gluten is already developed. Watch it closely this time.

The fix (after collapse): If the dough has already collapsed, you can still salvage it as flatbread, focaccia, or pizza dough. Press it into an oiled pan, dimple the surface with your fingertips, drizzle with olive oil and salt, and bake at 450°F for 15–18 minutes. It won't be a sandwich loaf. But it'll be bread. Over-proofed dough also works as a pizza base — the thin shape doesn't need the same structural lift. Run the adjusted weights through the flour converter if you decide to split it into multiple flatbreads.

How to prevent it: Set a timer. The poke test (cause #4) catches both under- and over-proofing — if the dent stays and the dough deflates, you've gone too far. At warm room temperature (75°F+), the window between "perfectly proofed" and "over-proofed" can be as short as 15–20 minutes.

6. Salt touched the yeast directly — or you forgot it entirely

Salt kills yeast on contact. Not "over time." Immediately. If you dump the salt directly onto the yeast in the mixing bowl before stirring, the salt draws water out of the yeast cells through osmosis and kills a significant portion of them before the dough ever comes together.

I learned this one from a professional baker who watched me dump salt, yeast, and flour into a bowl in that order. She stopped me mid-pour. "Salt on one side of the bowl, yeast on the other. Mix the flour in between them. Then add water." It's such a small thing — takes one extra second — but it's the difference between active yeast and half-dead yeast.

The fix: Salt goes on the opposite side of the bowl from the yeast. Or dissolve the yeast in water first, then add salt to the flour separately. In a stand mixer, add yeast and flour, mix briefly, then add salt — the flour coats the yeast granules and protects them. This is standard procedure in commercial bakeries for a reason.

And then there's the opposite mistake: forgetting the salt entirely. Salt-free bread rises faster than normal — salt slows yeast activity — but the result is a pale, bland loaf with no crust color and a gummy crumb. Salt strengthens gluten, controls fermentation rate, and enables the Maillard reaction that gives bread its golden crust. Without it, the dough over-proofs within half the expected time and the final loaf looks anemic. If you forgot the salt and the dough has already risen, there's no good fix — bake it anyway as practice, note what the crumb looks like so you recognize saltless bread next time, and use a scale for your salt: 1 teaspoon = 5.7 grams. Eyeballing salt is fine for soup. Not for bread. Most bread formulas target 1.8–2% salt relative to flour weight — see our baker's percentage guide for how to calculate that.

Not on this list: things that people blame but usually aren't the problem

Three things I see people blame for flat bread that rarely turn out to be the actual cause:

  • "The yeast was expired." Maybe — but dry yeast stored in the fridge or freezer lasts well past its printed date. I've used instant yeast 18 months past the date on the packet, stored in the freezer, and it bloomed fine. Test it before you blame it: ½ tsp yeast + ½ tsp sugar + ¼ cup warm water (105°F). If it foams within 10 minutes, the yeast is alive. The problem is something else.
  • "I used the wrong kind of yeast." Active dry and instant yeast are interchangeable in most recipes if you adjust the method. Active dry needs blooming in warm water first. Instant can be mixed directly into flour. If you use active dry without blooming it, it'll be slow. But it'll still work — it just needs more time. (Fresh/cake yeast is a different story: use 3× the weight of dry yeast, crumble into warm water, and use within 2 weeks. If you bought fresh yeast and it sat in the fridge for a month, it's probably dead.)
  • "The recipe was bad." It's possible. But bread is a ratio-based formula — flour, water, yeast, salt — and if the baker's percentage is roughly right (60–75% hydration, 1–2% yeast, 1.8–2% salt), the recipe is probably fine. The technique is what failed.

📋 The Pre-Bake Bread Checklist — Tape This to Your Fridge

  • Water temp for blooming yeast: 100–110°F (38–43°C) — not hot tap water
  • Yeast foamed within 10 minutes of blooming — if not, the yeast is dead; start over
  • Flour weighed, not scooped — 1 cup bread flour = 130g, all-purpose = 120–125g
  • Yeast and salt added to opposite sides of the bowl — 1 tsp salt = 5.7g
  • Dough kneaded until it passes the windowpane test — 10–12 min by hand
  • Proofing spot is 75–85°F — oven with light on, or microwave with a cup of hot water
  • Poke test before baking: dent springs back slow and leaves a slight indent = ready
  • Oven fully preheated before dough goes in — bread needs the initial heat shock for oven spring

Bread is less forgiving than cake. Cake has eggs and chemical leaveners as backup. Bread has yeast and gluten — two biological systems that have to cooperate. When they don't, there's no hiding it.

But bread is also more rewarding to get right. The first time you pull a loaf out of the oven that rose exactly how you wanted — tall, golden, with a crust that crackles as it cools — you'll understand why people get obsessed.

And if your bread didn't rise today: slice the brick thin, toast it, and call it artisan flatbread. You're still learning. Next weekend, go through this list. One of these six things was off. Fix that one thing. The rest will follow.
