# 3 Ways to Substitute Buttermilk (That Actually Work)

**Published:** 2026-07-11 | **Read time:** 6 min

---

You're halfway through a pancake recipe and it calls for buttermilk. You don't have any. The fridge has regular milk, maybe a lemon, maybe some yogurt. Will any of these actually work?

Short answer: yes — if you pick the right method for what you're baking.

Buttermilk does two things in a recipe. It adds acidity (which reacts with baking soda to create lift), and it adds liquid fat (for tenderness). A good substitute needs to do both. Here are the three methods that do, ranked from most reliable to "works in a pinch."

---

## Method 1: Milk + Lemon Juice or White Vinegar

**Ratio:** 1 cup milk + 1 tablespoon acid = 1 cup buttermilk substitute

This is the standard — the one every baking blog recommends, and for good reason. It works.

Pour 1 tablespoon of lemon juice or plain white vinegar into a liquid measuring cup. Fill with whole milk to the 1-cup line. Stir once. Let it sit for 5–10 minutes.

What happens: the acid curdles the milk proteins just enough to mimic buttermilk's tang and thickness. You'll see small curds form on the surface — that's the sign it's ready.

**Best for:** Pancakes, waffles, quick breads, muffins, biscuits — anything where buttermilk isn't the star flavor.

**Don't use for:** Delicate cakes where you'd taste the lemon. White vinegar is more neutral here, but if your palate is sensitive, use Method 2 instead.

**Scaling it:** Need ½ cup? Use ½ cup milk + 1½ teaspoons acid. Need ¼ cup? ¼ cup milk + ¾ teaspoon. The 1-tablespoon-per-cup rule scales linearly.

One detail most guides skip: **use whole milk if you have it.** Skim milk produces a thinner substitute that won't replicate buttermilk's body. 2% works okay. Whole is best.

---

## Method 2: Yogurt or Sour Cream, Thinned

**Ratio:** 1 cup plain yogurt (or sour cream) + 2–3 tablespoons milk, whisked together

Plain yogurt is already cultured — it has the same tangy acidity as buttermilk, just thicker. Thin it with a splash of milk until it pours like buttermilk (about the consistency of heavy cream), and you've got a near-perfect match.

Greek yogurt works but needs more thinning — start with ¾ cup Greek yogurt + ¼ cup milk.

Sour cream does the same job. Same ratio: 1 cup sour cream + 2–3 tablespoons milk.

**Best for:** Cakes, scones, cornbread, and anything where you want extra richness. This is actually an *upgrade* over buttermilk for pound cakes and coffee cakes — the extra fat makes a noticeably more tender crumb.

**Don't use for:** Recipes where you need buttermilk's thinner consistency without adjustment. If the batter already looks thick, add another tablespoon of milk.

**Flavor note:** Yogurt and sour cream are more tangy than buttermilk. If you're sensitive to sour flavors, cut the yogurt with an extra tablespoon of milk and add ¼ teaspoon of baking soda to neutralize some of the acid.

---

## Method 3: Milk + Cream of Tartar

**Ratio:** 1 cup milk + 1¾ teaspoons cream of tartar

Cream of tartar is pure tartaric acid in powder form. It delivers the acidity buttermilk needs without adding any liquid or flavor of its own.

Whisk the cream of tartar into the dry ingredients (it doesn't dissolve well in cold milk), then add the plain milk as your liquid. The acid activates as soon as it hits the milk in the batter.

**Best for:** White cakes, vanilla cupcakes, and any recipe where you absolutely don't want a hint of lemon or yogurt flavor. Also good if you're already using cream of tartar in the recipe (some snickerdoodles and angel food cakes call for it).

**Don't use for:** Recipes where buttermilk's thickness matters — the milk-alone texture won't match. If the recipe relies on buttermilk for a thick batter (like some biscuit recipes), use Method 1 or 2.

---

## Which Method for Which Recipe

| Recipe type | Best method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pancakes & waffles | Method 1 (milk + lemon) | Fast, always have ingredients, slight lemon note disappears in cooking |
| Muffins & quick breads | Method 1 or 2 | Either works; yogurt adds moisture for banana bread |
| Biscuits & scones | Method 2 (yogurt + milk) | Extra fat = flakier layers |
| Layer cakes | Method 2 (yogurt) or 3 (cream of tartar) | Method 2 for richness, 3 for neutral flavor |
| Cornbread | Method 2 (sour cream) | Traditional Southern shortcut — sour cream cornbread is its own thing |
| Marinades (fried chicken) | Method 1 (milk + vinegar) | Buttermilk brine for fried chicken is a classic; vinegar gets closest to the tang |

---

## What Doesn't Work

**Plain milk by itself.** You'll lose the acid reaction with baking soda, which means less rise and a heavier texture. If the recipe has baking powder (not just soda), you might get away with it — baking powder contains its own acid — but the result will be noticeably denser.

**Just adding more baking soda to milk.** Baking soda needs acid to react. Adding more soda to plain milk just makes things taste soapy. Don't do this.

**Plant milks + acid.** Almond milk + lemon juice technically works — the acid still curdles the proteins — but the result is much thinner than real buttermilk and lacks the fat content. If you need a dairy-free buttermilk substitute, soy milk is your best bet (highest protein content = best curdle), followed by oat milk. Add 1 tablespoon of melted coconut oil per cup of plant milk to replace some of the missing fat.

---

## The One-Minute Reference

- **Milk + 1 Tbsp lemon juice or white vinegar** → let sit 5 min → works for almost everything
- **Plain yogurt + splash of milk** → whisk together → best for cakes and scones
- **Milk + 1¾ tsp cream of tartar** → mix into dry ingredients → best neutral flavor
