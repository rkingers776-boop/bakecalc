How to Make Nutrition Labels for Homemade Baked Goods

The first time someone asked me "is that gluten-free?" at a market stall, I froze. That question is a nutrition-label question in disguise. Here's what I've learned about FDA rules, calories, allergens, and labeling — and the free tool that prints the whole thing.

You don't need a nutrition label to sell most homemade baked goods. That's the honest answer, and most articles make it the whole article. But here's the part they skip: the question people actually ask isn't "do I legally need one." It's "what's in this, and can I eat it?" That question comes from every customer — the one with a peanut allergy, the one counting carbs, the one who just read that muffin has 600 calories.

So this article is the label guide I wish I'd had: whether you're exempt, how to actually count calories in a recipe, the serving-size rule everyone gets wrong, and the free nutrition label generator that turns your recipe into a printable FDA-format label in about a minute.

First: do you actually need a label?

Here's the law, in plain English. The FDA requires nutrition labels on most packaged food — but there's a carve-out for small operations. You're exempt if:

  • You have fewer than 100 full-time-equivalent employees, and
  • You sell fewer than 100,000 units of that product per year in the US.

If you're a home baker selling at farmers markets, bake sales, and local orders, you're almost certainly under both thresholds. Congratulations — you don't legally need labels.

One trap, though: the moment you make a nutrient claim on the package, the exemption evaporates. Write "low fat," "high fiber," "sugar-free," or even "gluten-free" on a label, and you now need full nutrition labeling regardless of size. This is how the FDA shut down Butterfly Bakery in New Jersey back in 2013 — they printed "sugar-free" on products that had three times the sugar the claim implied. Shutdown, recalls, legal fees. All avoidable.

My recommendation, even though the law doesn't require it: label anyway. Here's why — a sticker with a nutrition panel costs about 15 to 30 cents. It tells every customer three things at once: this is a real product, this person knows what they're doing, and this baker has nothing to hide. At a market where you're competing with a dozen other tables of unlabeled cookies, that 25-cent sticker is the difference between "nice cookies" and "a brand."

The math: how to count calories in a recipe

Here's the process, and it's simpler than it sounds. You do it once per recipe, then you're done forever.

Step one: weigh everything in grams. Not cups. A cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 120 to 150 grams depending on who scoops it — that 25% swing propagates straight into your calorie count. If your recipe is written in cups, use a cups-to-grams converter and record gram weights. It's tedious the first time. It's permanent after that.

Step two: multiply each ingredient by its per-100g nutrient values. The reference is USDA FoodData Central — the free government database that powers MyFitnessPal and every serious nutrition app. For example, butter has about 81 grams of fat per 100g. Your 170g of butter contributes 170 × 0.81 = 137.7g of fat to the batch. Do that for calories, fat, carbs, protein, sodium, fiber — for every ingredient.

Step three: divide by servings. This is where most homemade labels go wrong, and it deserves its own section below.

The serving-size rule everyone gets wrong

The FDA doesn't let you pick "whatever I feel like cutting." It publishes Reference Amounts Customarily Consumed (RACC) per product category, and your label has to use them:

  • Cookies: 30 grams
  • Muffins & quick breads: 55 grams
  • Cake, plain (no frosting): 80 grams
  • Cake, frosted: 125 grams
  • Bread / rolls: 50 grams

Servings per container = total finished weight ÷ RACC for your category, rounded down to the nearest whole number. That 900g banana bread falls in the 55g quick-bread category: 900 ÷ 55 = 16.4 → 16 servings. Sixteen, not eight. Each serving is 55 grams and 190 calories.

This feels wrong to most home bakers — "nobody eats 1/16th of a loaf." And you'd be right. But the rule exists so a muffin from your stall and a muffin from the grocery store describe the same amount of food. If you'd rather label "1/8 loaf (113g)," you can use a different serving size as long as the label says the weight — the RACC is a default, not a cage. What you can't do is quietly label 1/8th of a loaf while pricing it as if it were half.

Allergens: the label part that can kill someone

Calories are important. Allergens are life-or-death, and this is where a lot of home bakers wing it. US law (FALCPA) requires you to declare the top nine allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame.

For baked goods, you'll almost always be dealing with four: wheat, milk, eggs, and tree nuts. You declare them one of two ways:

  • Inline in the ingredient list: "Flour (wheat), Butter (milk)..."
  • A separate Contains statement: "Contains: Wheat, Eggs, Milk, Tree Nuts"

And here's the uncomfortable part about selling baked goods: cross-contamination is real. If you bake peanut butter cookies on a sheet pan that later bakes plain sugar cookies, your "no peanuts" cookies can still trigger an allergy. If you're not running a fully dedicated allergen-free kitchen, the honest move is a "Made in a facility that processes tree nuts and peanuts" note — it's not legally required for all products, but it's the difference between a lawsuit and a thank-you.

What labeling actually changed for me

I started labeling about a year after I started selling, and the difference wasn't in the numbers — it was in the conversations. Before labels, every market stall started the same way: people would pick up a cookie, turn it over, squint at it, and put it back. After labels, they'd pick it up, read it, and say "oh, 190 calories, perfect" — or "wait, there's no nuts in this?" and buy three. The label did the selling that I was too awkward to do by talking.

The other thing it did was force me to standardize my recipes. You can't print a label for a recipe that changes every batch. Once I was labeling, I weighed every ingredient, wrote every recipe down, and stopped improvising. That alone made my product more consistent — and consistency is what turns one-time buyers into regulars. The label wasn't paperwork. It was the thing that made me bake like a business instead of like a hobbyist with a stall.

If you're just starting out and the idea of a full FDA panel feels like overkill, start smaller: print the calories per serving and the Contains statement. That's 80% of what your customers actually check, and it takes five minutes. You can upgrade to the full panel later — the recipe math is the same either way, so nothing you do now gets thrown away.

The baking-specific stuff most label guides miss

Generic nutrition-label articles assume you're labeling soup or granola. Baked goods have their own quirks:

Water loss changes weight, not calories

Your raw dough weighed 1,000g. After baking, the loaf is 900g — water evaporated. The calories didn't change; the per-gram density did. Always weigh the finished product and use that for servings-per-container math, or your per-serving numbers will be off by the moisture percentage (typically 5-12% for breads, more for cookies).

"Gluten-free" is a claim, not a description

Write "gluten-free banana bread" on the package and you've made a nutrient-content claim — which, as covered above, triggers the label requirement and requires you to actually verify the product is gluten-free. Don't put it on the label until you can back it up.

Your packaging label needs the basics too

In the US, a food package also needs: the common name of the food, net weight in both metric and US units, an ingredient list in descending order by weight, and your name and address (or the cottage-food equivalent in your state). A nutrition panel without an ingredient list is a half-label — and the ingredient list is the one customers with allergies actually read first.

Bottom line

You probably don't legally need a nutrition label for your home-baked goods. Add one anyway. It's 25 cents, it takes about a minute with the generator, and it's the single cheapest way to look like you know what you're doing. Use the RACC serving sizes, weigh your finished bake, list your allergens, and keep the "gluten-free" claims off until they're verified.
