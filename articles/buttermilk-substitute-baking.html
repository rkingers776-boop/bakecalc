3 Ways to Substitute Buttermilk in Baking (That Actually Work)

No buttermilk? Don't scrap the recipe. Three methods using milk, yogurt, or cream of tartar — each with exact ratios and the "why it works" behind them.

You're halfway through a pancake recipe. It calls for buttermilk. You open the fridge — no buttermilk. Regular milk, a lemon, maybe some plain yogurt on the bottom shelf.

Will any of these work?

Yes — if you pick the right substitute for what you're making. Buttermilk does two things in a recipe: it brings acidity (which reacts with baking soda for lift) and liquid fat (for tenderness). A substitute has to deliver both. Here are the three methods that do — ranked from the reliable standard to the specialized upgrade.

Method 1: Milk + Lemon Juice or White Vinegar

Ratio: 1 cup whole milk + 1 tablespoon acid = 1 cup buttermilk substitute

This is the one every baking blog recommends. It works — and there's a reason it's the standard.

Pour 1 tablespoon of lemon juice or plain white vinegar into a liquid measuring cup — use our oz-to-mL converter if your measuring cup has metric markings. Fill with whole milk to the 1-cup line. Stir once. Walk away for 5 to 10 minutes.

What's happening: the acid causes milk proteins to curdle — the same chemistry that makes buttermilk itself — producing a slightly thickened, tangy liquid. You'll see tiny curds on the surface. That's exactly what you want.

Scaling: Need ½ cup? ½ cup milk + 1½ teaspoons acid. Need ¼ cup? ¼ cup milk + ¾ teaspoon. The tablespoon-per-cup rule scales evenly. Weighing ingredients for precision? A half cup of this substitute weighs roughly the same as a half cup of buttermilk — about 120 grams per half cup.

Best for: Pancakes, waffles, quick breads, muffins, biscuits — anything where buttermilk isn't a featured flavor.

Skip it for: Delicate vanilla cakes where you'd taste the lemon. White vinegar is more neutral, but if your palate is sensitive, use Method 2.

One detail most recipes skip: use whole milk. Skim milk makes a thin, watery substitute that won't replicate buttermilk's body. 2% is okay. Whole is best. Buttermilk has about 2% milk fat — whole milk is closer to 3.25%, which gets you in the right ballpark.

Method 2: Yogurt or Sour Cream, Thinned with Milk

Ratio: 1 cup plain yogurt + 2–3 tablespoons milk, whisked together

Plain yogurt is already cultured — same bacterial fermentation that creates real buttermilk. It has the same tang, same acidity, just thicker. Thin it with a splash of milk until it pours like heavy cream, and you've got a near-perfect buttermilk match.

Greek yogurt needs more thinning: ¾ cup Greek yogurt + ¼ cup milk = roughly 1 cup buttermilk equivalent.

Sour cream works the same way. Same ratio: 1 cup sour cream + 2–3 tablespoons milk, whisked smooth.

Best for: Cakes, scones, cornbread — anything where extra fat is a feature, not a bug. For pound cakes and coffee cakes, this is an upgrade over regular buttermilk. The added butterfat produces a noticeably more tender crumb.

Skip it for: Recipes that already run heavy on butter or oil. You can overshoot on fat and get a greasy result.

Flavor note: Yogurt and sour cream are tangier than buttermilk. If you're not a sour-flavor person, thin the yogurt with an extra tablespoon of milk and add a pinch (⅛ teaspoon) of baking soda to the dry ingredients to neutralize some acid.

Method 3: Milk + Cream of Tartar

Ratio: 1 cup milk + 1¾ teaspoons cream of tartar

Cream of tartar is powdered tartaric acid — the same acid found in grapes and tamarind. It delivers the acidity buttermilk needs without adding liquid volume or flavor of its own. For some recipes, that's exactly the point.

Here's the catch: cream of tartar doesn't dissolve well in cold milk. The better technique is to whisk the cream of tartar into your dry ingredients (flour, baking soda, salt), then use plain milk as your liquid. The acid activates the moment it hits the milk in the batter — no waiting, no curdling, no taste.

Best for: White cakes, vanilla cupcakes, angel food cake — any recipe where you need buttermilk's acid but absolutely don't want a trace of lemon or yogurt flavor. Also handy if cream of tartar is already in the recipe (some snickerdoodles, meringues).

Skip it for: Recipes where buttermilk's thickness is structural — like drop biscuits where the batter needs to hold shape. Plain milk won't match that viscosity.

If you bake a lot: buying real buttermilk powder (sold in the baking aisle, near the powdered milk) is a better solution than cream of tartar for frequent use. It's shelf-stable for months and you just add water. But for the once-a-year recipe, cream of tartar is fine.

What Doesn't Work (Common Mistakes)

Plain milk with nothing added. You lose the acid reaction with baking soda. Less rise, heavier texture. If the recipe uses baking powder (which has its own acid), you might get away with it — but the result will be denser.

Adding extra baking soda to plain milk. Baking soda is a base, not an acid. Adding more soda to milk without an acid partner just leaves a soapy, metallic aftertaste. Don't.

Flavored yogurt. Vanilla yogurt in cornbread sounds interesting. It's not. The added sugar and flavoring throw off both the taste and the liquid ratio. Plain only.

Almond milk + lemon juice as a 1:1 dairy swap. It technically curdles, but the result is much thinner than real buttermilk and lacks fat. If you're dairy-free, soy milk is the best plant milk for this (highest protein = best curdle), followed by oat. Add 1 tablespoon melted coconut oil — about 14 grams per tablespoon — to replace some of the missing fat.

📋 One-Minute Reference — Save This

  • Milk + lemon juice or vinegar: 1 Tbsp acid per cup of milk, rest 5 min — works for almost everything
  • Plain yogurt + milk: Thin to pouring consistency — best for cakes, scones, cornbread
  • Milk + cream of tartar: 1¾ tsp per cup, whisk into dry ingredients — best neutral flavor
  • Always use whole milk (or at least 2%) for Methods 1 and 3 — skim milk is too thin
  • Scale linearly: the 1 Tbsp per cup rule works whether you need ¼ cup or 2 cups

The truth about buttermilk substitution: it's not about finding a perfect replica. It's about understanding what buttermilk does — acid activation and fat content — and choosing the substitute that delivers whichever one your recipe depends on more.

Pancakes need the acid for lift more than they need the fat. Pound cake needs the fat for tenderness more than the acid. Pick accordingly, and you won't regret not running to the store.
