Freeze Cookie Dough: Balls, Logs and Baking From Frozen

There is one afternoon in December when I mix four batches of cookie dough, portion all of it, and put it in the freezer. For the next three weeks I bake six cookies at a time, on a Tuesday, for no reason. The part that took me years to get right was not the recipe. It was the shape I froze the dough in, the weight of each piece, and knowing that the difference between a good frozen cookie and a bad one is usually two minutes of oven time and one bag with too much air in it.

⚡ Quick Answer: Portion, Tray-Freeze, Bag, Bake Frozen

The method: scoop the dough into balls, set them on a parchment-lined tray in one layer, freeze 1–2 hours until solid, then bag them with the air pressed out. Balls never need thawing.

The time: bake at the recipe's own temperature and add 1–3 minutes for a standard 25–50 g ball. More than that and the edges brown before the middle catches up.

The limit: 8 weeks is the target, 3 months the outer edge. At 0 °F the USDA calls frozen food safe indefinitely, so this number is about taste, not safety.

The one rule that matters: the colder the dough goes in, the thicker the cookie comes out. Freezing is not just storage, it is a tool for stopping spread.

Raw dough is not a snack. Freezing inactivates bacteria without killing them, and flour is a raw product — the FDA and CDC both say no to eating the dough.

Most freezer advice for bakers is written as if the freezer were a cupboard: put the thing in, take the thing out, no change in between. Cookie dough is more interesting than that. Freezing changes the water in it into ice crystals whose size depends on how fast you freeze, it hardens the butter into a solid that has to absorb a great deal of heat before it can flow, and it pauses the leavening chemistry in the middle of its work. All three of those changes show up in the baked cookie. Two of them help you.

This is the dough-only version of the question. If you are freezing cake layers, pie, frosting or already-baked cookies, the timings for those live in how to freeze cake and baked goods, which covers the whole freezer rather than one bowl of dough. Here the scope is narrow on purpose: what happens inside a ball of cookie dough between the mixing bowl and the oven, and the numbers that decide whether it comes out thick or flat.

#1 What the Freezer Does That Heat Cannot Undo

Cookie dough is roughly 15 to 20 percent water. Some of it is free water from the egg, some is bound in the flour, and a meaningful share arrives inside the butter itself — USDA lists butter at about 16 percent water, so a stick quietly contributes around 18 g of liquid to the bowl. At 0 °F all of that water freezes, and the size of the crystals it forms depends on how quickly it freezes.

The USDA's freezing guidance is blunt about why this matters: rapid freezing prevents large ice crystals from forming because the molecules do not have time to organise, while slow freezing creates large, disruptive crystals that damage structure when the food thaws. Their rule of thumb is that a food two inches thick should freeze completely in about two hours, and their instruction is to spread packages out in a single layer rather than stacking them. That instruction was written for steaks. It is also the exact reason a tray of portioned dough balls freezes better than a bag of the same dough.

Butter behaves differently from water here. Butterfat is solid at 0 °F, and unlike water it does not expand into jagged crystals; it simply becomes hard. That hardness is the useful part. To spread, a cookie needs its fat to liquefy, and butter melts somewhere around 90 to 95 °F. A frozen ball starts that journey roughly ninety degrees below the melting point, which buys the flour and egg proteins time to set the edges before the middle can flow.

Leavening is the third system, and it is the one most often described wrongly. Baking soda and baking powder produce gas when they meet moisture, and double-acting powder produces a second, larger push when it meets heat. Freezing does not reset either reaction. The moisture-driven stage has already happened in the mixing bowl; the heat-driven stage is paused, not spent, and resumes when the dough hits the oven. What freezing does do is buy time: the dough sits in a state where nothing further can happen, which is why a batch frozen in November bakes in December with the lift of a fresh one, and why a bag forgotten until March does not.

Now the part heat cannot undo. Freezing does not stop enzymes or oxygen, only slows them, and the USDA notes that foods frozen too long can develop a rancid or off odour. In cookie dough the fat is the vulnerable component: butter takes up freezer smells readily and its surface dries out as ice migrates away from it. That is freezer burn, which the USDA describes as greyish-brown leathery patches caused by air contacting the surface, and which is entirely a quality problem rather than a safety one. It is also why the bag matters as much as the temperature.

#2 Portion First: The Arithmetic Nobody Does

The single most common freezer mistake is freezing the dough as one mass. A bag of soft dough freezes into a single lump, and portioning it later means waiting for the whole thing to soften, which warms it unevenly and defeats the point of having frozen it. Portion while it is still soft, when a scoop works.

Decide the ball weight before you start, because it sets the bake time, the number of cookies per sheet, and the number of cookies in the bag. The arithmetic is one division. Weigh the bowl of dough, subtract the bowl, and you have the total. Then either pick a count and divide, or pick a weight and divide.

As a starting point, a standard drop-cookie batch built on 2¼ cups (270 g) of flour plus a cup of butter, two sugars, two eggs and two cups of chips weighs about 1.25 kg. That single number answers the usual question: 48 cookies at 26 g, 36 at 35 g, 25 at 50 g, or 12 bakery-sized ones at 100 g. Weigh your own bowl once and you will never guess again, and if you are scaling a recipe up to fill the freezer, run it through the recipe scaler first so the totals are already in grams.

One warning about scoops and spoons: dough density is not constant, so a two-tablespoon scoop does not always weigh the same. King Arthur's sourdough discard cookie dough runs about 50 g for a two-tablespoon portion, which is denser than a plain chocolate chip dough at the same volume. If you want repeatable results across recipes, weigh the first ball on a scale and trust that number, not the scoop size.

#3 Three Shapes, Three Jobs

How you freeze the dough decides how you bake it, and the three shapes are not interchangeable.

ShapeBest forHow to freeze itHow to bake it
Portioned ballsDrop cookies: chocolate chip, oatmeal, peanut butterScoop onto a tray in one layer, freeze solid, then bagStraight from frozen, no thaw, +1–3 min
LogSlice-and-bake: shortbread, icebox, pinwheelsRoll to a 1½–2 inch cylinder, chill firm, wrap tight, freeze15 min on the counter, slice ¼ inch, +0–1 min
Flat sheetCut-outs: sugar cookies, gingerbreadRoll between two sheets of parchment, freeze flat, cut frozenStraight from frozen, +1–2 min

The log is the shape people get wrong most often, in both directions. Too soft and the knife squashes it into an oval; too hard and the slices shatter into shards. Fifteen minutes at room temperature is the window: the dough should feel firm enough to hold its shape under its own weight and soft enough that a thin knife passes through without pressure. Slice at a quarter inch for a crisp shortbread, a half inch for something softer and thicker, and rotate the log a quarter turn every few slices so it does not flatten on one side.

Flat sheets have their own advantage: because the dough is already at its final thickness, the freezer does the work of a very long chill, and cut-outs hold their edges through the bake. Roll between parchment, stack the sheets flat with a piece of cardboard if your freezer shelves are wire, and cut with the dough still cold. If you cut first and freeze the shapes, you get the same result with less counter space; either works, but cutting frozen dough gives cleaner edges.

#4 The Tray Hour, and Why Bagging Soft Dough Fails

Every source that has tested this says the same thing, and it is the step people skip when they are in a hurry: freeze the portions on a tray first, then bag them.

The USDA's reasoning applies exactly. Freezing is fastest when cold air can reach every surface, which is why they say to spread packages in one layer and stack them only after they are frozen solid. A bag of touching dough balls insulates itself, so the middle of the bag freezes slowly and forms the large crystals the USDA warns about. The parts that touch also freeze together into a single mass, and you lose the ability to take out six instead of forty.

One hour is usually enough for balls under 50 g; ninety minutes to two hours is safer for larger ones, and the test is simple. Pick one up. If it is rigid all the way through and does not deform when pressed, it is done. Then bag it, press the air out from the bottom up, seal, and put the sealed bag inside a second one if it is going to be there more than a few weeks. Double-bagging is not fussiness; it is the cheapest freezer-burn insurance there is, because burn comes from air and moisture reaching the surface, and the inner bag is where pinholes happen.

Label before it goes in. Date, ball weight, oven temperature, minutes, and the recipe name. In six weeks you will not remember whether that bag is the brown butter batch or the one with the extra salt, and the label costs ten seconds.

#5 How Long It Actually Keeps

This is where most guides quote a single number and move on. The single number is misleading, because there are three different clocks running and only one of them is about safety.

StorageTimeWhat sets the limit
Counter, raw dough2 hoursFood safety. One hour above 90 °F
Fridge, raw dough3–4 daysRaw egg, the same limit as eggs out of the shell
Freezer, portioned dough8 weeks target, 3 months outerFat oxidising and surface drying, not bacteria
Freezer, baked cookies2–3 monthsCrumb texture, and how well the bag was sealed

The safety clock stops at 0 °F. The USDA is categorical that food stored constantly at zero stays safe indefinitely and that only quality suffers with long storage, and the same page describes storage times as being for quality only. So nothing in your freezer goes bad at a date; it declines.

The quality clock is shorter than the internet claims. King Arthur's own recipe notes suggest freezing dough for up to two months, and their Thanksgiving planning post says dough will last several months in the freezer — a range, not a promise, which is the honest way to state it. Eight weeks is where I would draw the line, because it is long enough to be useful and short enough that the butter has not yet picked up the smell of everything else in there. Past three months, the cookies still bake, but the fat tastes flat and the edges come out drier, which is the sublimation problem showing up on the plate.

If you are baking from an older bag, pull the cookies a minute early rather than a minute late. Dough that has been in for months has lost surface water, so it browns faster at the edge even though the middle is still cold.

#6 Baking From Frozen: The Time Table

The rule from the sources is consistent: same oven temperature, extra minutes. King Arthur's guidance is an extra minute or a few, and their sourdough cookie tip is specific at two additional minutes. That range holds for a normal scoop. It does not hold for a hundred-gram ball, because heat has further to travel and the oven has more mass to warm.

Ball weightAdd to the recipe timeWhat to watch
20–26 g+1 to 2 minThese are small enough to overcook in a minute
30–35 g+2 to 3 minThe standard case; start at +2
45–55 g+2 to 3 minSame as above; the centre hides the cold longer
90–110 g+4 to 6 minBeyond the tested range, so bake a test one

Two practical rules sit on top of the table. First, space them two inches apart, more than you would for fresh dough, because the sheet pan is cold when they go on and the first minute of spread happens before the oven recovers. Second, bake one test cookie from every new bag. It costs one cookie and it tells you, in twelve minutes, whether this bag needs two minutes or four — and whether the dough was frozen solid or only chilled.

The visual cue beats the timer in every case. Pull them when the edges are set and golden and the centre still looks slightly underdone, because a cookie keeps cooking on the hot sheet for several minutes after it leaves the oven. A frozen cookie looks more underdone at that moment than a fresh one does, and it firms up the same way.

#7 Same Oven, or Twenty-Five Degrees Lower?

You will find both answers, sometimes in the same article. Keeping the recipe temperature and adding time is what King Arthur recommends, and it is the answer for anything up to about 50 g. Dropping the oven 25 °F and adding more time is a technique for very large cookies, and it comes with a cost that is rarely mentioned.

Here is the trade. A frozen ball entering a hot oven has a cold core, so the outside is exposed to full heat while the inside is still catching up. At the recipe temperature, the outside browns on schedule and the extra minutes go into the middle. If you lower the oven, the outside browns more slowly — good for a 100 g cookie whose surface would otherwise scorch — but the bake gets longer, and a longer bake at any temperature means more time for the fat to melt and the cookie to spread. For a normal-sized ball, lowering the temperature trades the thickness you were trying to gain for a slightly more even crumb, which is usually a bad trade.

So: recipe temperature plus one to three minutes for standard balls; 25 °F lower plus four to six extra minutes only for bakery-sized ones, and only after a test cookie says the surface is browning too fast. If you are working in Celsius, or your recipe is written for a fan oven, convert before you start rather than adjusting mid-bake — the oven temperature conversion chart has the gas marks and the fan-oven offset.

#8 Why Frozen Dough Spreads Less, and How to Use It

This is the part that turns freezing from storage into technique. A cookie spreads when its fat melts faster than its structure sets. Butter is fully liquid around 90 to 95 °F, and a room-temperature dough ball is already part of the way there before it meets the oven. A frozen one is not, so the edge sets first, the middle stays put for another minute or two, and the finished cookie is taller with a softer centre.

That is why bakeries bake from frozen on purpose, and why the same trick works on dough you are baking today. If your cookies habitually spread into lace, portion the batch and put the balls in the freezer for 30 minutes before baking. You do not need the full freeze; you need the fat to be solid when the pan goes in.

The opposite problem is worth knowing about too. If your dough was frozen properly and your cookies still spread flat, the freezer is not the culprit — look at butter temperature at mixing, flour measurement, sugar ratio and oven calibration, all of which are covered in why cookies spread too much. Freezing cannot rescue a dough that was too warm, too wet or too sweet going in; it can only delay the consequence.

#9 What Should Never Go in the Bag

Freezing flatters fat-and-flour doughs and punishes everything built on air or water. The failures are predictable once you know what each one depends on.

DoughWhat happensInstead
Yeasted doughSome yeast dies in the cold, so it never rises as well after freezingBake fresh, or freeze the baked roll
Cake-like batters: madeleines, tuiles, spongeHigh water content makes large crystals that wreck the delicate crumbBake and freeze the finished cake
Meringue-topped or meringue-foldedThe foam collapses and weeps on thawFreeze the base, top it after baking
Dough with fresh fruitFruit water freezes into shards and releases on bakingDried fruit, or add fresh fruit at the last minute
Cream cheese or custard fillingsThe emulsion breaks and thaws grainyFreeze the dough, fill after baking

The yeasted dough entry is the one that surprises people, and it comes straight from King Arthur's Baker's Hotline rather than from theory: their position is that freezing yeasted doughs is a last resort because some of the yeast will inevitably die in the cold, so the dough will never rise as well as one baked the day it was made. Cookie dough has no such problem, because its lift comes from chemical leaveners and its structure from fat and sugar rather than from a living colony.

#10 Thawing: Three Safe Ways, One Wrong One

The USDA lists exactly three safe ways to thaw food: in the refrigerator, in cold water, or in the microwave. It also rules out the method most people use, which is leaving it on the kitchen counter.

For cookie dough you rarely need any of them, because balls bake from frozen and logs need only fifteen minutes of firming, not thawing. When you do need a full thaw — a disc of dough for rolling out, say — the refrigerator is the right answer: overnight, in the bag, on the bottom shelf. Cold-water thawing works for a sealed bag but is pointless here, and the microwave is a bad idea for dough specifically, because it heats unevenly and can melt the butter in patches, which shows up later as a greasy cookie with an odd structure.

The counter is wrong for two independent reasons. The safety one is that the outside of the dough spends hours in the range where bacteria multiply while the inside is still frozen, which is exactly the condition the USDA's thawing rules exist to prevent. The quality one is condensation: a cold ball in warm humid air collects water on its surface, that water dissolves sugar, and a wet sugary surface spreads further and browns faster than a dry one.

Refreezing is allowed once if the dough was thawed in the refrigerator — the USDA is explicit that this is safe, with some quality loss. What you should not do is cycle it repeatedly, because each freeze-thaw pass grows the ice crystals that damage structure. If you thawed too much, bake it rather than re-freezing it a second time.

#11 The Ingredient People Forget Is Raw

Everyone knows raw egg is a risk. Almost nobody applies the same logic to flour, and the FDA has been trying to change that for years. Their consumer guidance is that most flour is a raw food which has not been treated to kill bacteria, that grinding and bleaching do not kill anything, and that cooking and baking are what make it safe. A 2019 FDA survey found that most people in the U.S. did not consider raw flour risky to eat.

The CDC is more specific: it has investigated outbreaks linked to raw flour or cake mix in 2016, 2019, 2021 and 2023, with Salmonella and E. coli as the culprits, and its advice is not to eat raw dough or batter at all — including the dough for cookies, brownies, cake, pie crust, pizza, tortillas, biscuits and pancakes. It also warns against letting children play with raw dough, and against stirring homemade dough into ice cream or a milkshake. The cookie dough ice cream in the shop is made with treated flour and pasteurised egg, which is a different product from yours.

Freezing does not change this. The USDA's phrasing is that freezing to 0 °F inactivates microbes, and that once thawed they can become active again and multiply. Inactivation is not destruction, so a frozen ball of dough is still raw food and still needs to go in the oven. That is the whole reason the phrase "edible cookie dough" exists as a separate category: heat-treated flour, pasteurised egg, and a recipe built to be eaten cold.

#12 Label It Like the Recipe Won't Exist Later

Six weeks is long enough to forget everything. The bag needs five things written on it in marker: the date it went in, the weight of one ball, the oven temperature, the minutes to add, and what the dough is. That last one matters more than it sounds, because a bag of pale dough could be sugar cookie, shortbread or snickerdoodle, and the three bake differently.

Write the total count too, so you know whether you are looking at 36 cookies or the last eleven. And if you are the sort of person who freezes several batches at once, write the same information on the bag in the same order every time; the second bag is where labelling habits either hold or collapse.

Two rows carry most of the weight. The lump row is solved entirely by the tray hour, which is the one step worth being strict about. The dark-edge row is solved by trusting the visual cue over the clock, because a frozen cookie at the right moment looks less done than a fresh one does, and the temptation is always to leave it in for another three minutes.

One last thing worth saying plainly: the freezer is a tool for making fresh cookies on a weekday, not a museum. Dough that goes in at peak quality comes out close to it, and dough that was mixed warm, over-handled or under-floured goes in already carrying the problem. Freeze it cold, freeze it in the shape you want to bake, and label it — the rest is a two-minute adjustment you will make once per bag.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you freeze cookie dough?

Yes, and it is the best use of a freezer in a baking kitchen. Portion the dough into balls first, freeze them on a tray in a single layer for one to two hours until solid, then move them into a bag with the air pressed out. Bake straight from frozen at the recipe's temperature, adding one to three extra minutes for a standard scoop. Freezing does not kill bacteria, so the dough is still raw food until it is baked.

How long does frozen cookie dough last?

Eight weeks is the target, and three months is the outer edge for quality. The USDA says food held at 0 °F stays safe indefinitely and only quality declines, so the limit here is not safety but the fat slowly oxidising and the surface drying out. King Arthur's own recipes say two months; their blog says several months. Frozen dough does not go bad at a date, it just bakes drier and tastes flatter the longer it sits.

Do you have to thaw cookie dough before baking?

Not for portioned balls, which should go into the oven frozen, because a cold core is what keeps the cookie thick. A frozen log is different: it needs fifteen minutes at room temperature, just long enough for a sharp knife to pass through, or the slices squash and shatter. Never thaw dough on the counter to soften it, since that is the one thawing method the USDA rules out on safety grounds.

Why do my cookies spread more after freezing?

Usually the dough was not frozen solid when it went in, or it sat out long enough to thaw, or condensation from the bag wetted the surface and dissolved sugar there. Cold dough spreads less than fresh dough, not more, so spreading after freezing points to a warm start, an oven that was not fully preheated, or balls placed closer than two inches apart on a cool sheet pan.

Can you eat frozen cookie dough?

No. Freezing to 0 °F inactivates microbes but does not kill them, and most flour is a raw agricultural product that has not been treated to kill Salmonella or E. coli. The FDA and CDC have investigated flour-linked outbreaks in 2016, 2019, 2021 and 2023. Commercial edible dough is made with heat-treated flour and pasteurised eggs, which is a different product from the dough in your freezer.

🍪 The Freezer Check, In Six Questions

Answer these before the bag goes in. Each one has a number attached, and the first that fails is the one that will show up in the cookie.

  1. Is it portioned? Balls at a known weight, not one lump. Weigh the first one and trust that number.
  2. Did it sit on a tray in one layer? One to two hours until rigid, never stacked.
  3. Is the air out, and is it double-bagged? Burn comes from air and moisture, and the inner bag is where pinholes happen.
  4. Does the label carry five things? Date, ball weight, oven temperature, minutes, dough name.
  5. Is the freezer at 0 °F? Not “cold”. Zero is where safety stops being the question.
  6. Will it be baked within 8 weeks? Three months is the edge, and past it the fat goes flat before the dough goes bad.

If all six pass: you have a bag that bakes like fresh dough on a Tuesday, and the only remaining variable is the two minutes you add at the oven.

If you take one thing from this article: portion before you freeze, on a tray, in one layer. The tray hour is the difference between a bag of loose balls and a solid lump, and it is the only step that cannot be fixed later.

Second thing: bake from frozen at the recipe's temperature and add 2 minutes for a standard scoop. Cold dough spreads less, so the thickness you were chasing is already in the freezer.

Third, the limit is 8 weeks for quality and longer for safety. The freezer keeps dough safe far longer than it keeps it good, which is the opposite of what most people assume.

🛒 What makes the difference here

A scoop gives you the same weight every time, which is what makes the bake-time table work at all; a scale tells you the real weight when the dough is denser than the scoop assumes; and freezer bags that actually seal are the difference between eight weeks and freezer burn.
