Dark Pan vs Light Pan: The 25 °F Rule in Celsius

The first time I baked a cake in a black tin, I did what the internet said and turned the oven down 25 degrees. The cake came out fine. The second time I did the same thing with a silver tin, because I had misremembered which tin was which, and it also came out fine, which told me the rule had stopped meaning anything to me. What fixed that was not a better rule. It was understanding that a dark pan does not make the oven hotter, it makes the metal hotter, and that the number everyone quotes is a Fahrenheit interval that has been translated into Celsius badly.

⚡ Quick Answer: Dark Pan, 25 °F Less — Which Is 14 °C

The rule: a recipe written for light metal, baked in a black or very dark pan, goes in at 25 °F lower — 350 °F becomes 325 °F — and gets checked 5–10 minutes early.

The Celsius: 25 °F is 13.9 °C, so 350 → 325 °F is 177 → 163 °C. The "10 °C" printed on some boxes is wrong by about 7 °F.

The rule does not apply to: bright silver aluminium, which reflects roughly 90% of the radiant heat and bakes exactly as the recipe intends.

The exception: pie, pizza and focaccia want the dark pan's fast bottom. Lowering the oven there removes the reason you reached for the pan.

Never add them up: dark pan, fan oven and glass pan each carry a 25 °F adjustment. Applying all three gives 275 °F where the recipe said 350 °F.

Celsius bakers have a second problem: a temperature difference converts with 5/9 alone, while an absolute temperature also carries the 32-degree offset. Mixing the two is where the 10 °C came from.

The advice is everywhere and it is usually written in one line: if you use a dark pan, reduce the oven temperature by 25 °F. It is correct often enough to survive, and vague enough to be applied in situations where it does nothing, or where it actively undoes what you were trying to achieve. The sources behind it are narrower than the way it gets repeated, and one of them is a manufacturer whose own pans are the dark ones in question.

This article is about one variable inside the oven: the colour and finish of the metal, and the single number that compensates for it. The wider question of whether your oven is telling the truth belongs in how to test your oven temperature, and the arithmetic of converting any temperature in either direction belongs in the oven temperature conversion chart. Here the scope stays narrow: what the coating does, which pans count, what the number should be in Celsius, and when the right answer is to leave the dial alone.

#1 Two Different Temperatures, One Dial

Every dark-pan argument starts with a confusion between two numbers that both get called "the oven temperature". The first is the air temperature, which is what the dial on the front sets and what a thermometer clipped to the middle rack reads. The second is the temperature of the metal the batter is sitting in contact with. They are not the same, and only one of them is affected by the colour of the pan.

Heat reaches a pan through two routes. Air moving past it, which is convection, and infrared radiation arriving from the oven walls and the heating element, which is radiation. Convection does not care what colour the pan is: a black surface and a white one sitting in the same moving air end up at the same temperature. Radiation cares a great deal. A shiny surface reflects most of the infrared energy that arrives and absorbs little of it; a dark matte surface absorbs most of it and reflects almost none. The absorbed energy becomes heat in the metal, and the metal passes it straight into the batter touching it, by conduction.

That is the whole mechanism, and it explains why the effect is strongest exactly where the damage shows: the base and the sides. The centre of a cake is heated mostly by the air around it and by conduction through the batter, which is slow. The outer millimetre is in direct contact with a surface that has been collecting radiant energy for twenty minutes. In a black tin, that outer layer browns and sets while the middle is still climbing, which is the domed top and cracked crust described in why cakes sink in the middle — the same physics, arriving at the opposite failure.

Two practical consequences follow immediately. First, the effect depends on how much of the oven's heat arrives as radiation, which varies by oven type: an electric oven with exposed elements, or a gas oven with a hot floor, delivers more of it than a well-shielded modern oven, and a fan-assisted oven stirs the air and makes the air temperature matter more. Second, the effect depends on the thickness of the metal. A thin disposable tin heats and cools almost instantly and spikes hard; a heavy anodised pan has enough mass to smooth the same energy out. Colour is a proxy for how a pan behaves, not a complete description of it.

#2 What a Black Coating Absorbs

The quantity that describes this is emissivity, written as ε, and by Kirchhoff's law a surface absorbs at the same wavelengths and in the same proportion as it emits. Published tables give the number for the surfaces that end up in a kitchen, and the range between them is much wider than most baking advice implies.

SurfaceEmissivity εRadiant heat absorbed
Aluminium, highly polished0.039–0.0574–6%
Aluminium, commercial sheet0.099%
Aluminium, heavily oxidised0.20–0.3120–31%
Aluminium, anodised0.60–0.9560–95%

Read the first and last rows against each other and the rule stops looking like folklore. A commercial aluminium sheet reflects about nine units of radiant heat out of every ten that reach it. An anodised or black-coated pan absorbs somewhere between six and nine and a half of those ten, and the difference is spent on the underside of your cake. Nothing about the air in the oven has changed; the surface the batter is touching has changed by a factor of ten.

The same number explains why the recommendation is a fixed 25 °F rather than something calculated from the emissivity gap. Narrowing a tenfold difference in absorptivity to a single temperature offset is a rough correction by design. It is a starting point that gets the bottom of the bake out of trouble, not a value derived from the physics. Manufacturers state it as a rule because a rule is what a home baker can act on.

#3 Who Actually Says 25 °F, and How They Say It

Three statements carry the rule, and the wording of each matters more than the number.

Nordic Ware, whose Bundt pans and commercial lines are dark-coated, states it twice in its own care instructions: for dark-coloured pans, reduce the recommended oven temperature by 25 °F. Their commercial use-and-care page gives the same figure, and their Bundt guide repeats it for pans with a black interior coating. This is a manufacturer describing the behaviour of the product it sells, which makes it the most direct evidence available.

King Arthur Baking states it conditionally, and this is the part that gets lost in repetition. Their pan guide recommends reducing the oven 25 °F for a glass pan, and separately says that if you own a dark-coloured non-stick cookie sheet that tends to burn the bottoms of your cookies, reduce the temperature by 25 °F. Their preferred cookie sheet is light-coloured and shiny, precisely so that the reduction is unnecessary. The same guide describes dark metal pie pans as browning the crust more quickly, and calls that a plus.

King Arthur's gluten-free brownie formula puts both halves into one recipe line, and it is the cleanest test case on the internet: preheat to 350 °F, or 325 °F if using a glass or dark metal pan, then bake 30 to 35 minutes in a dark pan and 35 to 40 minutes in a light one.

Put together, the rule reads: a dark pan is a property of the pan, not a rule about your oven. If the pan is light, the recipe stands as written. If the pan is dark and the recipe was written for light metal, subtract 25 °F and shorten the check. If the bake is one where a fast bottom is the goal, the dark pan is doing its job and the temperature should not be touched.

#4 25 °F Is 14 °C, Not 10 °C

Here is where a large share of Celsius bakers quietly bake 7 °F hot. Nordic Ware prints the drop as "25 °F (10 °C)", and their commercial page prints the same. Ten Celsius is eighteen Fahrenheit. Follow the Celsius number with a Fahrenheit recipe and you land at 167 °C instead of 163 °C for a 350 °F bake.

The error comes from mixing two different conversions. Converting an absolute temperature between scales uses both the 5/9 factor and the 32-degree offset: 350 °F is 177 °C, and 325 °F is 163 °C. Converting a temperature difference uses the factor alone, because the offset cancels: 25 °F × 5/9 = 13.9 °C. Whoever drafted the label appears to have converted the absolute temperatures, seen a gap of about 14, and then written a rounder, smaller number that felt safer.

The gap is small enough that most bakes survive it, and large enough to matter in a delicate one: a 4 °C overshoot on a sponge or a cheesecake is the difference between a set centre and a cracked top. It is also a good reason to distrust any source that writes "25 degrees Fahrenheit (or Celsius)", which appears in print more often than it should and implies a drop of 45 °F.

#5 Which Pans Count as Dark

The rule is quoted far more often than it is defined, and the definition is the useful part. These are the pans people actually own, in the order the rule should be applied to them.

Two rows are worth arguing about. The anodised row is the one where bakers most often over-correct: anodised aluminium is dark, but it is also thick and an excellent conductor, so it behaves less aggressively than a thin black non-stick tin with the same colour. If your dark pan is heavy and your cake comes out evenly, the 25 °F may be unnecessary — and the way to know is to bake one at the recipe temperature and look at the base colour, not the top. The silicone row is the opposite case: no colour problem at all, and a completely different one, because silicone barely reaches the temperature that browns.

#7 What 25 °F Does to the Bake Time

The widespread assumption is that lowering the oven cancels the dark pan's speed, so the bake takes the same time at a lower temperature. The brownie formula says otherwise, and it is worth sitting with the numbers because they are the only measured pair in the sources.

PanOvenBake timeResult
Light metal350 °F (177 °C)35–40 minThe reference bake
Dark metal325 °F (163 °C)30–35 minStill five minutes faster than light
Glass325 °F (163 °C)+5 min on the light timeSlow conductor, so it finishes late

At 25 °F lower, the dark pan is still five minutes quicker than the light one at full temperature. So the reduction is not a compensation that equalises the two pans; it is a partial correction that stops the base scorching while leaving some of the speed advantage in place. That is why the advice always comes with "start checking early" attached, and why treating the temperature drop as sufficient on its own is the most common way to end up with a dry edge.

Glass runs the other way, and it is the neatest demonstration that the two 25 °F rules are different things. Glass conducts heat slowly, so it takes longer to get going, and then holds heat, so the base keeps cooking from stored energy once the oven is off. The same 25 °F drop is applied, and the time goes up rather than down. Anyone who learned the rule as "dark pan, faster bake, lower temperature" has learned it as one rule when it is two.

#8 Where Lowering the Temperature Is the Wrong Move

Four bakes ignore the rule, and in three of them the dark pan is the reason to pick the pan in the first place.

Pie. A pale, soggy bottom crust is the standard failure, and dark metal is the fix: King Arthur notes that dark metal pie pans become hotter and brown the crust more quickly, which is exactly what a blind-baked shell needs. Dropping 25 °F fights the property you bought. Bake the pie as written and watch the rim instead, shielding it with foil if the edge darkens before the base sets. The ratio side of pastry lives in pie crust ratio; the pan side is this.

Pizza, focaccia and roasted vegetables. Same logic at a higher temperature. A dark sheet at 450 °F is how you get a crisp base in a domestic oven, and a 25 °F reduction is the difference between a crust that snaps and one that bends.

Cookies. This is the conditional one. A light shiny sheet is the easier fix, and King Arthur recommends it for exactly this reason: it keeps the bottoms from browning too much. If you already own a dark non-stick sheet and it burns the bottoms, their advice is to drop 25 °F. If it does not burn the bottoms, do not drop it — a lower oven makes the cookies paler, and a longer bake at a lower temperature gives the fat more time to melt before the structure sets, which tends to spread them. Shortening the time is the better lever, and the spread question has its own article in why cookies spread too much.

Bread at 450 °F. The base is the first thing to scorch and the last thing you can see. Either bake on parchment, which separates the dough from the metal by a millimetre of air and paper, or drop to 425 °F and add five minutes. The dark pan is still useful here, because a dark tin gives a loaf a thicker bottom crust than a pale one at the same temperature, which is the outcome most home bakers want from a tin loaf.

#9 Do Not Stack Three Different 25 °Fs

There are at least three adjustments that all sound like the same advice, and adding them together is the most expensive mistake in this article. A 350 °F recipe in a dark pan in a fan oven, corrected by all three, goes in at 275 °F, which is 91 °F below where it belongs and roughly the temperature for drying fruit.

AdjustmentAmountWhat it is correctingStack?
Dark pan−25 °FRadiant heat absorbed by the metalOnly against a light-metal recipe
Fan / convection−25 °FMoving air transferring heat fasterOnly if the recipe is written for still air
Glass pan−25 °FSlow conduction, then stored heatIt is not also a dark pan — never both
High altitude−15 to −25 °FLower air pressure, faster risingGenuinely additive with the others

My rule is to apply the largest single correction and check early, rather than sum them. Dark pan plus fan oven is the one pair where plenty of bakers do stack 50 °F and get away with it on a sturdy loaf; on a sponge it produces a pale, collapsed cake. If you are going to stack, stack once, bake one test item, and write down what you did, because the second attempt is where the information pays for itself.

#10 If You Cannot Change the Temperature

Sometimes the dial is already at the bottom of its range, or the recipe is one where a lower oven breaks something else. There are four ways to buy the same protection without touching the temperature.

Shorten the time and check early. In the brownie case the dark pan finishes five minutes ahead of the light one even after the drop. Start checking ten minutes before the recipe's earliest time and trust the look of the base over the clock.

Put parchment between the batter and the metal. One sheet of parchment interrupts the contact and the radiant coupling at the base, which is where the dark pan does its damage. It does not fix the sides, but the sides brown late.

Move the rack up one position. The bottom of a domestic oven is the hottest radiant surface in it. A rack in the lower third puts the pan straight in the path of that energy; the middle rack is the position most recipes assume, and it is also the position most likely to be wrong in an oven with a hidden element at the base.

Insulate the outside. Baking strips wrapped around a dark cake tin slow the heat at the rim, which is the same job the 25 °F does, targeted at the one place the cake fails. They cost a few pounds and they work on any tin, dark or light.

What none of these do is change the base temperature, so if the base is already scorching at the recipe setting, the pan is the thing to change. That is worth saying plainly because the temptation is always to keep adjusting the oven instead of buying a pale tin.

#11 Glass Pans Are a Separate Rule

Glass gets the same 25 °F for the opposite reason, and conflating them is how bakers end up applying the correction twice or not at all. Glass is a poor conductor: it heats slowly, so the batter near the wall lags at the start, and it retains heat, so the same wall keeps delivering energy after the centre has caught up. The failure is a hard, overdone rim on a cake whose middle is just barely set.

King Arthur's recommendation is to reduce the oven 25 °F for glass, and their brownie formula applies it with a time penalty rather than a saving. So the pattern is: dark metal, lower and shorter; glass, lower and longer. The one thing both share is that a clear glass pan lets you watch the base colour, which is the single most useful signal you have and the reason some bakers keep one.

The other glass trap is the water bath. A cheesecake in a glass springform in a bain-marie is being heated through water at 212 °F, so the pan's radiant behaviour matters much less and the oven setting matters more than usual. That is a case for dropping 25 °F anyway, because the water is already capping the base temperature, and for checking the set rather than the colour. Cheesecake doneness is a wobble question, not a browning one.

The cookie row is the one people argue with, and the argument is usually about the wrong thing: whether a dark sheet is "bad". It is not bad, it is fast. A fast sheet is ideal for a thin, crisp biscuit and wrong for a thick, soft one, and the temperature is a blunt tool for that preference while the time and the chill are precise ones. If you are not sure which you have, bake three cookies at the recipe temperature and look at the undersides before changing anything.

Two rows carry most of the weight. The dark ring is the classic, and it is the one the rule was invented for. The last row is the quiet one: a Celsius baker following the number on the box will not see an obvious failure, just a bake that is consistently a little further along than the recipe intended, which is much harder to diagnose than a burnt base.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to lower the oven temperature for a dark pan?

Only if the pan is genuinely dark — a black or very dark coating, a dark anodised finish, or black carbon steel — and only if the recipe was written for light metal. In that case drop the oven 25 °F (14 °C) and start checking five to ten minutes earlier. Bright silver aluminium needs no adjustment at all, because it reflects most of the radiant heat rather than absorbing it. King Arthur is explicit that the cookie-sheet version of this rule applies when a dark non-stick sheet is already burning the bottoms, not as a default.

How much is a 25 °F reduction in Celsius?

13.9 °C, normally rounded to 14 °C. A temperature difference converts with the 5/9 factor only, so 25 °F equals 25 × 5/9 = 13.9 °C. This is separate from converting an absolute temperature, where the 32-degree offset comes in: 350 °F is 177 °C and 325 °F is 163 °C, and the gap between them is 14 °C, not 10.

Why do some sources say 25 °F is 10 °C?

Because the figure was written for Fahrenheit and the Celsius gloss was added afterwards without being recalculated. Nordic Ware's own instructions give the drop as 25 °F (10 °C). 10 °C is 18 °F, so a baker following the Celsius number sets an oven roughly 7 °F hotter than intended. The arithmetic is unambiguous: a 25 °F interval is 13.9 °C, and 350 °F to 325 °F is 177 °C to 163 °C.

Should I lower the temperature for a glass pan too?

Yes, by the same 25 °F, but for a different reason. Glass conducts heat slowly and then retains it, so the edges and bottom keep cooking after the centre has caught up. King Arthur recommends reducing the oven 25 °F for glass pans and gives the brownie example directly: 350 °F becomes 325 °F for glass or dark metal, with about five extra minutes for glass. Because the mechanisms differ, the two reductions are never added together — a glass pan is not also a dark pan.

Can I use a dark pan for cakes and cookies?

Yes. Lower the oven 25 °F, check five to ten minutes earlier, and expect the bake to finish sooner rather than later: in King Arthur's brownie formula a dark pan at 325 °F takes 30 to 35 minutes while a light pan at 350 °F takes 35 to 40. For cookies, the drop is the wrong first move — a light shiny sheet is the easier fix, and if you already own a dark non-stick one, keep the recipe temperature and shorten the time unless the bottoms are actually burning. Dark metal is an advantage rather than a problem for pie, pizza and focaccia, where a fast-browning base is the point.

🍰 The Dark Pan Check, in Five Questions

Ask these before the pan goes in. The first one that fails is the one that will show on the plate.

  1. Is the pan actually dark? Black, dark grey or anodised. Bright silver is the reference case and needs nothing.
  2. Was the recipe written for light metal? If it already specifies a dark pan, the author has done the arithmetic for you.
  3. Is the bake one where a fast bottom is wanted? Pie, pizza, focaccia: leave the dial alone.
  4. Have you applied only one correction? Dark pan, fan oven and glass are alternatives, not a sum.
  5. Is your Celsius number 14, not 10? 350 °F to 325 °F is 177 °C to 163 °C, and the box is wrong.

If all five pass: you are 25 °F lower and ten minutes early, which is the whole of the rule and none of the folklore.

If you take one thing from this article: the colour of the pan changes the temperature of the metal, not the temperature of the oven. That is why the correction is a fixed 25 °F rather than something measured.

Second thing: 25 °F is 13.9 °C. Subtract 14, not 10, and if you are converting an absolute temperature first, convert that correctly and only then subtract the interval.

Third, the rule is conditional. It exists for a dark pan doing a light pan's job. Where the fast bottom is the goal — pie, pizza, a tin loaf — the pan is already doing what you asked of it.

🛒 What makes the difference here

A light aluminium sheet removes the problem instead of compensating for it; an oven thermometer tells you whether the 25 °F you dialled is the 25 °F the oven delivered; and parchment interrupts the radiant contact at the base, which is where a dark pan does its damage.
