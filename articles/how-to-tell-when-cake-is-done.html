How to Tell When Your Cake Is Done — 5 Reliable Methods (for Every Cake Type)

Toothpick came out clean but your cake is still raw inside? You're not crazy — the toothpick test lies. Here are 5 doneness checks ranked from most to least reliable, the correct internal temperature for every cake type, and what to do when you pull a cake too early.

⚡ Quick Answer: How to Know When Cake Is Done

A cake is done when its internal center temperature reaches:

Cake TypeDone TempBest Test
Butter / pound / layer cakes200–210°F (93–99°C)Thermometer + spring-back
Sponge / chiffon / angel food195–205°F (90–96°C)Thermometer + spring-back
Chocolate cake (with chips)200–210°F (93–99°C)Thermometer (don't trust toothpick)
Banana / zucchini / carrot cake200–210°F (93–99°C)Thermometer + toothpick (moist crumbs OK)
Cheesecake150°F (65°C)Thermometer + jiggle test
Flourless chocolate torte185–200°F (85–93°C)Thermometer + visual

Use at least two tests together. If they disagree, the thermometer wins. For large cakes, pull 5°F below target — carryover heat will close the gap.

I pulled a chocolate cake out of the oven last winter. Toothpick came out clean — four different spots, all dry. I set it on the rack, felt good about myself, went to level the dome 20 minutes later. The middle was soup. Raw batter all the way through the center three inches.

The toothpick had gone through a melted chocolate chip pocket. Clean toothpick, raw cake. I'd been baking for years and still got faked out by the one test every recipe tells you to trust.

That was the bake that made me stop relying on any single doneness check. Since then I've tested every method against a thermometer and found that some of the most-quoted tests are borderline useless for certain cakes. Here's what actually works, ranked by reliability — not tradition.

#1 The Thermometer Test — Most Reliable (Use This First)

Every professional bakery — every single one — uses a probe thermometer for cakes. Not because they're fancy. Because it removes the guesswork. Cake crumb sets at a specific internal temperature. If that temperature is hit at the center, the cake is done. No debate.

The exact numbers for every cake type:

  • Butter cakes, pound cakes, layer cakes: 200–210°F (93–99°C). Pull at 200°F for slightly fudgy center; 208–210°F for a fully-set crumb.
  • Sponge cakes, chiffon cakes, angel food: 195–205°F (90–96°C). These rely on egg foam structure and dry out fast above 210°F.
  • Chocolate cake: Same 200–210°F range, but probe carefully. A melted chocolate chip reads hotter than the batter around it. Take three readings and trust the lowest number — the coolest spot in the center is your true doneness.
  • Cheesecake: 150°F (65°C) in the center. It will still jiggle — that's correct. Cheesecake carries over 10–15°F as it cools. If you bake to 170°F, you've overbaked and the top will crack.   • Flourless chocolate cake / torte: 185–200°F (85–93°C). Dense and custard-like; carryover does more work here than in butter cakes. Pull at the lower end.

How to probe correctly (most people get this wrong):

  • Insert at a slight angle, not straight down. Straight down puts the tip near the pan bottom — the hottest spot. Angle in from the side so the tip sits dead center.
  • Count to 5. Instant-read thermometers need 3–5 seconds to stabilize. Jab-in-glance-pull-out in under a second reads the outer crumb the probe passed through, not the center.
  • Withdraw slowly and watch the numbers — the lowest reading you see on the way through is your doneness temperature.

The thermometer doesn't care what pan you used, whether your oven runs hot, or what's mixed into your batter. It gives you a number. If you buy one piece of baking equipment this year: $15 instant-read thermometer > any other gadget. It'll save more cakes than everything else in your drawer combined. #2 The Spring-Back Test (Finger Press Test)

Press the center of the cake gently with the pad of one finger — like checking an avocado. If it springs back immediately and leaves no dent, it's done. If your fingerprint stays visible or the cake feels wobbly underneath, it needs more oven time.

What you're actually testing: the gluten and egg protein network has set enough to hold its shape. Raw batter is fluid — it deforms and stays deformed. Baked crumb is elastic — it pushes back. The spring-back reads that transition directly. It's the same physical change the thermometer detects (protein denaturation), just measured by touch.

Works best for: butter cakes, pound cakes, layer cakes. Less reliable for: very light sponge cakes — the surface is so delicate that even a fully-baked sponge dents under pressure.

The common mistakes:

  • Pressing too shallow — you're only testing the top crust, which sets long before the center. Feel about a quarter-inch below the surface.
  • Pressing too hard — you'll deflate a nearly-done cake. Use the pad of your finger, not the tip.

Pro tip: if you're unsure, press near the edge first (which bakes fastest) to calibrate what "done" feels like, then test the center. If the toothpick test just burned you, the spring-back likely would've caught it — a finger samples a wide disc of crumb, not a single thin column.

#3 Edge Pull-Away — The Oldest Visual Sign a Cake Is Done

When a cake is fully baked, it shrinks slightly and pulls away from the sides of the pan. Look for a hairline gap — 1 to 2 millimeters — between the cake edge and the pan wall. This contraction happens because proteins tighten during the final stage of baking. It's a physical event you can't fake with a chocolate chip.

What to look for:

  • The gap should be even around most of the circumference. A gap on one side only = your oven has a hot spot, not a done cake.
  • Slight browning at the rim where cake meets pan is a companion signal. Pale, doughy-looking edge = not done yet.
  • Dark or nonstick pans: the gap is harder to see. Use the oven light and look from an angle.
  • Parchment-lined pans: no visible gap. Instead, lift the parchment edge — the cake should release cleanly. Sticking and tearing = underbaked.

When this test fails: heavy nonstick spray + modern nonstick pans. The cake slides down the sides during baking instead of climbing and gripping — there's nothing for it to pull away from. If you use cooking spray, skip this test and rely on the thermometer + spring-back instead.

#4 The Sound Test — Optional Cross-Check (Anecdotal, Not Primary)

Note: this method is less scientifically verifiable than the first three. Use it as a bonus confirmation, not a go/no-go decision.

Take the cake out of the oven. Put your ear close. A fully-baked butter cake sometimes makes a faint crackling sound as it starts to cool — like quiet Rice Krispies. What you're hearing: the crumb structure settling and steam escaping through tiny fissures in the starch network. An underbaked cake tends to be silent or makes a wet, bubbling sound.

A pastry chef I worked with swore by this — she could walk past a cooling rack and tell by ear whether a tray was underbaked. It took me about a dozen bakes to hear it reliably; now I can't un-hear it. But I wouldn't bet a cake on it alone. It's most useful for large, dense cakes (10-inch rounds, pound cakes) that hold enough thermal mass to crackle for 30–60 seconds. It's least useful for thin layers under 2 inches and flourless cakes. Treat it as a secondary confirmation that your thermometer and spring-back were right — not as a primary doneness check.

#5 The Toothpick Test — Know When It Lies (and When to Trust It)

I'm not saying throw out the toothpick. I'm saying use it last, after at least two other methods agree, and know the exact situations where it gives false results.

When the toothpick test works:

  • Plain vanilla or white cakes with no mix-ins. Clean crumb, nothing to fake out the pick.
  • Pound cakes and butter cakes with uniform texture.
  • Any cake where you probe three spots in a triangle around the center — and all three give the same result.

When the toothpick test lies (cake still raw inside but toothpick clean):

  • Chocolate cake with chips or chunks. Melted chocolate coats the toothpick in liquid that looks like wet batter. Worse: probe near a chip and the chip's heat bakes the surrounding batter faster — the toothpick near a chip reads done while batter three inches away is still liquid. This is exactly how my chocolate cake disaster happened. For any chocolate cake with mix-ins, skip the toothpick entirely — use the thermometer.
  • Banana, zucchini, carrot, apple cakes. These hold so much fruit moisture that a toothpick often comes out with moist crumbs even at 208°F — fully baked. "A few moist crumbs" is the target here, not perfectly dry. Bone-dry toothpick in banana bread = overbaked and dry.
  • Large cakes (10-inch or bigger). A standard toothpick is 2.5 inches long. The center of a 10-inch cake is ~5 inches from the edge. The toothpick literally can't reach the thermal center. Use a wooden skewer or thin knife — you need at least 4 inches of reach.
  • Cheesecake. Meaningless. It's done based on jiggle and temperature, not crumb set. Clean toothpick in a cheesecake = overbaked soufflé-texture mistake.

How to do the toothpick test properly if you use it: use a wooden toothpick or skewer — wood is porous and batter sticks to it; metal cake testers are too slick and give false-clean results. Insert at a slight angle into the absolute center. Pull out and look at the full length of the pick, not just the tip. Wet, shiny, translucent batter = not done. A few moist crumbs = right zone for most cakes. Completely dry = check the temperature — you might be overbaked.

Why Your Cake Overbakes After You Pull It — Carryover Cooking Explained

A cake doesn't stop baking when it leaves the oven. The residual heat in the pan and outer crumb keeps cooking the center for 5–10 minutes. The bigger the cake, the more carryover matters.

  • Thin 8-inch layer: gains ~3–5°F. Negligible — pull at target temp.
  • 10-inch pound cake / loaf pan over 3 inches deep: gains 8–12°F. Pull 5°F below target — carryover closes the gap. If you pull at 210°F, carryover pushes it past 218°F into dry, crumbly territory.

Pan timing matters too: don't leave cake in the pan more than 10–15 minutes after baking. The pan holds heat and the trapped steam from the bottom keeps cooking the crumb. 30 minutes in the pan = roughly 3–5 extra minutes of effective bake time. Transfer to a wire rack as soon as the cake is cool enough to handle.

Cake Still Raw Inside? Here's What to Do (3 Rescue Options)

You cut into a cake and find a dense, gummy band. Top was set, toothpick was clean-ish, you called it — and you were wrong. Now what?

Option 1: Put it back (only if still warm)

If the cake has been out less than 10 minutes and is still warm: return to the oven at the original temperature for 5–8 minutes. The crumb hasn't fully set and can recover. This works roughly two-thirds of the time.

Option 2: Surgical removal

If it's been out longer than 10 minutes: the starch has retrograded — it collapsed into a solid state and re-baking won't re-inflate it. Slice off the gummy layer and level the cake above it. Lost more than a third of the height? Turn it into a thinner cake with extra filling between layers. Nobody notices. Option 3: Rebrand it

If only the very center is raw: scoop it out with a spoon, fill with frosting or ganache, and serve as "molten center cake." I've done this. It works. Not proud — but it works. Also: cube the baked outer portions, toast at 300°F for 8 minutes, use as trifle or ice cream topping. Cake that can't be a cake can still be a dessert component.

Most importantly: figure out which doneness signal you misread so it doesn't repeat. Trusted the toothpick on a chocolate cake? Next time, thermometer only. Spring-back felt right but center was raw? You were pressing too shallow — test deeper. Write it in your recipe notes. Three months from now you won't remember which method you used.

If you take one thing from this article: buy a $15 instant-read thermometer and use it. Every other test is backup. The thermometer doesn't care what color your cake is, what's mixed in, or whether your oven runs 20 degrees hot. It gives you one number. That number either says "done" or "not done."

Second thing: the toothpick isn't useless, but trusting it alone is how you end up with a clean pick and a raw center. Pair it with the spring-back test — it's the easiest to add — and you'll catch false positives before the cake hits the cooling rack.

Every baker I know has pulled a raw cake. The difference between one who improves and one who doesn't: whether they change their routine after it happens. Next cake — thermometer first, everything else second.
