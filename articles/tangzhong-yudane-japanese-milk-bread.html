Tangzhong (Water Roux) — The 5-Minute Technique That Makes Bread Stay Soft for Days

A spoonful of cooked flour-and-water paste — that's it. Five extra minutes of work, and your sandwich bread stays pillowy for four days instead of going stale overnight. The science of gelatinized starch, step-by-step ratios, and the five mistakes that undo it.

I bought a loaf of Japanese milk bread from a bakery in San Francisco's Japantown about eight years ago. It came in a plain white bag. No branding. The loaf itself looked unremarkable — square, pale golden, the shape of a Pullman pan. I tore off a piece in the car. It pulled apart in wispy, cottony strands. The crumb was so soft it felt like biting into a savory cloud. I ate a third of the loaf before I got home, standing in my kitchen, no butter, no jam, just tearing off piece after piece like I'd never encountered bread before.

The next morning I expected it to be stale — homemade bread always goes stale by morning. This loaf was still soft. Day three: still soft. Day four: slightly drier but still perfectly pleasant as toast. I went back to the bakery the following weekend and asked the baker what kind of sorcery was involved. She laughed and said one word: "tangzhong."

Tangzhong — also called water roux — is a cooked paste of flour and water (or milk) that you add to bread dough. It takes five minutes. It uses flour and liquid you've already measured out for the recipe — no special ingredients, nothing you don't already have. And it single-handedly solves the fundamental sadness of homemade bread: that it goes stale before you can finish the loaf. Here's exactly how it works, why it works, and how to use it in anything you bake.

What Tangzhong Actually Is — and the 60-Second Science

Take a small portion of your recipe's flour. Mix it with five times its weight in liquid. Heat it gently in a pan, stirring, until it thickens into a smooth, pudding-like paste. That's tangzhong.

What just happened: at about 65°C (149°F), starch granules absorb water, swell, and burst — a process called gelatinization. The starch molecules unravel from their tight crystalline structure into a loose, water-trapping gel. This gel holds vastly more water than raw flour can — about five to six times more, by weight. When you mix this gel into bread dough, you're embedding thousands of microscopic water reservoirs throughout the dough.

During baking, the gelatinized starch stays hydrated instead of releasing its water as steam. After baking, as the bread cools and ages, that trapped water stays in the crumb instead of migrating out through the crust (which is the primary mechanism of staling — water loss, not "drying out"). The result: bread that's noticeably softer on day one and stays soft for 3-4 days instead of the standard 1-2.

The technique is not new. Japanese bakers have used yudane (the traditional boiled-water version) for at least a century. Chinese bakers adapted it into tangzhong for mass production of soft, shelf-stable sandwich bread. In Taiwan and Hong Kong, virtually all commercial sandwich bread uses some form of pre-gelatinized starch. The method predates modern food science by decades — bakers knew it worked long before anyone explained why. Now we know: it's starch gelatinization, and it's dramatically effective for about five minutes of work.

Tangzhong vs Yudane — Two Roads to the Same Cloud-Bread

Before we get to the method, a quick distinction that confuses people:

  • Tangzhong (汤种, Chinese method): flour and liquid are cooked together in a pan over low heat, stirred constantly, until the mixture reaches 65°C (149°F) and thickens into a paste. Takes 2-3 minutes. More consistent because you can watch the temperature and stop at exactly the right point.
  • Yudane (湯種, Japanese method — same characters, different reading): boiling liquid is poured over flour and stirred into a paste — no stovetop cooking. The boiling water provides the heat for gelatinization through contact alone. Faster in theory, but the exact temperature is harder to control, and the paste can be lumpy if you don't stir fast enough.

They produce functionally identical results. I use the tangzhong stovetop method because I can see the transformation happen — the mixture goes from milky and thin to thick and pudding-like in about 90 seconds, and I know exactly when to pull it off the heat by watching for the whisk to leave trail marks. If you're more comfortable with the yudane method (pour, stir, done), it works — just make sure the water is genuinely boiling when it hits the flour, not merely hot.

How to Make Tangzhong — Step by Step with Exact Ratios

The ratio is dead simple: 1 part flour to 5 parts liquid, by weight. For a standard loaf:

  • 25g bread flour (about 3 tablespoons) — taken from the total flour in your recipe
  • 125g liquid (about ½ cup) — water or milk, taken from the total liquid in your recipe. Milk makes a richer tangzhong and a more tender crumb. Water works fine. Half milk, half water is my default — best of both.

If you're scaling up for a larger batch, keep the ratio. For a double loaf: 50g flour + 250g liquid. For dinner rolls that use 300g total flour: about 18g flour + 90g liquid. The baker's percentage calculator helps if you're working from a recipe that only gives percentages.

The process, in real time:

  1. Whisk the flour and liquid together in a small saucepan before turning on the heat. No lumps. It should look like thin crepe batter — milky and smooth.
  2. Turn the heat to medium-low. Stir constantly with a whisk or a silicone spatula — a whisk is better for the first minute because it breaks up any lumps instantly. Don't walk away. This goes from liquid to paste fast and it will burn if you ignore it.
  3. At about 60 seconds: the mixture starts to thicken. You'll feel resistance through the whisk. Keep stirring.
  4. At about 90 seconds: the whisk leaves trail marks on the bottom of the pan. The mixture has the consistency of thick pudding or wallpaper paste. If you have a thermometer, it should read 65°C (149°F). If you don't, the trail-mark test is reliable — when the whisk leaves a clear path that doesn't immediately fill in, it's done. Scrape it into a small bowl.
  5. Cool it completely before adding to your dough. Hot tangzhong kills yeast. Spread it on a plate to cool in 10 minutes, or make it the night before, cover with plastic wrap touching the surface (to prevent a skin from forming), and refrigerate. Cold tangzhong straight from the fridge is fine — it'll warm up immediately when mixed into room-temperature dough. Just not hot.

Why 5 Minutes of Work Doubles Your Bread's Shelf Life

Bread goes stale through a process called starch retrogradation. During baking, starch granules absorb water, swell, and gelatinize — the same process we're exploiting in tangzhong, just happening inside the dough as it bakes. After baking, as the bread cools and ages, those gelatinized starch molecules slowly recrystallize. They expel water (which migrates to the crust and evaporates) and lock into a rigid, crystalline structure. That's what makes stale bread feel dry, hard, and crumbly. It's not that the water vanished — it's that the starch molecules reorganized and kicked the water out.

Tangzhong front-loads the gelatinization. By pre-gelatinizing a portion of the flour, you create starch that's already done absorbing water and swelling — it can't retrograde because it's already in its gelatinized state, locked into a water-trapping gel. In the finished loaf, these pre-gelatinized starch pockets act as moisture reservoirs: they hold water that would otherwise be free to migrate, evaporate, and leave the crumb dry. The surrounding starch in the crumb still retrogrades normally — tangzhong doesn't stop staling, it just gives the bread a much larger water buffer to work through.

This is also why tangzhong bread toasts so well on day two or three. Standard bread by day two has already lost enough water that toasting makes it dry and brittle. Tangzhong bread still has water locked in those gel pockets — when you toast it, the surface crisps while the interior stays tender. It's the closest thing to fresh-baked texture you'll get from a three-day-old loaf.

One more thing: tangzhong also slightly increases the dough's effective hydration without making it wetter or harder to handle. A dough with 65% hydration plus tangzhong behaves — and bakes — more like a 72% hydration dough in terms of crumb softness and moisture retention. But it handles like a 65% dough because the extra water is bound up in the gelatinized starch, not sloshing around as free liquid. This is the real magic: you get the softness of high-hydration bread without the sticky, unmanageable dough that usually requires. For bakers who've struggled with high-hydration doughs sticking to everything, tangzhong is a workaround that doesn't compromise results. (If you want the extreme version of hydration-driven bread with no kneading at all, no-knead bread operates on a completely different principle — autolysis at 75-85% hydration — and produces a crusty artisan loaf rather than a soft sandwich bread.)

How to Add Tangzhong to Any Bread Recipe

Once you've made tangzhong once, adding it to other recipes becomes second nature. Here's the universal conversion formula:

Step Action
1. Calculate Take 6% of your recipe's total flour weight. That's your tangzhong flour. Multiply it by 5: that's your tangzhong liquid. Both come out of the recipe's existing flour and liquid — you're not adding anything extra.
2. Cook Whisk the tangzhong flour and liquid. Heat to 65°C, stirring constantly. Cool completely.
3. Adjust Add the cooled tangzhong to your dough along with the remaining flour and liquid. Everything else in the recipe stays the same — yeast, salt, sugar, fat, eggs.
4. Knead The dough will be stickier than usual. That's the gelatinized starch. Don't compensate with extra flour — the stickiness is temporary and mostly disappears as the gluten develops. Knead to full windowpane.

Worked example: your sandwich bread recipe calls for 400g flour and 260g water (65% hydration). Tangzhong flour = 400 × 6% = 24g (round to 25g). Tangzhong liquid = 25 × 5 = 125g water. Cook the 25g flour + 125g water into tangzhong. The main dough now uses 375g flour (400 − 25) and 135g water (260 − 125). Nothing else changes. The effective hydration moves from 65% to roughly 72% without the dough feeling wetter.

If your recipe uses milk instead of water, use milk for the tangzhong. If it uses a mix (like milk + egg as liquid), use whatever liquid is most abundant. The recipe scaler will recalculate all the adjusted weights for you if you're also scaling the batch size up or down. And for the base flour measurement, flour cups to grams ensures you're working from accurate weights — volume scoops throw off the 6% calculation enough to matter.

5 Mistakes That Undo All That Extra Work

Mistake 1: Adding Hot Tangzhong to the Dough

The tangzhong comes off the stove at 65°C (149°F). Yeast starts dying at about 50°C (122°F). If you dump hot tangzhong directly into your flour-yeast mixture, you'll kill a significant portion of the yeast before the dough even comes together. The loaf will under-rise or not rise at all.

The fix: cool the tangzhong to room temperature before using. Spread it on a small plate — the increased surface area cools it in about 10 minutes. Or make it the night before and refrigerate; cold tangzhong from the fridge is perfectly fine to add to room-temperature dough. If you're in a rush, stir the hot tangzhong constantly for 3-4 minutes to release steam, then let it sit for 5 more. The target is below 35°C (95°F) — warm is okay, hot is not. For precision, use an instant-read thermometer. A few degrees above room temperature won't hurt. A steaming blob of paste straight from the pan will.

Mistake 2: Too Much Flour During Kneading — Fighting the Stickiness

Tangzhong dough is stickier than standard dough. At the start of kneading, it will cling to your hands, the bowl, the counter — everything. The instinct is to add flour until it feels "normal." That instinct is wrong. Every tablespoon of extra flour you add (about 8g) drops the effective hydration and undoes some of what the tangzhong achieved. By the time the dough feels like standard bread dough, you've probably added 30-40g of flour — enough to produce a noticeably drier, denser loaf.

The fix: trust the process. Wet your hands with water instead of flouring them when handling sticky dough. Use a bench scraper to lift and turn the dough during kneading. By minute 6-7 of kneading (in a stand mixer) or minute 9-10 (by hand), the gluten will have developed enough that the stickiness mostly resolves itself. The dough will still be tacky but no longer cling aggressively. That's the target. If you absolutely must add flour, add no more than 1 tablespoon total, and only after you've kneaded for at least 5 minutes and confirmed the dough is genuinely too wet, not just in its normal sticky phase.

Mistake 3: The Tangzhong Was Too Thin — Undercooked

If you pull the tangzhong off the heat at the first sign of thickening — when it's still a liquid, just a slightly thicker liquid — the starch hasn't fully gelatinized. You've made a warm flour slurry, not tangzhong. It'll incorporate into the dough fine, but it won't trap significantly more water than raw flour. Your bread will be slightly softer than standard and you'll wonder what the fuss was about.

The fix: cook it until the whisk leaves clear trail marks on the bottom of the pan. The mixture should have the consistency of thick pudding or very soft mashed potatoes. If you tip the pan, it should move sluggishly as one mass, not pour. If you're unsure, cook it 15 seconds longer — slightly overcooked tangzhong (70°C) still works perfectly. Undercooked tangzhong (below 60°C) doesn't. If using a thermometer, 65°C is the minimum target; 68°C is fine.

Mistake 4: Using Tangzhong in the Wrong Kind of Bread

Tangzhong makes bread softer and moister. For sandwich bread, dinner rolls, burger buns, cinnamon rolls, brioche, and challah, that's exactly what you want. For baguettes, artisan boules, sourdough, and pizza — breads defined by their crunchy crust and chewy crumb — softness is a bug, not a feature.

I made a tangzhong sourdough boule once. The crumb was lovely — tender, evenly open, stayed fresh for days. But the crust was soft. Not "tender" soft. "Sandwich loaf" soft. It didn't crackle when it cooled. It didn't shatter when you cut into it. It was good bread. It was not what I wanted when I set out to make sourdough. The technique had worked against the goal.

The rule of thumb: tangzhong for enriched doughs and soft breads. Skip it for lean, crusty breads. Exceptions exist — tangzhong works beautifully in soft pretzels and some enriched flatbreads like naan — but if the word "crusty" appears in the bread's description, tangzhong is probably not your friend. For crusty bread, the no-knead method is a better fit. For troubleshooting soft bread that came out dense, why bread didn't rise covers the dough side.

Mistake 5: Under-Kneading Because the Dough Feels Slack

Tangzhong dough plays tricks on your hands. The gelatinized starch lubricates the dough, making it feel softer and more extensible than it actually is — like it's already fully kneaded even when the gluten is only halfway developed. You stop kneading early because the dough feels smooth and stretchy. Then the loaf comes out dense and squat because the gluten network wasn't strong enough to trap the extra moisture.

The fix: ignore how the dough feels. Use the windowpane test. Pull off a golf-ball-sized piece and stretch it between your fingers. If it stretches into a thin, translucent membrane without tearing, the gluten is developed. If it tears, keep kneading. Tangzhong doughs typically need the same kneading time as standard doughs (10-12 minutes by hand, 6-8 minutes in a stand mixer at speed 2-3) — the dough just doesn't feel like it needs it. This is the mistake I made on my second tangzhong loaf. Trust the windowpane, not your hands.

For understanding what proper gluten development looks like and how dough texture changes from start to finish, the windowpane test guide linked above has photos of each stage. If you've never done the test before, it takes 15 seconds and tells you more about your dough than any timer can.

📋 Tangzhong Cheat Sheet — Tape It Inside Your Cabinet

Five extra minutes. Bread that stays soft for days. These seven checkpoints catch every mistake I've made.

  • Ratio: 1 part flour : 5 parts liquid — by weight. For a standard loaf: 25g flour + 125g milk/water. Use the Recipe Scaler to recalculate for different batch sizes
  • Flour and liquid come FROM the recipe — you're pre-cooking a portion, not adding ingredients. Total recipe amounts don't change
  • Cook to 65°C (149°F) — whisk leaves trail marks on the pan bottom. Use a thermometer or the trail-mark test. About 90 seconds over medium-low heat
  • Cool completely before adding to dough — hot tangzhong kills yeast. Spread on a plate for 10 min, or make the night before and refrigerate
  • Don't add extra flour during kneading — the dough will be stickier than usual. Wet your hands instead. Trust the process
  • Knead to windowpane — tangzhong dough feels softer than it is. The windowpane test tells you when it's actually ready
  • Best for: enriched breads — sandwich loaves, dinner rolls, buns, brioche, challah, cinnamon rolls. Skip for crusty artisan loaves. For crusty bread: no-knead method

Tangzhong is the rare technique that costs almost nothing — five minutes, flour and water you've already measured — and delivers a genuinely transformative result. The first time you pull a tangzhong sandwich loaf out of the pan, press your finger into the top, and watch it spring back like a memory foam mattress, you'll understand why this method has been standard practice in Asian bakeries for decades and why it's been slowly migrating into Western home kitchens over the past ten years.

The technique doesn't just make bread softer. It changes the economics of home baking. The reason most people don't bake their own sandwich bread every week is that homemade bread goes stale before you can finish the loaf. You bake on Sunday. By Tuesday morning it's dry. By Wednesday it's toast-only territory. By Thursday it's croutons. Tangzhong stretches the usable window from 1-2 days to 3-4. That's the difference between baking bread as a weekend novelty and baking bread as a practical weekly staple.

Start with a simple milk bread or dinner rolls. Get the feel for how the dough handles. Once you've nailed the technique once or twice, you'll start seeing every bread recipe through a tangzhong lens: could this be softer? Could it last longer? For most enriched breads, the answer is yes. And the cost is five minutes and a saucepan you were going to wash anyway.
