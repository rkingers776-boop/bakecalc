How to Price Homemade Baked Goods — Real Numbers, Real Math

Stop guessing what to charge for your cookies, cakes, and bread. Ingredient cost, packaging, labor, overhead, and profit — real formulas with real numbers from someone who's done it.

Most home bakers price their stuff wrong. Not a little wrong — dramatically wrong. They charge $15 for a dozen cookies that cost them $12 to make, and they call the $3 "profit." It's not profit. It doesn't cover the electricity. It doesn't cover the parchment paper. It sure as hell doesn't cover the three hours they spent mixing, scooping, baking, cooling, and packaging.

I know because I did it too. The first time I sold cookies, I priced them at what "felt right." Six months later I sat down and actually ran the numbers. I was making about $4 an hour. Below minimum wage. For something people told me was "the best cookie they'd ever had."

This article is the system I built after that wake-up call. It covers every cost that matters, in the order it hits your wallet. At the end, there's a free cost calculator that does all the math for you — punch in your ingredients, quantities, and target margin, and it spits out a price per unit. No spreadsheet required.

1. Ingredient Cost — the foundation

This is where everyone starts and where most people stop. It's the right place to begin. It's just not the whole picture.

The core loop: you buy ingredients in bulk (pounds, kilograms, liters) and use them in small amounts per batch (grams, tablespoons, milliliters). To price a single batch, you need to convert everything to a per-gram or per-milliliter cost, then multiply by how much the recipe uses.

The per-gram method

Cost per gram = purchase price ÷ total grams in the package.

Example: a 5-pound bag of all-purpose flour costs $4.29 at Costco. 5 lb × 454 g/lb = 2,270 grams. $4.29 ÷ 2,270 = $0.00189 per gram — less than two-tenths of a cent.

Your chocolate chip cookie recipe uses 1 cup of flour = 120 grams. 120 × $0.00189 = $0.23 in flour for the whole batch.

That's the math. Now do it for every ingredient. Flour, sugar, butter, eggs, chocolate, vanilla, salt, baking soda. Every single one.

The ingredient most people undercount: butter

Butter is almost always the most expensive ingredient in a cookie or cake recipe — and the one home bakers most frequently leave out of their mental cost tally because "I already had it in the fridge."

Butter at retail runs $4–6 per pound (4 sticks = 454 g). That's roughly $0.009–0.013 per gram. A recipe calling for 1 cup of butter = 227 grams costs $1.98–2.95 in butter alone. A recipe calling for ½ cup = 113 grams is about $1.00–1.50 in butter.

If you're not accounting for butter at its real replacement cost — the price you'd pay to buy it again tomorrow — you're undercounting. Use our butter cups-to-grams converter to get the gram weight, then multiply by your per-gram butter cost.

Sugar and flour — cheap per gram, expensive in volume

Granulated sugar at ~$0.001 per gram and all-purpose flour at ~$0.002 per gram feel trivial. But a batch of 36 cookies might use 400 grams of flour and 300 grams of sugar — that's $0.80 + $0.30 = $1.10 just in dry bulk ingredients. Add $2.50 in butter, $1.50 in eggs, $4.00 in chocolate chips… suddenly a "cheap" batch is $9.10 before packaging or labor.

Run your recipe through the converters to get exact gram weights:

  • Sugar: 1 cup = 200 g · 200g→cups · brown sugar · powdered sugar
  • Butter: 1 cup = 227 g · 1 tbsp = 14.2 g
  • Liquids: 8 oz→mL (water/milk) · oil oz→mL

2. Packaging — the silent margin killer

Packaging costs more than you think. A single cupcake insert, a bakery box, a sticker, and a piece of parchment can easily hit $0.80–1.50 per unit — before the food even enters the equation.

Here's what I pay per unit for common packaging (mid-2026 pricing, US, buying in quantities of 100–500):

  • Standard bakery box (8×8×3" white, windowed): $0.38–0.55
  • Cupcake insert (holds 6, fits standard box): $0.12–0.18
  • Parchment paper sheet (pre-cut, 500-pack): $0.02 each
  • Sticker / label (2" round, custom printed, 500-pack): $0.15–0.30
  • Twine or ribbon (per package, baker's twine): $0.08–0.15
  • Plastic clamshell (for cookies, 6-count): $0.25–0.45
  • Cellophane bag + twist tie (for bread loaves): $0.10–0.20
  • Thank-you card (business card stock, 1000-pack): $0.03 each

For a half-dozen cookies in a clamshell with a sticker and a thank-you card: you're at $0.43–0.78 in packaging. For a decorated 8-inch cake in a windowed box with a board, insert, and ribbon: $1.20–2.00.

This is not optional cost. You cannot sell baked goods without packaging. Price it in.

3. Labor — the line between hobby and business

This is the part most home bakers skip. "I enjoy baking," they say. "I'd be doing it anyway." And that's fine — for a hobby. But if you're selling, you're running a business, and businesses pay for labor.

There are two honest ways to price your time:

Method A: Hourly rate (what's your time worth?)

Pick an hourly rate. $15/hour is the floor — that's roughly what a bakery counter person makes. $20–25/hour is reasonable for skilled baking labor in most US markets. $30–40/hour is specialty cake decorating territory.

Now actually track your time for one batch. Not just the mixing and baking — the shopping, the prep, the cooling, the packaging, the labeling, the delivery coordination, the customer messages. A batch of decorated sugar cookies that "takes 3 hours to decorate" probably takes 5–6 hours when you count everything.

Labor cost per batch = your hourly rate × total hours.

If you made $25/hour and a batch takes 4 hours total: that's $100 in labor. Divided across 24 cookies, that's $4.17 per cookie — before ingredients or packaging.

Method B: Percentage-based (the professional kitchen approach)

Professional bakeries typically target labor at 25–35% of the final selling price. If a cake sells for $40, $10–14 of that covers labor. This is easier to scale but harder to set initially — you need to know your selling price first, which depends on… labor. Circular, I know.

My advice: start with Method A for your first three products. Figure out what you're actually spending in time. Once you have data, switch to Method B for speed.

4. Overhead — the invisible costs

Overhead is everything that isn't ingredients, packaging, or your direct labor. It's easy to ignore because you don't see it leave your wallet per-batch. But it's real.

Electricity and gas: an electric oven running at 350°F draws about 2–3 kWh per hour. At $0.15/kWh (US average), that's $0.30–0.45 per hour of oven time. If you bake for 4 hours, that's $1.20–1.80. The mixer, the fridge, the lights — call it $2–3 per full baking day.

Equipment amortization: your stand mixer cost $400 and will last roughly 2,000 mixing hours in a home setting. That's $0.20 per hour of mixing. Sheet pans ($15 each, ~300 uses before they warp) = $0.05 per bake. Piping tips, silicone mats, cooling racks — it all wears. A fair equipment overhead estimate for a home bakery is $0.50–1.50 per batch.

Other overhead you might not think about:

  • Water: washing dishes, hand-washing, cleaning surfaces. Trivial per batch — but real.
  • Waste: the cookie that broke, the frosting that got over-whipped, the parchment you used once and threw away. Estimate 3–5% of ingredient cost.
  • Samples and giveaways: the extras you give to customers, the tasting box for a potential wedding client. These are marketing, not gifts. Budget them.
  • Insurance and permits: if you're operating legally (cottage food license, liability insurance), these are fixed monthly costs that need to be spread across every unit you sell.
  • Transaction fees: payment processors (Square, Stripe, Venmo Business) take 2.6–3.5%. On a $40 cake, that's $1.04–1.40. Don't forget it.

5. Profit margin — the number that makes it a business

After ingredients, packaging, labor, and overhead: profit. This is the money the business keeps — to reinvest, to grow, to pay you beyond your hourly wage. Without profit, you have a job that pays less than minimum wage and requires you to clean flour off every surface in your kitchen.

Standard food business margins

  • 10–15% net profit — typical for a well-run bakery. Tight but sustainable.
  • 20–25% net profit — excellent. You're doing something right (likely: high-margin products, efficient process, or premium positioning).
  • Below 5% — you're one broken mixer away from losing money. Raise prices or cut costs.

The full pricing formula

Here's the equation your cost calculator runs for you:

Selling Price = (Ingredient Cost + Packaging Cost) ÷ (1 − Labor% − Overhead% − Profit%)

Real example — a dozen chocolate chip cookies:

  • Ingredient cost: $6.20 (flour, butter, sugar, eggs, chocolate, vanilla, leavening, salt)
  • Packaging cost: $0.65 (clamshell, sticker, thank-you card)
  • Direct cost subtotal: $6.85
  • Labor (25%): $6.85 ÷ (1 − 0.25 − 0.10 − 0.20) − $6.85 — let's unpack this below

Run the full formula with 25% labor, 10% overhead, 20% profit target:

$6.85 ÷ (1 − 0.25 − 0.10 − 0.20) = $6.85 ÷ 0.45 = $15.22

$15.22 for a dozen cookies. Split 12 ways: $1.27 per cookie.

That number surprises most home bakers. "Who pays $15 for a dozen cookies?" The answer: people who buy from professional bakeries. Walk into a good bakery in any US city and a dozen cookies is $14–22. The price isn't unreasonable — your expectations were just calibrated to grocery-store numbers.

The 3× rule (quick-and-dirty estimate)

If the full formula feels like too much math: multiply your ingredient cost by 3. That's your rough selling price, assuming a standard cost structure (ingredients ~30% of price, everything else ~70%). It's not precise — it overcharges for cheap-ingredient, high-labor products (decorated sugar cookies) and undercharges for expensive-ingredient, low-labor products (brownies). But it's a useful gut check.

For the cookies above: $6.20 × 3 = $18.60 per dozen. Higher than the formula's $15.22 because the formula accounts for the specific cost structure. Use 3× as a floor, not a ceiling — and then run the real numbers through the cost calculator.

7. Six things most pricing guides leave out

  1. Refunds and redos. The cake that got dropped. The cookies that burned because you got distracted. The customer who changed their mind about the frosting color after you finished. Budget 3–5% of revenue for mistakes and accommodations.
  2. Seasonality. Butter prices spike in November–December (holiday baking demand). Eggs spike when avian flu hits. If you're quoting prices months in advance, either lock in your ingredient costs or build a 5–10% buffer into custom orders.
  3. Delivery cost. Gas, parking, the 20 minutes you spend waiting for the customer to answer their door. Either charge a delivery fee ($5–10 within reasonable radius) or build it into the price. Never eat delivery costs silently — it's the fastest way to turn a profitable order into a loss.
  4. Customization time. The back-and-forth messages to design a custom cake topper. The three rounds of "can you make it more teal?" These are billable hours. Either quote custom work at a higher rate, or include a "design consultation" line item.
  5. Taxes. In most US states, food sold directly by the producer is tax-exempt — but prepared food sold for immediate consumption may not be. Check your state's cottage food laws. If you do need to charge sales tax, it's on top of the price, not included in it.
  6. Your own threshold. There is a price below which you will resent the work. You'll dread the order. You'll cut corners. You'll burn out. That price is different for everyone, but it exists. Find it. Never go below it — no matter what the customer says, no matter what the market says. The market doesn't have to bake the cookies. You do.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the easiest way to calculate all this without a spreadsheet?

You can print the results as a cost card — useful for markets and pop-ups where customers ask "how did you arrive at this price?"

How do I know if my price is too high?

If customers consistently say "that's too expensive" without buying, your price might be high for your market — or your positioning might be wrong. A $6 cookie in a paper bag feels expensive. A $6 cookie in a branded box with a flavor card and a story about the single-origin chocolate feels special. Same cookie, different perceived value. Try the packaging and story first, before you lower the price.

Should I charge different prices for different flavors?

Yes, if the ingredient cost difference is significant. Chocolate chip cookies vs. plain sugar cookies: same price. Chocolate chip vs. white chocolate macadamia: macadamia nuts are 3–4× the cost of chocolate chips — that flavor should cost more. Your cost calculator handles this naturally — just run each variant's ingredient list separately and compare the results.

What if I'm just selling to friends and family — do I still need all this?

Friends and family are the hardest customers to charge properly. They expect a discount because they know you. You feel guilty charging full price because you know them. My advice: pick a "friends and family" discount (15–20% off your standard price) and apply it consistently. Tell them the real price, then apply the discount visibly on the invoice. It accomplishes two things: they understand the value, and they don't expect the discount to be the real price when they refer you to someone else.

📋 The Home Baker's Pricing Checklist

  • Every ingredient priced per gram — use cups-to-grams conversions for American recipes
  • Packaging costed per unit (box, insert, sticker, ribbon, card)
  • Labor tracked for one full batch — shopping through delivery, not just baking
  • Overhead estimated (electricity, equipment wear, water, waste, transaction fees)
  • Profit margin set at 15–25% above total costs
  • Price compared to 3× ingredient cost as gut check (floor, not ceiling)
  • Delivery fee charged separately or built into price — never eaten silently
  • Price reviewed every 6 months — ingredient costs change, your skill improves, your market grows

Pricing is uncomfortable. It forces you to say out loud what you think your work is worth. Most home bakers would rather talk about anything else — flour types, mixing techniques, oven spring. But pricing is the thing that determines whether baking stays a hobby or becomes a business. Learn it. Get comfortable with it. Use the calculator until the numbers feel automatic. And then — this is the hard part — actually charge what the math says.
