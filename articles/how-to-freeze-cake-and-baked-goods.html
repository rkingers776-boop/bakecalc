How to Freeze Cake and Other Baked Goods So They Thaw Fresh

The first cake I ever froze came back out tasting of the freezer and nothing else, with a rim of crumb around the edge that had gone the texture of dry toast. I assumed that was what freezing did. It is not. Freezing is the only thing a home baker can do that genuinely stops a cake from getting older — it just also punishes every shortcut you take on the way in and on the way out.

⚡ Quick Answer: Cold, Fast, Tight, Slow

Cold in: cool to room temperature first. A warm cake carries its own steam into the wrap.

Fast down: thin and spread out, on a tray with air around it. The centre's cooling speed decides the ice crystals.

Tight shut: two layers and the air pressed out. Air is what burns, not cold.

Slow out: thaw in the refrigerator, still wrapped, and unwrap only at room temperature.

And the rule that is not a technique: freezing pauses staling, it does not reverse it. A three-day-old cake comes out a three-day-old cake.

There are two separate things happening when you put a cake in a freezer, and almost every disappointing result comes from confusing them. The first is that the water in the crumb turns to ice and the staling process — starch recrystallising, water migrating to the crust — stops dead at about 0 °F. That is the good news, and it is genuinely good: nothing else available in a home kitchen comes close.

The second is that ice takes up more space than water, and how much space depends entirely on how fast the water froze. Freeze a cake quickly and you get a multitude of crystals small enough to sit inside the existing air cells. Freeze it slowly and you get fewer, larger crystals that push against the walls of those cells, and when they melt on the way out the walls do not spring back. That is the gummy, wet, collapsing crumb people blame on the freezer itself.

The short version: the freezer is not a shelf, it is a pause button, and a pause button only works if you press it cleanly. Everything below is how to do that for cake, cookies, pastry and frosting, how long each one honestly keeps, and the short list of things that should not go in at all.

#1 What the Freezer Actually Does to a Crumb

A cake straight out of the oven is a foam: millions of gas cells held open by a network of gluten strands with gelatinised starch plastered over them, and every surface of that network is wet. The water is not in a puddle, it is adsorbed onto starch and protein, which is exactly why staling happens in the first place — over a day or two, that water drifts out of the starch granules and the granules re-order themselves into a rigid crystalline lattice. Firm, dry-tasting crumb. Nothing has evaporated; the water has simply changed address.

Take the whole structure below freezing and the water becomes ice where it stands, the molecules lose the mobility to re-order, and the clock stops. At 0 °F (−18 °C), which is what a properly set home freezer holds, that pause is effectively complete. A loaf or a layer put in fresh will come out tasting like the day it went in, provided nothing else goes wrong. It is worth being precise about "provided nothing else goes wrong", because two things usually do.

The first is crystal size. Water does not freeze at one instant throughout a cake. The surface chills first, then a freezing front moves inward, and the last water to freeze is the water in the middle — which it reaches after the outside has already been solid for an hour or more. Meanwhile, ice is greedy: a growing crystal pulls water toward itself from the surrounding unfrozen crumb. The slower that front moves, the longer the crystals have to grow, and the bigger the holes they leave behind.

The second is air. A freezer is a very dry place, and the ice on the surface of anything exposed will sublime — turn straight to vapour and leave — whenever the air around it is drier than the ice. That is all freezer burn is: a surface that has been slowly freeze-dried. It is not spoiled and it is not unsafe. It is just leathery, and no amount of thawing brings it back.

Both problems have the same two answers, which is why the rest of this article is mostly about cooling speed and packaging. Fast freezing keeps the crystals small. Airtight wrapping keeps the sublimation at zero. Nothing else you can do at home moves the needle much.

#2 The Four Rules That Beat Any Recipe

Everything that follows is a specific application of four rules. If you remember nothing else, these cover about ninety percent of the failures.

1. Nothing goes in warm. A cake pulled from the oven is still driving moisture outward — it will keep doing that for an hour, because the centre is holding heat that its crumb insulates well. Wrap it at that point and the steam has nowhere to go. It condenses on the inside of the plastic, and you have built a small humid greenhouse around a cake, which gives you a tacky, weeping surface and, if it stays cold long enough, a much friendlier environment for mould than a dry one. Wait until the cake is at room temperature to the touch and the tin is completely cold, which for a 9-inch layer is two to three hours on a rack.

2. Get it cold fast. This is the one most people skip. A whole 9-inch layer is a thick slab of wet insulation, and its centre might take four hours to reach 0 °F in a home freezer — four hours during which the freezing front is crawling inward and the crystals are growing. A 20 °F head start is worth more than it sounds: chill the cake in the refrigerator for 30 to 45 minutes first, or better, split the layers horizontally before freezing so each piece is half as thick. Halving the thickness does not halve the cooling time, it cuts it much further than that, because both the distance the heat has to travel and the mass it has to come from are reduced at once.

3. Two layers, no air. Plastic wrap directly against the surface, pressed so it actually touches, then a freezer bag or a rigid container with the air pressed out. One layer of cling film is not airtight; it is water-resistant and it will let vapour through the folds. Foil alone is worse — it blocks light and air but it creases and tears, and it is not a barrier at all. The combination is what works: film for the barrier, bag for the mechanical protection and the second barrier.

4. Thaw slowly, and stay wrapped. This is where a good freeze is thrown away. Take a frozen cake out and unwrap it on the counter and the cold surface meets warm humid kitchen air; water condenses on it immediately, the crumb at the edge drinks it, and the frosting turns glossy and slack. Leave it wrapped until it is at room temperature, then open it. Better still, thaw it in the refrigerator overnight and let it finish on the counter for half an hour still in its wrapper.

One more, less a rule than a habit: label everything with what it is and the date it went in. A foil-wrapped brick in February is indistinguishable from the other foil-wrapped brick, and the whole point of the freezer is that it lets you be organised about a week you did not plan for.

#3 Cake: Layers, Frosted Cakes and Cupcakes

Bare layers are the easy case, and they are how you should freeze cake most of the time. Bake, cool completely on a rack, level the dome if you want flat layers later — do it before freezing, because a cold cake cuts cleanly but a frozen one can shatter at the edge — then chill 30 to 45 minutes, wrap in film, then bag. Two to three months, and they come out indistinguishable from fresh if they went in fresh.

Freeze layers with a sheet of parchment between them if you are stacking several in one bag. They will weld together otherwise, and separating them while frozen snaps the edges.

A frosted cake is a different job because the frosting has to be set before you wrap it or the film prints a pattern into the surface that no thaw will smooth out. Put the finished cake in the freezer uncovered for 30 to 45 minutes — long enough for the buttercream to firm to a light touch, short enough that the crumb does not start drying. Then wrap or box it. One to two months. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator, still wrapped, and unwrap at room temperature.

Two cake styles break these rules. Angel food and chiffon are egg-white foams with almost no fat, and their structure is a lattice of very thin protein films around air. Ice crystals puncture those films easily and the cake collapses on thaw into something dense and damp. They can be frozen for about a month, unadorned, in a rigid container so nothing presses on them, and they will be acceptable rather than good. Cakes with a custard or cream filling should not be frozen at all: the starch gel in pastry cream separates on thaw and weeps water into the surrounding sponge.

Cupcakes freeze well and are worth doing in batches. Unfrosted, in a single layer in a rigid box, two months; thaw at room temperature in about an hour. Frosted cupcakes are more fragile — freeze them uncovered until the frosting sets, then box them so nothing touches the tops, and give them one month rather than two.

#4 Cookies, Baked and Raw

Baked cookies freeze better than almost anything else in the kitchen, because a cookie is already a low-moisture, high-fat, high-sugar system with very little water to turn into ice. Two to three months in a bag, thawed twenty minutes on the counter, and the only detectable difference is a slightly softer edge that firms up once they are exposed to air.

To bring back the just-baked texture, three to five minutes in a 300 °F oven does more than any thawing method. It drives off the surface water that made them soft and re-crisps the edge without cooking the middle any further. This is the same refresh that rescues a batch that has gone hard on the counter, and it works on frozen cookies directly, no thaw needed.

Raw dough is the better investment, because it lets you bake six cookies on a Tuesday instead of sixty on a Sunday. Portion into balls first — a scoop, rolled briefly, on a tray lined with parchment — and freeze the tray flat for an hour or two until the balls are solid, then tip them into a bag. If you bag soft dough straight away you get one large cookie-shaped mass, and portioning it later warms the dough and defeats the purpose.

Bake them from frozen. Same oven temperature, two to four minutes longer. The result is usually better than baking fresh dough: the fat is cold and firm when it hits the oven, so the cookie spreads less and comes out thicker, with a chewier middle. Our own write-up of why cookies spread makes the same point from the other direction — warm dough spreads, cold dough holds its shape — and chilling is the cheapest version of that lever you have.

One caveat on the time. Frozen dough has lost a little surface water to sublimation even inside a bag, so a batch that has been in for three months will bake marginally drier at the edge than one frozen last week. If you are baking from an old bag, pull them a minute early rather than a minute late. For slice-and-bake logs, freeze the log solid, then slice at a quarter inch while it is still hard; a soft log squashes out of shape under the knife.

#5 Pie, Pastry and Anything With a Crust

Pastry is where the freezer earns its keep, because pie dough is largely fat and flour with just enough water to hold it together, and cold is what makes it handleable. A disc of dough wrapped in film keeps three months and is arguably better after two weeks than on the day it was made: the flour has had time to hydrate fully, and the dough rolls out more evenly and shrinks less in the oven.

Thaw a disc in the refrigerator, not on the counter — four to six hours or overnight. Counter-thawing softens the outside while the middle is still hard, and rolling a disc with a hard core gives you a crust with thick and thin patches that bake at different rates.

An assembled unbaked pie can be frozen whole, and this is the trick that makes Thanksgiving survivable. Line the tin, fill it, add the top crust, then freeze it solid and uncovered first — an hour or so — before wrapping. Freezing it solid before wrapping is what stops the wrap from pressing into the crimped edge. Bake it from frozen: 425 °F for 15 minutes to set the crust, then 375 °F for another 45 to 60 minutes, which is roughly 20 minutes longer than the same pie would take from fresh. Do not thaw it first; a thawed pie has a soggy bottom waiting to happen.

A baked pie freezes acceptably for about two months, but the crust will never be crisp again — the water that migrates out of the filling on thaw goes straight into the pastry. Reheat it uncovered at 350 °F for 15 minutes to drive some of that back off, and serve it warm, which is how it tastes best anyway.

Scones, biscuits and rough puff all freeze well as unbaked shapes, and all of them bake better from frozen for the same reason cookies do: cold fat makes steam late, and steam is what makes the layers. Add five minutes to the bake and do not let them sit out first. A meringue-topped pie, on the other hand, should never be frozen; the foam collapses and weeps sugar syrup onto the plate.

#6 Frosting and Fillings: Which Ones Survive

Frosting is mostly fat and sugar with a little water, which is a very freezer-tolerant combination — with one exception that catches people out. Buttercream freezes beautifully; cream cheese frosting freezes adequately; anything built on a starch gel or on whipped cream does not.

American buttercream — butter, icing sugar, a splash of milk — keeps three months. Freeze it flat in a bag, pressed thin so it chills fast and thaws fast, and squeeze the air out. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator, then let it come to room temperature in the bag and re-whip it for three to five minutes. It will look curdled and separated when it first warms up, and this is normal: the emulsion has broken and re-whipping brings it back. Adding a tablespoon of the reserved liquid helps if it stays grainy.

Swiss and Italian meringue buttercream freeze just as well and re-whip the same way. They are more forgiving than people expect, largely because the meringue is cooked and stable before the butter goes in.

Cream cheese frosting is the marginal one. It freezes for about two months, but the stabilisers in some brands do not survive the thaw and the frosting turns loose and pourable. Re-whipping cold, before it fully warms, recovers most of it. If you are frosting a cake you intend to freeze whole, this is the one case where I would frost after thawing rather than before.

Ganache freezes for three months in a flat bag and thaws to exactly what it was, because it is an emulsion of fat and water with no structure to damage. Whipped cream, pastry cream and anything set with gelatine should not be frozen; the foam collapses, or the gel network breaks and releases water that soaks whatever it is sitting on.

#7 Thawing: The Half Nobody Plans For

A thaw is not the absence of a freeze. It is a second process with its own rules, and it is where the difference between "tastes fresh" and "tastes frozen" is actually decided.

The governing fact is that the surface of a frozen object is below the dew point of your kitchen, so the moment you expose it, water from the air condenses onto it. That water does not sit politely on top. It soaks into the nearest dry thing, which is the crumb at the edge of a cake or the pastry of a pie. This is why a cake unwrapped on the counter develops a dark, damp, slightly rubbery band around its edge that the middle does not have. Keeping it wrapped keeps the water in the air where it belongs.

The refrigerator is the right default for anything large. A single cake layer takes four to six hours; a whole frosted cake takes eight to twelve, so overnight is the practical unit. Cookies, cupcakes and scones are small enough that twenty to sixty minutes on the counter, still covered, is fine and better than a fridge thaw that makes them taste of the refrigerator. Bread is the exception that proves the rule: a frozen slice goes straight into the toaster, and a whole loaf is better reheated than thawed.

And then there is the oven, which is the most under-used thawing tool in the kitchen. A thawed cake layer given five to seven minutes at 325 °F comes out with a surface that tastes baked rather than defrosted, which matters for anything with a crust. Do it uncovered, and do it right before serving, because it also drives off the water that made the surface soft.

#8 Baking Straight From the Freezer

Four things are better baked from frozen than thawed, and it is worth knowing why so you can apply the same reasoning to anything else. The principle is that cold fat melts late, which delays the steam that lifts layers, and cold dough holds its shape while the outside sets. Thawing removes both advantages.

#10 The Freezer-Burn Clock, and How to Beat It

Freezer burn runs on surface area and time, which means two things you can control. The first is how much surface you expose: a flat layer wrapped tight has less exposed area than a crumpled one, and a bag with the air pressed out has almost none. The second is where in the freezer it sits.

Freezers are not the same temperature everywhere. The door is the warmest spot and the one that swings twenty degrees every time it opens; the back and the bottom are the coldest and the most stable. Put the things you care about at the back, and never store a cake against the door. If your freezer is a frost-free model — most are — it runs a fan and a periodic defrost cycle, both of which actively pull moisture out of the air and, by extension, out of anything loosely wrapped. In a frost-free freezer, the second layer of packaging is not optional.

It is also worth checking the actual temperature rather than the dial. A freezer set to "cold" and holding 10 °F is not freezing things properly; it is freezing the surface, letting the middle hover, and cycling everything through partial thaws every time the compressor kicks in. A cheap refrigerator thermometer costs less than one wasted cake. Below 0 °F, nothing is growing and nothing is staling; between 0 °F and 15 °F, you are storing things in a slow-motion ruin.

Label with contents and date, and use a marker that does not rub off in a damp freezer. Then read the labels when you open the door. A freezer works as a system only if the oldest thing gets eaten first, and the alternative — a foil brick from an unknown month — is exactly the situation the freezer was supposed to prevent.

#11 The Ten-Minute Version

None of this takes long if you do it at the right moment, which is the hour after the cake comes out, not the day you need the tin back.

Cool completely on a rack, two to three hours for a layer. Level the dome while it is still soft enough to cut cleanly. Chill it in the refrigerator for half an hour — that is the step that buys small ice crystals. Wrap in film pressed against the surface, then bag or box with the air out, and write the date on it. Put it at the back of the freezer, not the door.

Then, when you want it back: refrigerator overnight in the wrapper, room temperature for half an hour still in the wrapper, unwrap, and if it is a layer you are serving plain, five minutes at 325 °F to bring the surface back to life. That is the whole method, and it is the difference between a cake that tastes like it was baked this morning and one that tastes like the inside of a freezer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you freeze a cake that is already frosted?

Yes, and buttercream holds up better than most people expect. Put the finished cake in the freezer uncovered for 30 to 45 minutes, until the frosting is firm to a light touch, then wrap it or slide it into a container. That short uncovered spell is what stops the wrap from printing a pattern into the surface. A frosted cake keeps one to two months. Thaw it in the refrigerator overnight, still wrapped, and unwrap only once it has come to room temperature, or condensation will bead on the frosting and leave it sticky.

How long can you keep baked goods in the freezer?

At 0 °F nothing spoils, so the limits are about texture, not safety. An unfrosted cake layer or a batch of cookies is good for two to three months, a frosted cake one to two months, cookie dough three months, pie dough three months, and buttercream three months. Bread is two to three months. Everything past those dates is still edible; it just tastes older than it should, which is why the label matters more than the calendar.

Can you bake cookie dough straight from the freezer?

Yes, and it bakes better than thawed dough because the fat stays cold and the cookie spreads less. Add two to four minutes to the recipe time and keep the oven temperature the same. Do not press a frozen ball flat, and do not put frozen dough on a hot sheet that already has cookies on it. If the dough has been frozen for months, expect slightly less spread and a marginally drier edge, because some water has sublimated out of the surface.

🔧 Before It Goes In — Five Checks

Run down this list once. Every item is on it because it is the step people skip.

  1. Is it actually cold? Room temperature to the touch, and the tin cold. Not "cool enough".
  2. Is it in the thinnest form you will use? Split layers, slice loaves, portion dough. Thin freezes fast and thaws without a raw middle.
  3. Is the film touching the surface? Air pockets are where freezer burn starts, and loose film is a bag of air.
  4. Is there a second layer? Bag or rigid box. In a frost-free freezer, one layer is not enough.
  5. Does it say what it is and when? Two seconds with a marker now, or ten minutes of guessing in March.

And one for the way out: leave the wrapper on until the cake is at room temperature. Opening it early is the single fastest way to undo a good freeze.

If you take one thing from this article: freezing pauses staling but never reverses it. Put it in fresh, or do not bother — a cake that has already spent two days on the counter will come out of the freezer tasting like a cake that spent two days on the counter, because that is exactly what it is.

Second thing: chill before you wrap. Thirty minutes in the refrigerator is the cheapest improvement available to a home freezer, because crystal size is decided while the centre is still warm, and once the crystals are large no wrapping technique fixes the crumb.

And if you have already frozen something badly — the damp edge, the freezer-tasting corner — cut it off and eat the middle. It is not a loss, it is a smaller cake with a lesson attached.

🛒 What makes the difference here

Heavy-duty film that actually clings, bags thick enough to be a barrier rather than a suggestion, and a refrigerator thermometer so you know what your freezer is really holding.
