Sourdough Troubleshooting — 10 Common Loaf Problems & Exactly How to Fix Each One

Your starter is alive. Your dough rose. But the loaf still came out wrong. Here are 10 things that go wrong between mixing and cooling — and exactly how to fix each one, tested by someone who has made every mistake in the book.

Here's something nobody tells you when you start baking sourdough: getting the starter alive is only the first 20% of the battle. The remaining 80% is everything that happens after you mix it into dough — fermentation timing, gluten development, shaping, scoring, steam, baking temperature. Each one of those steps has about three ways to go wrong, and if any one of them fails, the loaf fails.

I've baked somewhere around 400 sourdough loaves in the last three years. This article is a catalog of everything that went wrong during the first 200, and what I learned fixing each one. It's organized by the symptom you see when you cut into the loaf — because that's how you actually troubleshoot. You don't think "hmm, was my bulk fermentation 45 minutes too short." You think "why is this crumb so dense." Start with the symptom, work backward to the cause, apply the fix. Next loaf will be better.

1. Dense Crumb — No Holes, Heavy, Like a Brick

What you see: you cut into the loaf and the inside looks more like a sandwich loaf than an artisan sourdough — tight, uniform, small holes, heavy. The bread tastes fine but the texture is wrong. It's not light and airy. It's bread, but it's sad bread.

The culprit, 90% of the time: under-proofing. Sourdough takes much longer to ferment than commercial yeast — often 4-6 hours of bulk fermentation at room temperature, sometimes more. Beginners consistently cut this short because recipes say "let rise until doubled" and sourdough often doesn't visually double in the same dramatic way commercial yeast dough does. It might only increase in volume by 50-75%, but that's enough if the dough feels puffy and jiggly when you shake the bowl.

Other suspects: your starter wasn't strong enough (it doubled in the jar but couldn't lift a full loaf — feed it at 1:3:3 for 2-3 cycles to rebuild strength), you didn't develop enough gluten (fewer than 4 sets of stretch-and-folds over the bulk fermentation), or your hydration is below 65% (which produces tight crumb by design — nothing wrong, just a style choice).

The fix: extend bulk fermentation. Push it 30-60 minutes longer than you think it needs. The dough should feel puffy, airy, and a little jiggly — not dense and resistant. Do the poke test: flour a finger, poke ½ inch into the dough. If it springs back fast, it needs more time. If it springs back slowly and leaves a slight indent, it's ready. For numbers-based adjustments, use the hydration calculator to make sure your dough is in the right hydration range for open crumb (68%+ for noticeable holes).

2. Flat Loaf — No Oven Spring, Looks Like a Pancake

What you see: you preheated the Dutch oven for 45 minutes, carefully transferred the dough, scored it beautifully, and 45 minutes later you're staring at a loaf that's wider than it is tall. It spread sideways instead of springing up.

The culprit: over-proofing. The dough fermented past its peak, the gluten structure started breaking down, and the yeast had exhausted its food supply. When it hit the hot oven, there was no gas left to expand and no structure left to contain it if there were.

Other causes to check:

  • Weak shaping. If you didn't build enough surface tension during shaping, the dough spreads instead of rising. A properly shaped boule should hold its shape on the counter — it shouldn't immediately start spreading into a puddle. Practice building tension: drag the dough toward you across an un-floured surface, rotate 90 degrees, repeat. You'll feel the surface tighten under your hands.
  • Dutch oven not hot enough. The first 10 seconds after the dough hits the hot pot are critical — that's when the water in the dough flashes to steam and creates the initial lift. If the pot isn't screaming hot (preheated at 500°F for at least 30 minutes), you lose that burst. Drop the temp to 450°F when you load the bread.
  • Starter used past its peak. If your starter was collapsing when you mixed it into the dough, it was already exhausted. Use it at its dome — active, bubbly, at maximum volume. Feed at the right ratio so it peaks when you're ready to mix dough.

3. Gummy, Wet Crumb — Feels Under-Baked Even Though the Crust Is Dark

What you see: the crust is beautifully browned, maybe even a little dark, but when you cut into the loaf the inside is moist, dense, slightly translucent. The knife comes out with a gummy residue. It's not raw — it's fully cooked — but the texture is wrong.

This one has two causes, and they often happen together:

Cause 1: you didn't let it cool. This is the single most common "problem" that isn't a problem at all — the loaf is fine, you just cut it too soon. Sourdough needs a minimum of 2 hours to cool completely. Ideally 3-4. During cooling, the starch continues to set, the moisture redistributes, and the crumb texture finalizes. Cut into a hot loaf and the steam that should have escaped during cooling condenses inside the crumb and turns it gummy. Every single time. I know it's hard to wait. The smell fills the kitchen. But cutting early ruins the crumb — there's no shortcut around cooling time.

Cause 2: under-baked. Use a thermometer. Sourdough is done when the internal temperature reads 205-210°F. Not 190°F. Not 200°F. 205-210°F. At 190°F the starch hasn't finished gelatinizing. Get an instant-read thermometer — it's $15 and it eliminates the guesswork. If the crust is getting too dark before the center hits temp, your oven is too hot — for temperature conversion between scales and settings help, check the oven temp tool.

4. Too Sour — Bread Tastes Like Vinegar or Is Unpleasantly Tangy

A little tang is the whole point of sourdough. But if your bread makes your jaw tighten and your mouth pucker — that's too much acetic acid, and it comes from fermentation that went too long or too warm.

The fix: shorten the cold proof. Most sourdough recipes call for an overnight cold proof in the fridge (8-16 hours). If your bread is too sour, cut it to 6-8 hours. The longer the cold proof, the more acetic acid the bacteria produce. Also, check your starter's health — an over-acidic starter (one that's been fed infrequently at low ratios) dumps excess acid into the dough. Feed it 1:5:5 or 1:10:10 for 2-3 cycles to reset the acid balance. The starter should smell like yogurt, not vinegar.

Temperature matters too. Warmer fermentation (above 78°F) favors the bacteria over the yeast and produces more acid. If your kitchen is warm, shorten bulk fermentation and proof in a cooler spot.

5. Not Sour Enough — Tastes Like Regular White Bread

The opposite problem. Your starter is alive and the bread rose, but it barely tastes sour. This usually means the fermentation was too fast or too cold — not enough time for the bacteria to produce acid.

The fix: use a longer cold proof (12-16 hours in the fridge). Use less starter in the dough (reduce from 20% to 10-15% of flour weight — less starter means slower fermentation means more flavor development). Feed your starter with some whole wheat or rye flour before bake day — whole grain flours carry more lactic acid bacteria. And stir the hooch back into your starter for a few feedings before you bake — that liquid is concentrated acid and flavor.

6. Burnt Crust, Pale Inside — or the Reverse: Pale Crust, Done Inside

Burnt crust / pale inside: your oven temp is too high, or you're using a dark-colored Dutch oven that absorbs more radiant heat. Reduce the oven temp by 25°F and put a baking sheet on the rack below the Dutch oven to deflect bottom heat. Also check your oven with a separate thermometer — most home ovens lie by 25-50°F.

Pale crust / done inside: not enough steam during the first phase of baking. Steam keeps the crust soft and allows maximum expansion before the crust sets and browns. If you're using a Dutch oven, make sure the lid is on tight for the first 25-30 minutes. If you're baking on a stone, add steam: preheat a cast iron pan on the bottom rack, and when you load the bread, pour a cup of boiling water into the pan and close the door fast. Or spray the loaf with water from a spray bottle right before closing the oven.

7. Scoring Doesn't Open — the Bread Bursts Through the Side or Bottom Instead

The score is a controlled weak point — it tells the expanding dough "open here." When the dough ignores your score and bursts out the side, it's because the score wasn't actually the weakest point. Either the score sealed back up, or the crust set somewhere else first, or the surface tension from shaping was uneven.

The fix, in order of likelihood:

  1. Score deeper. Go ¼ to ½ inch deep. A timid, shallow score doesn't create enough of a weak spot. Use a single confident stroke — a lame or razor blade, not a kitchen knife, which drags and seals the cut.
  2. Score at an angle. Hold the blade at 30-45° to the surface, not straight down. An angled cut creates a flap of dough that lifts during baking, creating that dramatic "ear." A vertical cut just seals back up as the dough expands.
  3. Score with authority. Slow, hesitant cuts are jagged and don't open cleanly. One decisive swipe. Don't saw back and forth.
  4. Check your shaping. If the dough is tighter on one side than the other from uneven shaping, it'll burst at the loosest spot regardless of where you score. Build even surface tension across the whole loaf.

8. Large Tunnels / Holes Just Under the Crust — "Flying Crust"

The crumb looks okay in the center, but right under the top crust there's a horizontal tunnel — a big open gap separating the crust from the crumb. This is called flying crust, and it's a shaping problem: the dough wasn't degassed evenly during shaping, and a large air pocket got trapped right under the surface. During baking, that pocket expanded and lifted the crust off the crumb.

The fix: when you shape, gently press out any very large visible bubbles with your fingertips — not aggressively degassing like you would with sandwich bread, but enough to break up pockets bigger than a grape. Be more thorough at the edges when folding during pre-shaping. The goal is even gas distribution, not the complete removal of gas.

9. Can't Tell if It's Proofed — the Poke Test, Explained Properly

This isn't a "loaf problem" — it's the thing that prevents all the other problems. The poke test is how you know when the shaped loaf is ready to bake, and most descriptions of it online are wrong or incomplete.

How to do it right: flour your index finger. Poke the dough about ½ inch deep — not a tap, a deliberate poke. Then observe:

  • Springs back immediately and completely: under-proofed. The gluten is still tight and hasn't relaxed. Wait 30 minutes and test again.
  • Springs back slowly (2-3 seconds) and leaves a slight indentation: perfectly proofed. The gluten has relaxed just enough. Bake now.
  • Doesn't spring back at all — the dent stays: over-proofed. The gluten has broken down past the point of recovery. Bake it anyway — it won't be your best loaf, but it'll still be bread. Next time, shorten the proof.
  • Dough deflates when you poke it: severely over-proofed. The structure is collapsing. Bake immediately and hope for the best. The loaf will be flat, but it'll taste fine.

Run the poke test on the shaped loaf during the final proof, not during bulk fermentation. The dough should be in its banneton or on the counter in its final shape. For your starter's readiness separately, the float test and consistent doubling are better indicators — see the starter feeding guide for that.

10. Bottom Crust Is Too Thick and Hard — Like a Shoe Sole

The bottom of the loaf is twice as thick as the sides and top, dark brown or nearly black, and hard enough that cutting through it requires serious knife work. This is a Dutch oven problem: the pot sits directly on the oven rack or oven floor, absorbing intense radiant heat from the bottom element, and the bottom of the loaf bears the brunt of it.

The fix, in order of effectiveness:

  1. Put a baking sheet on the rack below the Dutch oven. This deflects the direct radiant heat from the bottom element. It's the simplest fix and works for most people.
  2. Line the bottom of the Dutch oven with parchment, then sprinkle a thin layer of cornmeal or semolina on top of the parchment. The cornmeal acts as insulation between the pot and the dough.
  3. Move the rack up one position. If your Dutch oven is on the lowest rack, the bottom element is cooking it directly. Raise it to the middle rack and the thicker bottom crust usually disappears.
  4. Reduce the oven temperature after loading. Preheat at 500°F, load the bread, reduce to 450°F immediately. The initial high heat creates steam and oven spring; the lower sustained heat prevents the bottom from overcooking.

📋 Sourdough Loaf Troubleshooting Cheat Sheet

Symptom → most likely cause → what to change next bake. Work through ONE change at a time.

  • Dense crumb, heavy: Under-proofed. Let bulk fermentation go longer. Dough should be puffy and jiggly before shaping. Check hydration →
  • Flat loaf, no spring: Over-proofed OR weak shaping. Shorten proof; practice building surface tension.
  • Gummy, wet inside: Cut too soon (wait 2+ hrs) OR under-baked (internal temp must reach 205-210°F).
  • Too sour / vinegary: Cold proof too long OR starter too acidic. Cut cold proof to 6-8 hrs; feed starter 1:5:5 for 2-3 cycles. Starter guide →
  • Not sour enough: Cold proof too short OR too much starter. Extend cold proof to 12-16 hrs; use less starter.
  • Burnt crust / pale crumb: Oven too hot. Verify with separate thermometer; reduce by 25°F; add baking sheet below Dutch oven.
  • Scoring won't open: Score deeper (¼-½ inch), at 30-45° angle, with a sharp lame. One confident stroke.
  • Flying crust (gap under top): Shaping — large air pockets trapped near surface. Press out big bubbles during shaping.
  • Bottom crust too thick: Dutch oven getting too much bottom heat. Baking sheet on rack below; parchment + cornmeal; raise rack.
  • Starter is fine but loaf fails: The problem is fermentation timing, not the starter. Focus on the poke test and bulk fermentation duration. Baker's % calc →

Sourdough is not an exact science — it's a conversation between you, your flour, your water, your starter, and your kitchen. The starter changes with the seasons. The flour absorbs water differently in summer humidity than in winter dryness. The fermentation clock runs faster when the kitchen is 80°F and slower when it's 65°F. Recipes are starting points, not guarantees. The skill — and it is a skill, learned through repetition and failure — is learning to read the dough. The poke test. The jiggle. The way the surface feels under your hand. The way the shaped loaf holds itself. These are signals the dough sends you. Your job is to learn the language.

If you're on your tenth loaf and it still isn't right, you're not failing — you're calibrating. Every "bad" loaf teaches you something that makes the next one better. The baker who has never made a flat, dense, gummy, ugly loaf is a baker who has never baked sourdough. Keep going. The 50th loaf is better than the 10th, and the 100th is better than the 50th. The learning curve is long and gentle, and the only way through it is to bake another loaf.
