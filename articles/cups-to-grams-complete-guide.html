How to Convert Cups to Grams — The Complete Baking Measurement Guide

Every cups-to-grams conversion you'll ever need — butter, flour, sugar, liquids, tablespoons, teaspoons. One page. No hunting. Built from real kitchen testing, not textbook theory.

Here's the thing about baking: cups are fast but grams are honest.

A cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 115 to 165 grams depending on how you fill it. A cup of butter is always 227 grams — until you melt it, and then it's not. A cup of packed brown sugar and a cup of granulated sugar are off by 20 grams from each other.

This page is the reference I built for myself after one too many dense cakes and flat cookies. It covers every common baking ingredient, with the exact gram weights I use in my own kitchen, backed by the USDA database and cross-checked against King Arthur Flour's published standards.

Each section links to a dedicated calculator where you can punch in your exact amount — half a cup, three tablespoons, 200 grams going the other direction — and get a precise number without doing any math. 🧈 Butter: Cups ⇄ Grams

Butter is the easiest conversion in baking — and also the one with the most traps for the unwary. Let's do the easy part first, then the gotchas.

The baseline: solid stick butter

1 US cup of solid butter = 227 grams = 2 sticks = 16 tablespoons. This is the number stamped into every American butter wrapper and confirmed by USDA FoodData Central. It does not vary by brand — Land O'Lakes, Kerrygold, store brand, all the same within a gram or two.

From that one number, everything else derives:

  • ½ cup butter = 113 grams — exactly 1 stick, 8 tablespoons. This is the most common butter measurement in cookie recipes.
  • ¼ cup butter = 57 grams — half a stick, 4 tablespoons. Common for small-batch baking.
  • 1 tablespoon butter = 14.2 grams — derived from 227 ÷ 16. Our tablespoon calculator lets you switch between unsalted, salted, and European-style butter because the density shifts by about 1% per type.
  • 1 cup butter = 227 grams — the anchor. Two sticks, sixteen tablespoons, one full US cup.

The gotcha: melted butter is denser

Solid butter has air pockets. Melted butter doesn't. If you melt 1 stick (113 g solid) and pour it into a ½ cup measure, it won't reach the ½ cup line — because melted butter packs more fat into the same volume. A full ½ cup of already-melted butter weighs about 120 grams, not 113.

Our butter converter has a dropdown for stick, melted, and whipped butter so you don't have to remember this.

Does butter type matter?

For most home baking: no. The difference between unsalted (227 g/cup), salted (~229 g/cup), and European-style 82%+ butterfat (~225 g/cup) is about 2 grams per cup — invisible in a batch of cookies. But if you're scaling a commercial recipe or developing one for publication, those 2 grams compound. Our tablespoon butter calculator accounts for all three types.

🌾 Flour: Cups ⇄ Grams

Flour is where cups-to-grams conversion stops being academic and starts being the difference between a tender cake and a doorstop.

The problem with flour and cups

Unlike butter, flour is compressible. A "cup" of all-purpose flour can weigh anywhere from 115 grams (sifted, spooned loosely) to 165 grams (scooped directly from a firmly packed bag). That's a 43% range. No other common baking ingredient varies this much by measuring technique.

The standard that American recipe developers use: 1 cup all-purpose flour = 120–125 grams, measured by the spoon-and-level method. (If you're not familiar with spoon-and-level, read our flour measuring guide — it's a 15-second technique that fixes more baking problems than any piece of equipment.)

Flour type changes the weight

Different flours have different densities. Protein content affects how the particles pack:

  • All-purpose flour: 120–125 g per cup — the workhorse. 120g if you're a strict spoon-and-leveler, 125g if you're using King Arthur's standard.
  • Bread flour: 127–130 g per cup — higher protein (12–14%) means denser particles. Most bakers use 130g.
  • Cake flour: 110–115 g per cup — lower protein (7–9%), finer mill, lighter per cup. Use 110g if sifted, 115g if spooned.
  • Whole wheat flour: 120–130 g per cup — the bran flakes create irregular packing. Split the difference at 125g unless your recipe specifies.

Our flour cups-to-grams calculator has a dropdown for all four types so you get the right number instantly.

The half-cup and quarter-cup benchmarks

Most home bakers don't need a full cup. Here are the most-requested amounts:

  • ½ cup flour = ~60–63 grams (all-purpose, spooned-and-leveled)
  • ¼ cup flour = ~30–31 grams — common for thickening a sauce or dusting a work surface

Going the other way: grams → cups

If your recipe is in grams and you only have measuring cups:

  • 100g flour to cups ≈ ¾ cup + 1 tbsp (all-purpose)
  • 150g flour to cups ≈ 1¼ cups
  • 200g flour to cups ≈ 1⅔ cups
  • 250g flour to cups ≈ 2 cups
  • 300g flour to cups ≈ 2½ cups
  • 400g flour to cups ≈ 3⅓ cups
  • 500g flour to cups ≈ 4 cups + 2 tbsp

🍚 Sugar: Cups ⇄ Grams

Sugar is simpler than flour but more complicated than butter — because "sugar" in baking means at least three different ingredients with three different densities.

Granulated white sugar

1 cup granulated sugar = 200 grams. This is the most consistent sugar measurement in baking. Sugar crystals are heavy, uniform, and don't compact significantly — scooping straight from the bag adds maybe 5% extra, not the 30% you get with flour. Most recipes are forgiving enough that this doesn't matter.

  • ½ cup granulated sugar = 100 grams
  • 1 tablespoon granulated sugar = 12.5 grams

Brown sugar (light or dark — packed)

1 cup packed brown sugar ≈ 220 grams. Most recipes that call for brown sugar explicitly say "packed" — and they mean it. Press the sugar firmly into the cup until it holds its shape when turned out. The molasses content makes brown sugar denser and sticker than granulated; that's what "packing" leverages.

  • 100g brown sugar to cups ≈ scant ½ cup (packed)
  • 150g brown sugar to cups ≈ ⅔ cup
  • 200g brown sugar to cups ≈ ¾ cup + 2 tbsp
  • 250g brown sugar to cups ≈ 1 cup + 2 tbsp

Powdered sugar (confectioners' / icing sugar)

1 cup powdered sugar ≈ 120 grams — unsifted. Sifted powdered sugar is lighter (~100 g/cup) because you're intentionally aerating it. Most recipes mean unsifted unless they say "sifted powdered sugar."

  • 100g powdered sugar to cups ≈ ¾ cup + 1 tbsp
  • 150g powdered sugar to cups ≈ 1¼ cups
  • 200g powdered sugar to cups ≈ 1⅔ cups

The sugar swap that changes everything

Granulated and brown sugar are not interchangeable by weight. A recipe that calls for "1 cup white sugar + 1 cup brown sugar" by volume is actually 200 g + 220 g — a 20-gram imbalance that shifts the cookie's spread, chewiness, and browning. 💧 Liquids: Fluid Ounces ⇄ Milliliters

Fluid ounces and weight ounces are not the same thing — but your measuring cup doesn't know that, and most American recipes treat them interchangeably. Here's the actual conversion:

1 US fluid ounce = 29.5735 milliliters. For practical kitchen use, round to 29.6 mL. Over a full cup (8 fl oz), the rounding error is about 0.2 mL — invisible in any recipe.

Common fluid ounce conversions

  • 1 oz to mL = 29.6 mL — a single shot or a tablespoon-plus-a-teaspoon
  • 2 oz to mL = 59.1 mL — a standard espresso double shot
  • 4 oz to mL = 118.3 mL — half a US cup
  • 8 oz to mL = 236.6 mL — one full US cup
  • 16 oz to mL = 473.2 mL — one US pint (2 cups)
  • 32 oz to mL = 946.4 mL — one US quart (4 cups)

Does the liquid type matter for oz → mL?

No — fluid ounces measure volume, not weight. 1 fl oz of water, milk, cream, and oil all occupy the same volume (29.57 mL). But their weight in grams is different because density varies. We have separate calculators for each so you can convert fluid ounces to milliliters with the right context:

  • Water — 10 fl oz = 296 mL ≈ 296 g (water is 1 g/mL at room temp)
  • Milk — 10 fl oz = 296 mL ≈ 305 g (milk is ~1.03 g/mL)
  • Heavy cream — 10 fl oz = 296 mL ≈ 295 g (cream is ~0.99 g/mL)
  • Vegetable oil — 10 fl oz = 296 mL ≈ 272 g (oil is ~0.92 g/mL)

Notice the 24-gram gap between milk and oil at the same volume — that's why weighing liquids matters for large-batch baking.

🥄 Tablespoons & Teaspoons ⇄ Grams

Spoon conversions are where precision matters most — because a small measurement error as a percentage of the total is huge. Being off by 1 gram on a teaspoon of baking powder (4.6 g) is a 22% error. The same 1-gram error on a cup of flour (120 g) is 0.8% — invisible.

Butter by the spoonful

1 tablespoon butter = 14.2 grams (unsalted standard US). This is 227 ÷ 16 — pure math from the cup weight. Salted butter adds roughly 0.1 g per tablespoon; European-style subtracts about 0.1 g. Our calculator switches between all three.

American butter wrappers have tablespoon markings printed on them, which is brilliant design — but if you're using block butter or a tub, the tablespoon converter is faster than eyeballing.

Flour by the spoonful

1 tablespoon all-purpose flour = 7.5 grams (120 g/cup ÷ 16 tbsp). This assumes spoon-and-level measuring — if you're scooping a heaped tablespoon from the bag, it's closer to 9–10 grams. For small amounts like this, a scale is infinitely better than volume. See our flour measuring guide for the full breakdown.

Sugar by the spoonful

1 tablespoon granulated sugar = 12.5 grams. Brown sugar (packed): ~13.8 g/tbsp. Powdered sugar (unsifted): ~7.5 g/tbsp.

Leaveners — the most measurement-sensitive ingredients in your kitchen

1 teaspoon baking powder = 4.6 grams. Baking soda: also ~4.6 g per teaspoon. But here's the thing: a "teaspoon" from your flatware drawer is NOT a measuring teaspoon — it can be anywhere from 2.5 to 7 mL. If you're off by even half a gram on baking powder, your cake can collapse (too much) or stay dense (too little). See why cakes sink in the middle for the full leavening explainer.

Other common teaspoon conversions:

  • Cream of tartar: 1 tsp ≈ 3.1 g
  • Ground cinnamon: 1 tsp ≈ 2.8 g
  • Table salt: 1 tsp ≈ 5.7 g (note: kosher salt is lighter per teaspoon because of the flake shape — about 3–5 g/tsp depending on brand)

Frequently Asked Questions

Why don't American recipes use grams?

Historical momentum. US cookbooks standardized on cups in the early 20th century (Fannie Farmer, 1896) and never switched. Cups are faster when you're scooping and pouring — but they introduce variability that grams eliminate. The trend is shifting: most serious American baking blogs now publish gram weights alongside cups, and virtually all professional kitchens use metric. If you bake more than once a month, a $15 kitchen scale pays for itself in avoided failures.

Is 1 cup always 240 mL?

In US customary: yes — 1 cup = 8 fl oz = 236.6 mL, but most recipes round to 240 mL. In metric countries, a "metric cup" = 250 mL (common in Australia and New Zealand). In the UK, recipes typically use weight (grams) or milliliters rather than cups. If you're converting a recipe from a non-US source, check which "cup" the author is using. For our calculators, 1 US cup = 240 mL.

Should I weigh or use cups?

Weigh if you can. Cups if you must. The hierarchy: scale > spoon-and-level cups > scooping cups > eyeballing. For forgiving recipes (pancakes, muffins, quick breads), cups are fine. For precision work (macarons, laminated dough, scaled commercial recipes), a scale is non-negotiable. Our converters exist for the gap between "I have a recipe in cups" and "I want it in grams" — use whichever direction your recipe requires.

Do these conversions work at high altitude?

The gram weights don't change — 227 g of butter is 227 g of butter at sea level, in Denver, or on a mountaintop. What changes at altitude is how ingredients behave (leaveners expand faster, liquids evaporate sooner, sugar concentrates differently). If you're baking above 3,000 feet, keep the gram weights the same but reduce leavening by 15–20% and increase liquid slightly. Our baking powder calculator helps with the adjusted amounts.
