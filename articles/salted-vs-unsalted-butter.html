Salted vs Unsalted Butter: When It Matters and How to Swap

A recipe says unsalted, the fridge holds salted, and the question is whether that matters. For most of what comes out of a home oven it does not, and the fix is a quarter teaspoon. For four or five specific things it decides the batch. The difference between those two groups is not opinion — it is a number, and the number is smaller than the internet usually implies.

⚡ Quick Answer: Swap One for One, Then Cut the Salt

The swap: use the same weight of butter, and reduce the salt your recipe lists by ¼ tsp per stick (113 g). Measured, a stick holds about 0.3 tsp, so ¼ is deliberately a little short.

The number: salted butter is 643 mg sodium per 100 g; unsalted is 11 mg. That is the entire difference — the fat, water and protein are the same.

Fine to swap: cookies, brownies, bars, muffins, banana bread, spice cake, pie crust, sandwich bread.

Buy unsalted for: buttercream and frosting, caramel, shortbread and butter cookies, pound cake, plain sponge, anything where butter is the flavour rather than an ingredient.

If your salt is Diamond Crystal kosher rather than table salt, everything below is off by more than double — see the salt-type section before you measure anything.

I keep both kinds in the fridge, and for years I treated the distinction as a formality: salt is salt, the recipe adds its own, and the difference is a rounding error. Then I made the same buttercream twice, once with salted butter and the salt left out and once with unsalted butter and the salt left in, and tasted them side by side. Same sugar, same cream, same vanilla. One tasted like frosting. The other tasted like frosting on a pretzel.

The reason is that a stick of salted butter is not lightly seasoned. It carries roughly 1.6 percent salt by weight, which is about a third of a teaspoon, and a typical buttercream for a two-layer cake might call for an eighth of a teaspoon total. You are not adding a pinch to the recipe. You are adding two or three times the salt the recipe wanted, and it arrives pre-dissolved in the fat where you cannot taste it until the sugar is already in.

The short version: for anything with a crumb and a crust, salted butter is fine and one subtraction fixes it. For anything where butter is the entire flavour and nothing bakes the salt away, buy unsalted. Everything below is the arithmetic, what salt is doing in a batter besides seasoning it, and how to read the figure off your own box instead of trusting a range from a blog.

#1 The Only Real Difference Is Salt, and It Is Not a Small One

Both are churned cream. US federal standard for butter, 21 CFR 131.155, requires at least 80 percent milkfat and describes the product as made from milk or cream “with or without common salt.” The salted and unsalted versions are held to the same fat minimum, the same moisture ceiling and the same grading. Nothing in the manufacturing changes when salt goes in except the salt.

USDA's own nutrient records make that concrete. In the SR Legacy database, “Butter, salted” and “Butter, without salt” both list 81.1 g of fat and 15.9 g of water per 100 g. The sodium line is where they part: 643 mg against 11 mg, a sixty-fold gap produced by about 1.6 g of salt per 100 g.

So the difference is real but narrow. Salted butter is not a different ingredient, it is the same ingredient with a seasoning already built in. That framing matters because it tells you what the swap can and cannot fix. Swapping the butter changes the salt, and only the salt. It does not change the melting point, the creaming behaviour, how the fat coats flour, or how the butter browns, because all of those are functions of the butterfat and the butterfat is identical.

The one small exception, which gets its own section later, is water. Salt is a preservative, so manufacturers can leave a fraction more water in salted butter without shortening its shelf life, and some do. It is a difference of a gram or two per stick. In a cake it is invisible. In shortbread it is not.

#2 What One Stick Actually Carries

Running the USDA figures through the units you actually use is the fastest way to see why bakers bother. A stick is 113 g, a cup is 227 g, a tablespoon is 14.2 g, and a pound block is 454 g. Salt is sodium multiplied by 2.54; table salt runs about 6.1 g per teaspoon, which is 2,325 mg of sodium.

Amount of salted butterWeightSodiumSaltEquals
1 tablespoon 14 g 91 mg 0.23 g A heavy pinch
1 stick (½ cup, 8 tbsp) 113 g 727 mg 1.85 g 0.3 tsp
1 cup (2 sticks) 227 g 1,459 mg 3.7 g 0.6 tsp
1 pound block (4 sticks) 454 g 2,919 mg 7.4 g 1.2 tsp

Read the last column against what recipes ask for. A batch of chocolate chip cookies calling for a cup of butter typically lists half a teaspoon of salt separately. If that cup is salted, it has already delivered 0.6, and the recipe's half teaspoon would take the total to 1.1 — more than double. That is the whole problem in one line, and it is why the fix is always a subtraction rather than a substitution.

The 0.3 teaspoon figure is worth pausing on, because most sources round it to a quarter. They round down on purpose. A recipe writer would rather you end up marginally under-salted than over, since salt can be added at the table and cannot be removed from a batter. If you want to be exact, take out a rounded third of a teaspoon per stick; if you want to be practical, take out a quarter and taste the dough.

#3 Read Your Own Box Instead of the Average

Everything above describes a national average, and averages hide the range that makes this question annoying. Salted butter legally has no fixed salt content. Brands add what they add, and the figure is not printed on the wrapper in any unit a baker can use. What is printed, by law, is the nutrition panel, and it gives you sodium per tablespoon.

Three steps turn that into a number you can act on:

  1. Find sodium per 1 tbsp (14 g) on the panel. Most salted butters land between 80 and 100 mg.
  2. Multiply by 8 to get the sodium in a stick, since a stick is eight tablespoons.
  3. Divide by 2,325 — the milligrams of sodium in a teaspoon of table salt — to get teaspoons per stick. The shortcut is sodium per tablespoon ÷ 300, which is close enough to work in your head.

A box reading 90 mg per tablespoon works out to 720 mg per stick and 0.31 teaspoons, which is the average stick. A “lightly salted” butter at 45 mg per tablespoon comes to 0.15 teaspoons per stick, half the usual — you would cut an eighth of a teaspoon, not a quarter. And a European-style cultured butter that reads 60 mg per tablespoon is at 0.2 teaspoons per stick. Those three boxes differ by more than a factor of two, and no recipe can be written to accommodate all of them.

One caveat on the panel. Unsalted butter usually reads 0 to 10 mg of sodium per serving, and that is not added salt — it is the sodium naturally present in cream. Leave it out of your arithmetic. Ten milligrams is 0.004 teaspoons, which is below the resolution of any measuring spoon you own.

#4 Does Salted Butter Weigh More?

Effectively no, and this trips people up when they switch between converters. A stick of commercial salted butter is 113 g and a stick of unsalted is 113 g. The salt is inside that weight, dissolved in the butter's water phase, having displaced a little water and a little fat. A cup of either is about 227 g.

The 229 g figure you will see for salted butter in some tables — including the dropdown on our butter gram converter — is not a measurement of a commercial stick. It models a different question: what a cup weighs if you take unsalted butter and salt it to the same level yourself. That adds mass, because you are adding something to the 227 g rather than swapping something within it. The two answers describe two different kitchens, and both are correct.

Practically, the gap is under one percent and never changes a bake. If you are weighing — and for butter you should be, since a cup scooped from a soft stick can run 10 to 15 g heavy — the weight of the butter is the same either way and the only adjustment you make is to the salt. #5 What That Salt Does Beyond Seasoning

If salt only affected taste, this would be a one-paragraph article. It does a few other jobs, and they explain why the same substitution is invisible in one recipe and obvious in another.

It changes how you perceive sweetness. Sodium suppresses bitterness and rounds off acidity, which is why a pinch of salt makes chocolate taste more like chocolate and less like cocoa powder. In a dark chocolate brownie, salted butter and a reduced salt addition is not a compromise — it is often the better version, because the salt is landing where it does the most work.

It tightens gluten and slows fermentation. Salt pulls water toward the flour proteins and makes a bread dough feel stronger and less extensible. It also holds back yeast osmotically, which is why a dough with the right amount of salt ferments steadily rather than running away. Both effects are dose-dependent, and both are why bread formulas specify salt as a percentage of flour — usually around 2 percent. If you bake sandwich bread with salted butter and simply omit the recipe's salt, you are close to the intended figure, not over it, because bread carries far more salt than cake does. The mechanics are covered in baker's percentage.

It affects tenderness in pastry. In a pie crust or shortbread, salt interferes with gluten development enough that some bakers notice a slightly shorter, more crumbly result from an all-salted batch. Whether that reads as an improvement depends on what you wanted. In a crust destined for fruit, shorter is usually good.

It is loud in fat and sugar with nothing to hide behind. This is the effect that ruins batches. Salt dissolved in butterfat and then whipped into sugar is tasted immediately and completely, with no crumb, crust or bake time to blunt it. A cake batter spreads its salt through a large volume of flour and egg. A buttercream does not.

#6 Where the Swap Is Invisible

These are the recipes where you should not make a second trip to the shop. Use the salted butter, subtract the salt, and move on.

What you are bakingWhy the swap worksWhat to do
Chocolate chip and drop cookies Chocolate, brown sugar and vanilla all carry salt well, and the bake drives off moisture so the seasoning concentrates less than you would expect. Omit the recipe salt entirely; the butter has replaced it.
Brownies and bar cookies Dense, chocolate-heavy, and usually under-salted to begin with. A little extra reads as intentional. Cut the salt in half rather than omitting it.
Muffins and quick breads The crumb is coarse and the batter is wet, so salt disperses evenly through a large volume. Omit the added salt.
Spice cake, carrot cake, gingerbread Cinnamon, ginger and clove are strong enough to absorb a small shift in salinity without it reading as salt. Omit or halve the added salt.
Pie and tart crust Salt makes pastry marginally shorter, which most fruit pies benefit from. Halve the added salt; taste a scrap of raw dough.
Sandwich bread and rolls Bread formulas carry far more salt than cake — near 2 percent of flour weight — so a stick's worth is a small fraction of the total. Calculate rather than guess: weigh the butter, subtract its salt, keep the dough at 2 percent.

The pattern is that volume and competing flavours both buy you tolerance. A wet batter spreads salt through 800 g of ingredients. A crust has a strong competing flavour in the filling. What you are looking for is a place for the salt to go.

Cookies deserve the specific note, because they are where the swap most often goes wrong in the other direction. If you use salted butter and keep the recipe's salt, you push a batch from pleasantly seasoned to noticeably salty, and salt also affects how much the dough spreads. If your cookies are suddenly pooling thin, spread is usually a butter-temperature problem rather than a salt one, but the two get confused constantly.

#7 Where It Shows Up and Ruins the Batch

Short list, and it is worth memorising because these are the batches people bring to other people's houses.

Buttercream and frosting. This is the big one. A Swiss or American buttercream is butter, sugar and a little liquid, whipped into an emulsion. There is no flour, no bake, nothing to dilute the salt. A stick of salted butter per 250 g of sugar puts the frosting at roughly double the salt a good recipe intends, and the result tastes savoury in a way that reads as a mistake rather than as a choice. Salted butter also brings its extra water, which can make the emulsion looser — and if it breaks, the repair is a separate job.

Caramel and salted caramel sauce. Sugar cookery is the one place where salt is doing chemistry as well as flavouring. Commercial salted butter holds its salt dissolved in the water phase, so it will not seed crystallisation the way dry salt grains can if you tip them into a syrup. But it does bring extra water into a mixture whose whole success depends on the water boiling off at the right rate, and it delivers salt you did not plan for into a sauce where the salt is the headline. Use unsalted and add flaked salt at the end, where you can taste as you go. The crystallisation failure mode is covered in why caramel crystallizes.

Shortbread and butter cookies. Four ingredients, one of which is butter. There is nothing to mask a shift in salinity, and the extra water in salted butter loosens a dough that is supposed to be short and snappy. This is the clearest case in the whole article: buy unsalted.

Pound cake and plain sponge. The crumb is fine and the flavour is butter, eggs and sugar. Salt reads as a sharp edge on the finish. In a chocolate cake the same amount disappears. That is not inconsistency in the advice, it is the cocoa doing its job.

Anything you cannot taste before it bakes. This is the general rule behind the list. You can taste cookie dough and correct it. You cannot taste a cake batter for salt once the sugar is in, you definitely cannot taste a baked sponge before serving it to guests, and you cannot un-salt a batter. Where the correction is unavailable, use the ingredient that leaves you in control.

#8 The Swap, in Units You Can Measure

If you are using salted butter and want to match what the recipe intended, subtract from the added salt. Round down — being slightly under is recoverable.

Two notes on reading it. First, the middle column is what the butter contributes, not what the recipe needs — if the recipe lists no salt at all, there is nothing to subtract and you simply accept the butter's seasoning. Second, going the other way is not symmetrical in practice: converting a salted-butter recipe to unsalted means adding about a quarter teaspoon per stick, but the total will land under the original because the commercial stick was never exactly 0.3.

When you have more than four sticks in play — a big batch of cookie dough, a wedding cake — do the arithmetic instead of the table. Weigh the butter, multiply grams by 0.016 to get grams of salt, divide by 6.1 to get teaspoons. A 680 g batch of butter carries about 10.9 g of salt, which is 1.8 teaspoons. That number is worth writing down before you start, because it is the one you will want when you are three quarters through and cannot taste anything reliably. If you are scaling a formula up, the recipe scaler will keep the salt proportional for you.

#9 Which Salt You Are Subtracting

This is where the arithmetic quietly breaks, and it breaks badly. Teaspoon measurements of salt are not interchangeable, because the crystals are different sizes and a teaspoon is a volume.

SaltGrams per tspTeaspoons to equal 1.85 g
Fine table salt6.1 g0.30
Morton kosher salt4.8 g0.39
Diamond Crystal kosher salt2.8 g0.66
Fine sea salt5.5–6.0 g0.31–0.34

Read the last column. If your recipe was written with table salt and you are subtracting with Diamond Crystal, a quarter teaspoon removes less than half of what the butter brought — you will end up with a batch that tastes flat, and you will conclude the swap does not work. It did work. The subtraction was just measured in the wrong crystal size.

The fix is to weigh salt whenever the amount matters, which is any batch above two sticks or any formula you intend to repeat. A scale settles it in one step and removes an argument that has been running in baking books for fifty years. If you are weighing, ignore this section entirely: 1.85 g is 1.85 g in any crystal.

#10 The Second-Order Effect: Water

Salt is a preservative, and that has a quiet consequence. Because salted butter resists spoilage slightly better, a manufacturer can leave a fraction more water in it without shortening its shelf life, and many do. The difference is one or two grams per stick — under two percent of the weight.

Butter is roughly 80 percent fat and 16 to 18 percent water, and that water is not filler. It turns to steam in the oven, which is what makes laminated doughs separate into layers and what gives scones and biscuits their flake. It also counts as liquid in the formula, which is why 227 g of butter carries about 36 g of water that a recipe written for shortening never accounts for.

In a cake, one extra gram of water is invisible. In shortbread, where the fat to water ratio is the structure, it is the difference between a dough that holds a snap and one that spreads. In a laminated dough it changes how the butter block behaves when it is rolled. Those are the two places where I would not substitute, and the reason is water rather than salt.

There is a related effect on freshness. Salted butter sits on shelves and in warehouses longer, because salt masks the funky notes that develop as butter ages and slows the process that produces them. That is not a defect of salted butter, but it does mean that a stick of it is statistically older than the stick next to it. King Arthur's test kitchen makes this exact point when explaining why their recipes are developed on unsalted: it is not only about salt, it is also about knowing the butter is fresh.

#11 Which One to Buy, and How to Keep Both

The practical answer is both, and they are not interchangeable in the roles people actually use butter for.

Unsalted for baking, by the block. It is a known quantity, it is fresher, and it lets every formula control salt on its own terms. If you bake weekly, buy a pound block rather than sticks — it is cheaper per gram and you are weighing anyway.

Salted for the table. Toast, corn, pancakes, a slice of warm bread. This is what salted butter is for, and no recipe math applies because there is no recipe.

Storage. Both keep best wrapped and cold. Salted butter will hold a little longer in the fridge — a month or two past the unsalted version's comfortable window — but neither is a long-term proposition at room temperature. If you keep a butter dish out, keep it small and refill it, and if you are freezing butter, freeze it unsalted or use it for cooking afterwards, because the salt accelerates the off-flavours that freezing is supposed to prevent. The temperature question matters more than the salted one for how butter creams.

One buying note that saves money: “European-style” and “cultured” describe the fat and the culture, not the salt. A cultured butter at 82 to 86 percent fat behaves differently from a standard 80 percent butter in pastry, and it can be either salted or not. Read the panel, not the word on the front. And if you are making creamed butter and sugar the fat percentage will change the aeration more than a quarter teaspoon of salt ever will.

#12 A Decision Rule You Can Use at the Fridge

Three questions, in order. They resolve nearly every case without arithmetic.

Does the recipe list salt separately? If yes, the recipe was written for unsalted butter and there is a knob to turn. Use the salted butter, subtract a quarter teaspoon per stick, and you are done.

Is butter the flavour? Buttercream, caramel, shortbread, pound cake, plain sponge, a butter sauce. If the answer is yes, buy unsalted. The salt has nowhere to go and you cannot taste the mixture before it is finished.

Can you taste it before it bakes? Cookie dough, pie dough, bread dough — yes. Cake batter once sugar is in — no. Where you can taste, salted butter is a small risk and a small fix. Where you cannot, it is a decision you cannot undo.

And the fallback for everything else: swap one for one by weight, remove a quarter teaspoon of salt per stick, round down, and write on the recipe what you did. The next time you bake it you will know whether the batch wanted that quarter teaspoon back, and that is worth more than any table — including this one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you use salted butter instead of unsalted?

Yes, in almost everything. Use the same weight of butter and reduce the salt the recipe lists by about a quarter teaspoon per stick. A stick carries roughly 0.3 teaspoon of salt measured, but a quarter teaspoon is the measure most kitchens own and it errs on the side of under-salting, which is the recoverable direction. The exceptions are buttercream, caramel, shortbread and plain sponge, where salt has nowhere to hide.

How much salt is in a stick of salted butter?

USDA lists salted butter at 643 mg of sodium per 100 g and unsalted at 11 mg. A stick is 113 g, so it carries about 727 mg of sodium against 12 mg in the unsalted version. Salt is sodium multiplied by 2.54, which puts one stick at roughly 1.85 g of salt, or just under a third of a teaspoon of fine table salt. That is about 1.6 percent of the stick's weight, and it is the figure behind the 1.5 to 2 percent range quoted for salted butter generally.

Does salted butter weigh more than unsalted?

By volume, no. A cup of either is about 227 g and a stick of either is 113 g, because the salt in commercial salted butter sits inside that weight rather than on top of it, and it displaces a little water and butterfat. Some converters, including ours, list 229 g for a cup of salted butter to model what happens when you salt unsalted butter yourself. The gap is under one percent and will not change a bake.

Is salted butter bad for baking?

No. Bakers reach for unsalted butter because it is a known quantity, not because salt damages anything. Salt levels differ between brands and are not printed on the wrapper, so a recipe built around unsalted butter plus a measured half teaspoon of salt cannot be reproduced exactly with a stick of unknown salt content. Salted butter also holds a touch more water, and it keeps longer on the shelf, which means it is sometimes older. Those are reasons for caution, not reasons to avoid it.

What if I only have salted butter and no more salt to cut?

For cookies, brownies, quick breads and muffins, nothing. If the recipe already called for salt, simply leave it out; the butter has replaced it. For something salt-sensitive like buttercream, you can dilute by making a larger batch, add more unsalted fat such as cream cheese or heavy cream, or lean the flavour toward chocolate, coffee or citrus, all of which carry salt well. Do not try to remove salt. There is no way to take it back out of a batter.

🧈 The Butter Check, In Four Questions

Answer these before you unwrap anything. The first one that fails is the one to act on.

  1. Does the recipe list salt as its own ingredient? Then it was written for unsalted butter and the swap is a subtraction, not a substitution.
  2. Is there flour, cocoa or spice in the bowl? Those dilute salt. Sugar and fat alone do not.
  3. Do I know this box's sodium per tablespoon? Multiply by 8, then divide by 300. That is teaspoons of salt per stick, and it takes ten seconds.
  4. Am I about to taste it before it bakes? If not, and the answer to question two was no, go and buy unsalted.

If all four pass: swap one for one by weight, remove a quarter teaspoon per stick, and round down. That single move covers cookies, bars, muffins, quick breads, pie crust and sandwich bread — which is most of what a home oven does.

If you take one thing from this article: the difference between salted and unsalted butter is 632 mg of sodium per 100 g and nothing else. That makes the swap a subtraction — a quarter teaspoon per stick — and it makes the decision a simple one: subtract where there is flour to hide in, buy unsalted where there is not.

Second thing: the quarter teaspoon is rounded down on purpose. A stick measures closer to a third, and every honest table you will find rounds it the same way, because over-salting is the mistake you cannot fix.

Third, if the batch is already in the tin: nothing you do now will remove salt. Bake it, note what happened, and buy a pound block of unsalted for next time. It is the cheapest correction in baking.
