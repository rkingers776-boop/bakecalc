# Baker's Percentage Explained for Home Bakers — The One Ratio That Unlocks Every Recipe

If you've ever looked at a bread recipe that says "75% hydration" and closed the tab, you're not alone. Baker's percentage looks like math homework. It's actually the most useful tool in baking — and it's simpler than measuring cups.

Once you understand it, you can: scale any recipe to any size, compare recipes at a glance, troubleshoot dough that's too dry or too wet, and invent your own recipes from scratch.

Here it is, in plain English.

---

## The one rule: flour is always 100%

In baker's percentage, every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the **total flour weight** — not the total dough weight.

Flour is always 100%. Everything else is relative to that.

That's it. That's the whole system. The rest is just examples.

---

## A dead-simple example

You have:
- 500g flour
- 350g water
- 10g salt
- 5g yeast

Baker's percentages:
- Flour: 500g → **100%** (always)
- Water: 350 / 500 = 0.70 → **70%**
- Salt: 10 / 500 = 0.02 → **2%**
- Yeast: 5 / 500 = 0.01 → **1%**

This is a 70% hydration dough with 2% salt and 1% yeast. A baker anywhere in the world can read that and know exactly what the dough will feel like — wet and slack (70% is on the high side for beginner breads).

The total of all percentages? 100 + 70 + 2 + 1 = 173%. That doesn't add to 100%, and it's not supposed to. Baker's percentage is not parts-of-a-whole. It's "everything relative to flour."

---

## Why this is so useful

### 1. Scale anything to any size

Someone gives you a recipe in baker's percentages. You want to make exactly enough dough for two 9-inch pizzas — maybe 600g of dough total. How much of each ingredient?

First, figure out your flour weight.

Total dough ≈ flour × (1 + sum of percentages/100). If the recipe is 100% flour, 65% water, 2% salt, 1% yeast:

Total percentage factor = 1 + (65 + 2 + 1) / 100 = 1.68

Flour = 600 / 1.68 = **357g** (round to 355g for sanity)

Now everything else:
- Water: 355 × 0.65 = **231g**
- Salt: 355 × 0.02 = **7g**
- Yeast: 355 × 0.01 = **3.5g**

Done. Perfectly scaled.

### 2. Compare recipes instantly

Recipe A: 500g flour, 300g water → 60% hydration → stiffer dough, tighter crumb, good for bagels.
Recipe B: 500g flour, 400g water → 80% hydration → wet dough, open crumb, good for ciabatta.

You can tell at a glance that Recipe B will be wetter and harder to handle, without ever making either one.

### 3. Troubleshoot by feel

You're making dough and it feels too dry. The recipe has 60% hydration. Now you know: for this style of bread, try 65% next time. Or add 25g of water right now (5% × 500g flour = 25g).

Without baker's percentage, you'd just think "I'll add more water next time" — with no idea how much.

## Common hydration ranges (what they mean)

This is for wheat-based breads. Enriched doughs (with butter, eggs, milk) behave differently.

| Hydration | Dough feel | Typical for... |
|---|---|---|
| 50–57% | Stiff, dry | Bagels, pretzels, hard rolls |
| 58–65% | Firm, manageable | Sandwich bread, pizza dough, soft rolls |
| 65–75% | Tacky, stretchy | Baguettes, rustic loaves, most artisan bread |
| 75–85% | Wet, sticky, hard to handle | Ciabatta, focaccia, sourdough boules |
| 85%+ | Very wet, almost batter-like | Pan de cristal, some sourdoughs (advanced) |

Higher hydration = more open crumb structure, thinner crisper crust, faster staling (counterintuitively). Lower hydration = tighter crumb, softer crust that stays soft longer.

## What about enriched doughs?

Butter, eggs, milk, and sugar all add liquid to the dough, but they're not water. Most bakers still express them as percentages of flour weight — they just don't count them toward "hydration."

A brioche might be:
- 100% flour
- 50% butter
- 30% eggs
- 10% sugar
- 30% water
- 2% salt
- 1.5% yeast

The *water* hydration is 30% (very low), but the dough will feel soft and wet because butter and eggs are also liquid at dough temperature. You learn to read the whole formula, not just the water number.

## Baker's percentage for non-bread baking

The system was invented for bread, but it works for anything:

**Cake (simple pound cake):**
- 100% flour
- 100% butter
- 100% sugar
- 100% eggs
- 2% vanilla extract
- 1.5% baking powder
- 1% salt

Equal weights of flour, butter, sugar, eggs — that's literally why it's called a pound cake. Baker's percentage just formalizes the ratio.

**Cookies (basic chocolate chip):**
- 100% flour
- 70% butter (softened)
- 65% sugar (mix of brown + white)
- 25% eggs
- 50% chocolate chips
- 1% baking soda
- 1% salt
- 1% vanilla

Now you can see: more butter relative to flour = flatter, chewier cookies. More flour = cakier, puffier cookies. Adjust by 5% in either direction and observe.

## How to convert any recipe to baker's percentage

Take your recipe in grams. Find the total flour weight. Divide everything else by the flour weight. Multiply by 100.

That's literally all it is.

Your grandma's muffin recipe:
- 250g flour
- 150g sugar
- 120g butter
- 2 eggs (~100g)
- 120ml milk (~120g)
- 8g baking powder
- 3g salt

Flour = 250g = 100%.
- Sugar: 150/250 = 60%
- Butter: 120/250 = 48%
- Eggs: 100/250 = 40%
- Milk: 120/250 = 48%
- Baking powder: 8/250 = 3.2%
- Salt: 3/250 = 1.2%

Now you have the formula. Make it 1.5× by multiplying flour by 1.5, then each percentage. Done.

## The home baker's cheat sheet

You don't need to memorize this. But if you bake bread regularly, knowing three numbers will change everything:

- **2% salt** — almost every bread uses about 2% salt by flour weight. For 500g flour, that's 10g salt. (This is why your bread tastes bland at 1% and inedible at 4%.)
- **1% yeast** — standard for a same-day dough with commercial yeast. For 500g flour: 5g yeast. For overnight cold ferment: drop to 0.3–0.5%.
- **65% hydration** — the middle of the road for all-purpose flour. Dough is manageable, crumb is decent. Start here and adjust up as you get comfortable.

If you remember nothing else: flour = 100%, salt = 2%, yeast = 1%, start at 65% water. That's a basic bread recipe. Everything else is variation.
