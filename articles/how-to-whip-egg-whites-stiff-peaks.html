How to Whip Egg Whites to Stiff Peaks (and Fix Them)

Whipping egg whites is a race between proteins unfolding and proteins tightening up too far. Fat, temperature, and the moment you add sugar decide which one wins — and all three are things you control.

Whipping egg whites looks like the simplest thing in baking: put whites in a bowl, turn on the mixer, wait. Then you get a bowl that has been running for eight minutes and is still a puddle, or one that went from perfect to grainy while you turned around to grab the sugar.

The reason it misbehaves is that egg white foam is not a stable thing you build — it is a balance you hold. Protein strands unfold as you beat them and link up around trapped air bubbles. Beat a little and nothing sets. Beat a lot and the links tighten until they squeeze out their own water. The window where the foam is right is maybe ninety seconds wide, and it moves depending on how cold your eggs are and how much sugar is already in the bowl.

Everything below is about widening that window and knowing exactly where you are inside it.

⚡ Quick answer: how do you whip egg whites to stiff peaks?

Start with a spotlessly clean stainless or glass bowl — a trace of yolk or grease stops the foam dead. Whites at room temperature whip about twice as fast as cold ones. Beat on low until they froth, then on medium-high until they reach soft peaks, then add the sugar one tablespoon at a time and keep beating until the whisk pulled out upside down holds a point that does not move. Add 1/8 teaspoon of cream of tartar per white if you want a wider margin. Stop the moment it looks glossy and stiff — a few seconds past that is grainy, and a minute past that is ruined.

1. What is actually happening in the bowl

An egg white is about 90% water and 10% protein, and those proteins sit in the white coiled up into tight little balls. Whisking does two things at once. The physical force of the wires drags the balls past each other and pulls them open — this is denaturation — and the same motion whips air into the liquid so that trillions of tiny bubbles form. The uncoiled proteins then spread out around those bubbles and link to each other, building a mesh that holds the air in place.

The mesh is what you are actually after. Air alone would escape the moment you stopped; it is the protein network that traps it. This is also why the volume can look so extreme: a properly whipped white ends up six to eight times its original volume, because you have turned a dense liquid into a foam that is mostly air held in a protein skin.

The catch is that the same linking that builds the mesh, if it keeps going, becomes the thing that destroys it. The bonds between proteins get tighter and stronger, and as they tighten they squeeze the water out of the network. That is what "overwhipped" means physically: you did not beat too much air in, you tightened the protein web until it could no longer hold its own water. The foam turns dull, then lumpy, then separates into a curdled mass sitting in a puddle of liquid.

So the goal is not maximum whipping. It is whipping to the point where the network is continuous but the molecules are still relaxed — which is what the whole rest of this article is about finding.

2. Fat is what kills a bowl of egg whites

If your whites will not climb past a thin foam and you have been at it for five minutes, stop and assume fat. Not a cup of fat — a trace. A single speck of yolk that slipped through when you separated, a bowl that went through the dishwasher and still has a faint film, a whisk whose wires have a coat of oil dried onto them.

The reason a speck matters so much is mechanical. Egg white proteins build their network by touching each other and linking. Fat molecules are built to sit between water and air, and they happily coat the protein strands. Once a strand is coated, it cannot link to its neighbours, so the network never becomes continuous and the bubbles have nothing holding them. A little fat does not weaken the foam, it removes whole patches of it.

This is why the standard advice is not "wipe the bowl" but "use a bowl that has never had fat in it in a form you cannot remove." Stainless steel, glass, and copper are all fine after a hot wash. Plastic is not, because plastic is slightly porous and holds a permanent oil film that hand-washing does not clear — this is the single most common reason a home baker has one bowl that always fails.

Separating technique matters as much as the bowl. Cold eggs are easier to separate cleanly because the yolk is firmer and less likely to break. So separate straight from the fridge, letting each white drop into a small cup first and only then tipping it into the main bowl — that way one broken yolk costs you one white rather than the whole batch. Our egg weight guide covers the size differences you will run into, and the egg substitute guide covers what to do if you are avoiding eggs entirely — aquafaba is the only swap that foams, and it needs the same fat-free treatment.

3. Room temperature whites whip about twice as fast

Cold egg whites are more viscous, and viscosity is the enemy of foaming. A cold white takes noticeably longer to break into a froth and needs more total energy to reach the same volume. Bring the whites to room temperature — around 68°F (20°C) — and the whole process speeds up by roughly half.

There is a countervailing point that gets lost in the usual "always use room temperature" advice. Whites at room temperature whip faster and reach a higher volume, but very warm whites — above about 75°F — build a foam that is less stable and more likely to collapse. So the target is genuinely room temperature, not warm. If you have forgotten to take the eggs out, you can float the whole eggs in a bowl of warm tap water for ten minutes, or separate them and set the bowl of whites in warm water for five. Do not microwave them and do not use hot water; anything that starts cooking the proteins will cost you the batch.

The temperature of the bowl matters for the same reason. A stand mixer runs for several minutes, and the whisk dragging through the whites generates real friction heat. Starting with an ice-cold bowl does not help — it just makes the first two minutes slower — but starting with a bowl straight from a hot dishwasher does hurt, because the whites warm past the point where the foam is stable.

4. Add the sugar after soft peaks, one spoonful at a time

Sugar does two opposite things depending on when it arrives, and this is the part most people get wrong. Added early, before the foam has any structure, it dissolves into the liquid, raises the density, and holds the proteins apart — the whites whip up to a fraction of their potential and stay loose. Added after the proteins have begun to build their mesh, it does the opposite: it dissolves into the network, bonds to the proteins, and lends them water, which makes the mesh stronger and more elastic. Glencoe's food science module puts it plainly — sugar dissolved into the developing network increases its strength and elasticity, and more bubbles form as a result.

So the sequence is fixed. Whip the whites alone until they reach soft peaks, then start adding sugar a tablespoon at a time, giving each addition about 15 to 20 seconds to dissolve before the next. If you hear or feel grit when you rub a little of the foam between your fingers, the sugar has not dissolved — keep going on low speed. Undissolved sugar is what makes a meringue weep syrup beads in the oven, because the crystals pull water to themselves and then melt out into droplets on the surface.

Superfine or caster sugar dissolves fastest and gives the smoothest foam. Granulated works, but pulse it in a food processor for 20 seconds first if you want the same result. The usual ratio is about 2 tablespoons of sugar per egg white, so three whites take roughly 6 tablespoons. If you are unsure how much volume your whites are, weigh them — an average large white is 30 grams, so three whites is about 90 grams — and our egg weight converter will do that arithmetic for you. Weighing also matters because whites vary; counting three eggs can put anywhere from 80 to 105 grams in the bowl.

5. The three peak stages, and how to read them

Recipes ask for soft peaks or stiff peaks without ever showing you the difference, so here is the test that works every time: stop the mixer, lift the whisk straight up and out, and turn it upside down.

Soft peak. The foam clings to the whisk but the tip flops over, curling back on itself like a small wave. If you tilt the bowl, the mass slides. This is where you stop for a soufflé base or a mousse that needs to be folded with something heavy, and it is the point at which you start adding sugar.

Medium peak. The peak stands up but the very tip bends over, the way a soft-serve cone leans. Useful if a recipe says "medium peaks," which is common for lighter cake batters.

Stiff peak. The peak holds a sharp upright point and does not move at all when you invert the whisk. The surface looks glossy, and when you drag the whisk through the bowl it leaves a distinct ridged trail that holds its shape. Tilt the bowl and nothing slides. This is what macaron shells, angel food cake, and meringue cookies need.

The distinction that matters more than any of these labels is gloss. A foam that is ready is always shiny. The moment it turns matte and looks slightly dry, you are on the far side of ready, and every further second is making it worse. For a foam that has to hold its structure through a bake, the surface quality is a better signal than the peak itself.

6. What to do when you have overwhipped them

Overwhipped whites begin to look dry and then curdled, with visible lumps and a dull surface, and eventually they separate into a sponge-like mass sitting in a puddle of their own liquid. What happened is the protein network tightened past the point where it could hold water.

If you catch it at the first sign — dull rather than glossy, but still smooth — you can bring it back. Add one more fresh egg white and beat it in on low speed. The liquid from the new white loosens the tightened network, and the whole thing usually returns to a smooth, glossy, properly stiff foam. It takes about thirty seconds and it works more often than people expect.

If it has already gone lumpy and is leaking water, it is past saving. Some sources suggest a small amount of sugar or a splash of water, and neither reliably rescues a fully broken foam — the water you add leaks straight back out. A broken foam will deflate any batter you fold it into, so starting over is faster than baking something you already know will not rise.

The one place a fully broken white is still useful is anywhere the foam does not need to hold structure, like folding into a dense brownie batter or an omelette. If you cannot bear to throw it out, that is where it goes. This is a good reason to own a second bowl: the difference between a save and a start-over is whether you noticed ten seconds ago.

7. Cream of tartar, copper bowls, and what actually stabilizes foam

Two things push the odds in your favour, and they work in different ways.

Cream of tartar is the easy one. It is the acid salt of tartaric acid, and adding it lowers the pH of the egg whites. That acidification slows the formation of the strongest bonds between proteins — the disulfide bridges — which is exactly the process that turns a good foam gritty and dry when you push it too far. The practical effect is a wider usable window: you have more seconds between perfectly stiff and overwhipped. The standard dose is 1/8 teaspoon per egg white, whisked in at the very start with the raw whites. Lemon juice or a little vinegar will do the same job in a pinch, at about 1/2 teaspoon of lemon juice per white.

A copper bowl does the same thing from a different direction. As the whisk scrapes the copper, trace amounts of copper ions come off into the whites, and those ions bind to a sulfur-containing protein called conalbumin. Because the copper is occupying the sulfur groups, those groups cannot form the strong disulfide bridges that lead to overwhipped, grainy foam. A copper bowl does not make a better foam on its own — it makes the foam much more forgiving of an extra ten seconds of beating.

The important thing is that these two approaches overlap, so pick one rather than both. If you use a copper bowl, skip the cream of tartar; if you use a stainless bowl, add the acid. Doing both is wasteful and can actually slow coagulation more than you want. And if you have neither, the fat-free bowl plus correct timing gets you most of the way there — the stabilizers are insurance, not the foundation.

8. How whipped whites fit the rest of the bake

A perfect bowl of stiff peaks is only half the job, because the foam has to survive being folded into a batter and then survive the oven. Two things decide whether that happens.

Fold, do not stir. Folding means cutting down through the middle of the mixture with a spatula, sweeping along the bottom, and lifting up and over — a deliberate, slow motion that brings the batter up around the foam instead of dragging the foam through it. Add the whites in three or four additions rather than all at once, and fold each one in only until you no longer see white streaks. Every additional stroke past that point is air leaving the batter. The foam is delicate precisely because it is mostly air, and the same physics that let you beat air into it lets you beat it back out.

The bake is what sets it. In the oven, heat does three things in sequence: the trapped air bubbles expand, the water evaporates, and the proteins finally coagulate into a permanent structure. This is why a meringue or a sponge bakes low and slow — a moderate temperature lets the network set evenly all the way through. Bake it too hot and the outside sets and browns before the middle has finished, and the water still inside migrates out as syrup beads on the surface. Bake it too long and the same thing happens from the other direction, as you drive off more water than the structure needs. Glencoe's module describes both failure modes and notes that a meringue pie topping is best baked at around 325°F (163°C) — low enough that the proteins set at an even rate.

That interplay also explains why underbaked meringue on a cool pie filling collapses and weeps: the bottom of the foam never reaches setting temperature, so the weakest part of the structure is the part holding the most weight. Spreading the meringue all the way to the crust so it seals the filling, and putting it onto a filling that is still hot, are both ways to get that bottom layer cooking immediately. If you are working at altitude, the lower air pressure means water boils and evaporates sooner, so the same meringue dries faster and needs a shorter, gentler bake — our high-altitude guide covers the adjustments, and the high-altitude calculator does the numbers.

Once the foam is baked, storage is a humidity problem rather than a heat problem. Both egg whites and sugar are hygroscopic — they attract water — so a finished meringue left in a humid kitchen or a fridge will pull moisture from the air and go soft and sticky. It is the same thing as the weeping you sometimes see on a lemon meringue pie, and it is why meringue cookies are stored in an airtight container with the air squeezed out rather than left on a plate. If your pie has other problems, the broken buttercream guide covers the cream side of the same story, and why cakes sink covers what happens when a structure fails in the middle rather than at the surface.

9. By hand or by machine, and the speed you should actually use

A stand mixer is not automatically better than a whisk. It is faster and it frees your hands, but it is also the reason most people over whip, because once the machine is running it is easy to walk away for thirty seconds and come back to a bowl that has gone past stiff. The machine has no feel for the window. Your eyes do.

If you are using a stand mixer, the correct sequence is low speed first and then medium-high, not top speed from the start. Beating whites on high from the moment they are raw whips large, uneven bubbles into the foam, and those big bubbles make a coarse, unstable foam that collapses in the oven — the crumb comes out with tunnels and a coarse open texture rather than a fine even sponge. Starting on low and letting the foam build through the whole range produces a much finer bubble structure, and it is only after the whites have gone opaque and foamy that you move up to medium-high. That is also the stage where you add the cream of tartar if you are using it, since it needs to be in the raw whites to do its work.

By hand, a balloon whisk with plenty of wire loops works best because it pulls in air across a large surface. It takes roughly ten to twelve minutes of steady, vigorous whisking by hand to reach stiff peaks with three whites, and the motion is a combination of circular sweeps and side-to-side flicks of the wrist to break up the foam and re-aerate it. Hand-whisking has one real advantage: you feel the resistance change under your arm, so you slow down naturally as the foam stiffens, which is exactly when you should.

Either way, the practical rule is the same. The last thirty seconds are the ones that matter, so drop to a lower speed, or stop and check with a lifted whisk, more often the closer you get. Checking costs three seconds. Overwhipping costs a whole batch of whites.

10. Which type of meringue the recipe is actually asking for

Once the peaks are right, the next question is what you are making, because the three classic meringues are built from the same two ingredients in a different order, and the order is what decides how stable the finished foam is. A recipe that says "meringue" without specifying is usually asking for the first one, but not always, and choosing wrong is the difference between a topping that holds its swirl for a day and one that slides off the pie.

French meringue is the plain version: raw whites whipped to soft peaks, then sugar beaten in a spoonful at a time until stiff. It is the easiest and the least stable, and it is the one to use when the meringue is going to be baked anyway — piped meringue cookies, meringue nests, and the lightening of a batter before it goes in the oven. Because the whites are never cooked, it is also the type you should not serve raw to anyone in a high-risk group.

Swiss meringue starts by heating the whites and sugar together over a water bath to about 140°F (60°C), whisking the whole time, and only then whipping the warm mixture to volume off the heat. That initial heating dissolves every grain of sugar and pasteurises the whites, so the finished meringue is both smoother and safe to eat uncooked. It is more stable than French and is the usual choice for meringue buttercream and for torched toppings on tarts.

Italian meringue reverses the order again. The whites are whipped on their own while a sugar syrup cooks to about 240°F (116°C), and the hot syrup is then poured into the running mixer in a thin stream, cooking the whites as it goes. It is the most stable of the three by a wide margin and the only one that reliably holds a piped shape for hours at room temperature, which is why it is the standard for macaron shells and for anything that has to sit on a buffet.

The trade is always the same: more heat early means more stability later, and more steps along the way. If a recipe specifies a type, the type is not decoration — it is telling you how long the finished thing is expected to last.

11. The short version

Every failure in this article comes back to three decisions you make in the first thirty seconds: is the bowl clean, are the whites at room temperature, and are you holding the sugar until soft peaks. Get those right and the peak stages take care of themselves, because you will be able to see exactly where you are and stop there. Get them wrong and no amount of technique later in the process will rescue the bowl.

The one piece of equipment worth buying is a probe thermometer for the bake — the same one that tells you when a cake is done will tell you when your meringue has set. Frequently Asked Questions

How do you tell stiff peaks from soft peaks?

Lift the whisk straight out of the bowl and turn it upside down. A soft peak flops over at the tip like a wave — the peak curls back on itself and will not hold a point. A medium peak stands up but the very tip bends over slightly, like a soft-serve swirl. A stiff peak stands perfectly upright and does not move when you turn the whisk over; the foam also looks glossy and holds a clear ridge where the whisk dragged through it. The other test that never lies: tilt the bowl. Soft peaks slide; stiff peaks hold their shape and stay put in the bowl.

Why won't my egg whites whip?

Almost always fat, and almost always a trace amount you cannot see. A single speck of yolk, a bowl that still has a film of dish soap or butter, or a whisk with dried grease on the wires will stop the proteins from forming their network — fat molecules coat the protein strands and hold them apart. Other causes, in order of how often they turn out to be the culprit: egg whites that were frozen and thawed badly, a plastic bowl (plastic holds a permanent oil film that washing does not remove), humid air, and very old eggs, which are thinner and whip faster but hold less structure. If a bowl has been sitting at soft foam for several minutes and will not climb, throw it out and start fresh in a clean glass or stainless bowl. No amount of extra beating fixes a contaminated bowl.

When should I add the sugar to egg whites?

After the whites have reached soft peaks, and then a spoonful at a time. If you dump all the sugar in at the start, it dissolves and weighs down the foam before the proteins have built any structure, and the whites whip up to a fraction of their volume. The usual instruction is one tablespoon at a time, waiting about 15 to 20 seconds between additions so each one dissolves. The rule of thumb is roughly 2 tablespoons of sugar per egg white, so a 3-white meringue takes about 6 tablespoons. Caster sugar or superfine sugar dissolves faster than granulated and is less likely to leave the foam grainy; if you only have granulated, pulse it in a food processor for 20 seconds first.

Can you fix overwhipped egg whites?

Sometimes, and only if you catch it early. The first sign of overwhipping is that the foam turns from glossy to dull and starts to look dry or curdled, with visible lumps. At that point, add one more fresh egg white and whip it in on low speed — the liquid from the new white loosens the whole mass and it usually comes back to a smooth, glossy stiff peak. If the foam has already separated into a curdled, water-leaking mess, it is past saving and will not fold into a batter without deflating it. Left in that state it will not hold air through baking either, so it is better to start over than to bake it and hope.

Does a copper bowl actually make a difference?

Yes, and there is a real chemical reason. As the whisk scrapes the copper, trace amounts of copper ions come off the bowl and bind to a sulfur-containing egg white protein called conalbumin. That binding blocks the proteins from forming the very strong disulfide bonds that make overwhipped whites gritty and dry, so a copper bowl gives you a wider margin before you ruin the batch. Cream of tartar does a similar job from the other direction: it lowers the pH of the whites, which also slows those disulfide bonds. You do not need both — the standard advice is to pick one. A copper bowl is the nicer solution, but a clean stainless bowl plus 1/8 teaspoon of cream of tartar per egg white gets you nearly all the way there for a fraction of the cost.

📋 The Stiff Peak Checklist

  • Use stainless, glass, or copper — never plastic, which holds a permanent oil film
  • Wipe the bowl and whisk, then check for any trace of yolk
  • Separate cold eggs one at a time into a small cup before adding to the bowl
  • Let the whites come to room temperature, about 68°F (20°C), but not warmer
  • Beat on low until frothy, then medium-high — start slow or you trap big weak bubbles
  • Stop and check at soft peaks: tip flops over, foam still slides when you tilt the bowl
  • Add sugar a tablespoon at a time only after soft peaks, 15–20 seconds apart
  • Use about 2 tablespoons of sugar per white; superfine dissolves fastest
  • Add 1/8 teaspoon of cream of tartar per white if you are not using a copper bowl
  • Stop at stiff peaks, and stop while the surface is still glossy — matte means too far
  • Overwhipped but still smooth? Add one fresh white and beat on low to bring it back
  • Fold in three or four additions, only until the streaks disappear
  • Bake low and slow so the proteins set evenly — a meringue topping at about 325°F
  • Store finished meringue airtight; sugar and egg white both pull moisture from the air

Whipping egg whites is one of the few baking skills where the failure is fully visible in the bowl, before anything goes in the oven. That makes it unusually learnable — you get the feedback immediately, and you get it cheaply. Watch the gloss, hold the sugar, keep the bowl clean, and the peak stages stop being something you guess at.
