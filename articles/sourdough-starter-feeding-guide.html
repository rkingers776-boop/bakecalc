Sourdough Starter Feeding Guide — Daily Schedule, Ratios & How to Fix 7 Common Problems

Feeding ratios decoded (1:1:1 vs 1:5:5 — when to use which). Day-by-day guide for new starters. Seven starter problems with the exact fix for each. Plus a simple ratio calculator that takes 5 seconds.

My first sourdough starter died on day 6. The second one — I named him Bubbles, which was optimistic — made it to day 11 before growing a fuzzy gray patch on the surface. The third one survived but produced bread that tasted like a saltine cracker that had given up on life. No rise, no tang, no open crumb. Just a dense, sour-smelling hockey puck that I sliced and ate anyway because I'd spent three weeks getting to that point.

Turns out I was doing basically everything wrong. Wrong ratio. Wrong flour. Wrong schedule. I was treating the starter like a houseplant — water it once a day and hope for the best — when what I actually had was a living culture of yeast and bacteria that needed specific conditions to thrive. Once I figured that out, everything changed.

This guide covers what I learned across three dead starters and one very alive one that's been going strong for two years now. No theory for the sake of theory. Just the stuff that matters: ratios, schedules, and what to do when things go sideways.

What a Sourdough Starter Actually Is (60-Second Version)

Take flour. Add water. Wait.

Wild yeast living on the flour grains wakes up and starts eating the starch. Bacteria join the party — specifically lactobacillus, the same family that makes yogurt and kimchi sour. The yeast produces carbon dioxide (bubbles, rise). The bacteria produce lactic and acetic acid (tang, flavor, longer shelf life). Together they form a stable ecosystem that, with regular feeding, can live for decades. The oldest known continuously-maintained starter in the US is from 1847 — same culture, fed every day for over 175 years.

That's it. You're not "making" anything. You're creating the right conditions for something that already exists on the flour to multiply. Your job is just to feed it on time and not kill it with chlorine, extreme temperatures, or neglect. If you can keep a goldfish alive, you can keep a starter alive. It's actually easier — a starter doesn't need a filter change.

Feeding Ratios: The One Thing That Changed Everything

Every starter feeding follows the same structure: starter : flour : water. A ratio tells you how much of each, by weight.

When bakers write "1:1:1," they mean equal weights of all three. Example: 50g starter + 50g flour + 50g water. When they write "1:5:5," they mean one part starter, five parts flour, five parts water. Example: 20g starter + 100g flour + 100g water.

The ratio is the biggest lever you have. It controls how fast your starter peaks, what it tastes like, and how often you need to feed it. Most starter problems come down to using the wrong ratio for your situation.

The ratio that works for someone in Phoenix in August (85°F kitchen, 1:5:5 to slow things down) is wrong for someone in Chicago in January (65°F kitchen, 1:1:1 to keep things moving). Your room temperature is part of the equation. If you want the same peak time in a colder kitchen, use a lower ratio. In a warmer kitchen, use a higher one. The starter doesn't care about the clock — it cares about how much food is available relative to how fast the yeast can eat it.

The Ratio Rule Most People Miss

Flour and water are always equal weights. Always. Whether it's 1:2:2 or 1:10:10, the flour and water numbers match. This keeps the hydration at 100% — which is what nearly all home sourdough recipes assume. When you see "100% hydration starter," that's all it means: equal flour and water by weight. If you use volume measurements (1/2 cup flour + 1/4 cup water), your hydration is all over the place because a cup of flour weighs anywhere from 120g to 160g depending on how you scoop it. Weigh it instead — 1 cup of flour is 120-125g for AP, and that consistency matters a lot for a starter.

Starting from Scratch: Day-by-Day for the First 14 Days

If you've never made a starter before, the first two weeks follow a predictable pattern. Days 1-2: nothing happens. Days 3-4: a burst of activity (false hope — this is bacteria, not yeast). Days 5-7: everything stops and it looks dead. Days 8-14: the yeast colony finally stabilizes and real activity begins.

This timeline catches most people off guard. They see the day-3 bubble burst, think they've succeeded, bake with it on day 5, and produce a dense brick that didn't rise. Then they throw it out and start over. Don't do that. The "dead" phase on days 5-7 is actually when the pH is dropping and the yeast is slowly winning the turf war against the wrong bacteria. It's supposed to look dead. Keep feeding.

Day What Happens What You Do Signs It's Working
Day 1 Mix 50g whole wheat or rye flour + 50g water. Stir vigorously. Cover loosely. Let it sit 24 hours at room temp. No feeding yet. Nothing visible. Maybe a few tiny bubbles by hour 20.
Day 2 Still quiet. Maybe a slight sour smell starting. Discard half (about 50g). Add 50g flour + 50g water. Stir. Cover. A thin layer of bubbles on top is encouraging. No bubbles is also fine.
Day 3-4 ⚠ The false peak. Lots of bubbles, doubled in size, smells funky. This is leuconostoc bacteria — not the yeast you want. It'll die off as the pH drops. Discard half. Feed 1:1:1. Do NOT bake with it — it won't leaven bread yet. Rapid rise, lots of bubbles, smell like gym socks or parmesan. This is normal and temporary.
Day 5-7 The quiet phase. Activity drops sharply. Maybe no bubbles at all. The pH is dropping, bad bacteria are dying, yeast is slowly establishing. Keep feeding 1:1:1 every 24 hours. Switch to AP flour if you started with whole wheat — or keep using whole wheat for faster colonization. Stirring reveals small bubbles trapped in the paste. Faint sour smell but not unpleasant.
Day 8-10 Activity returns — for real this time. The yeast colony is establishing. Consistent bubbles, rise becoming reliable. Feed 1:1:1 every 12-24 hours. Watch for consistent doubling within 6-8 hours of feeding. Starter doubles in volume reliably. Smells tangy and pleasant — like yogurt or sourdough bread.
Day 11-14 Starter stabilizes. Consistent rise and fall cycle. Pleasant sour smell. If it's still sluggish, switch to a 1:2:2 ratio and do a few more days. Continue 1:1:1 or 1:2:2 feedings. By day 14, test it: does it double within 4-6 hours? Float test pass? Smell good? If yes, it's ready to bake. Doubles in 4-6 hours at 72-75°F. Bubbles throughout — not just on top. Smells like a sourdough bakery.

Whole wheat or rye flour will get your starter established about 3-5 days faster than all-purpose alone — they carry more wild yeast on the bran. Once established, you can switch to whatever flour you keep on hand. For flour weight conversions while scaling, use the flour converter.

Daily Maintenance: The 5-Minute Routine

Once your starter is established, the daily routine takes about as long as brewing a pour-over coffee:

  1. Weigh your jar (with the starter in it). Write the empty jar weight on the bottom with a Sharpie so you don't have to do this every time. I use a 32oz wide-mouth Mason jar — weighs 428g empty. Knowing the jar weight lets you calculate starter weight without transferring to a separate bowl.
  2. Discard most of it. Pour out everything except about 25-50g of starter clinging to the sides. Don't overthink the exact amount — anywhere in the ballpark is fine. (Save the discard in a separate container in the fridge for pancakes, crackers, or costed recipe development.)
  3. Add flour and water at your chosen ratio. For daily maintenance at room temp, I use 1:2:2 (25g starter retained + 50g flour + 50g water = 125g total). For a 24-hour schedule, use 1:3:3 or 1:5:5. Weigh everything. Volume measurements are too inconsistent for starter maintenance — 50g of flour scooped from the bag could be anywhere from 40g to 65g.
  4. Stir until no dry flour remains. Scrape the sides. You want a thick, uniform paste with the consistency of pancake batter. If it's too thick to stir easily, add a few grams of water — different flours absorb water differently, and a starter that's too stiff ferments unevenly.
  5. Mark the level. Put a rubber band or a piece of tape at the starter's current level on the outside of the jar. In 4-8 hours, you want to see it has at least doubled above that line.
  6. Cover loosely. The jar lid should sit on top without being screwed down — the starter produces CO₂ and needs to vent. A screw band with a coffee filter held in place works well. Or screw the lid on and then unscrew it a quarter turn. Do not seal it tight unless you want to clean exploded starter off your ceiling.

7 Starter Problems and Exactly How to Fix Them

1. Dark Liquid on Top (Hooch)

What it looks like: a layer of clear, gray, or dark liquid sitting on top of the starter. Smells sharply of alcohol or vinegar.

What it means: the starter has run out of food and the yeast is producing alcohol as a starvation byproduct. This is the equivalent of your starter holding up an empty bowl and staring at you.

The fix: you can pour off the hooch or stir it back in — it's not harmful, just sour. Stirring it in makes the starter more acidic (good if you like tangy bread). Then feed at a higher ratio — if you were doing 1:1:1, move to 1:3:3. If hooch appears within 12 hours of feeding, the room is too warm; increase the ratio or find a cooler spot.

2. Starter Won't Rise at All

What to check, in order:

Temperature first. A starter at 65°F rises at roughly half the speed of one at 75°F. Below 60°F, it barely moves. The sweet spot is 75-80°F. Put it in the oven with just the light on (door closed), on top of the fridge, or in a cooler with a mug of hot water next to it. Don't put it directly on a heating pad — the bottom gets too hot and kills the yeast before the top even warms up.

Water second. If your tap water smells like a swimming pool, the chlorine is suppressing yeast activity. Use filtered water, bottled spring water, or fill a pitcher and let it sit on the counter overnight — chlorine dissipates in about 12 hours. Chloramine (used by some municipal systems) doesn't dissipate; you need a filter that removes it, or use bottled water.

Flour third. Bleached all-purpose flour has fewer microorganisms than whole grain flour. If your starter has been sluggish for more than a week, do 2-3 feedings with 50% whole wheat or rye flour. The bran carries extra wild yeast and minerals that boost activity. Once it's active again, switch back to your regular flour gradually over a few feedings.

Ratio fourth. If you're feeding at 1:5:5 and nothing happens, your starter may not be strong enough for that ratio yet. Drop to 1:1:1 for a few days to rebuild the colony density, then gradually increase to 1:2:2, then 1:3:3, and so on. A starter that can't double in 8 hours at 1:1:1 isn't ready for a higher ratio.

3. Mold — Fuzzy, Colored Patches

Don't try to save it. If you see fuzzy growth — pink, orange, green, gray, or black — the starter is contaminated. Mold sends threads (hyphae) deep into the culture that you can't see. Scooping off the top doesn't remove them. Throw it out, wash the jar with hot soapy water and then with boiling water, and start over.

Mold happens when the starter is too weak to acidify fast enough to kill intruders — leaving it unfed for too long, or storing it un-fed in a warm place. A healthy, regularly-fed starter is acidic enough (pH 3.5-4.0) to resist mold on its own. If you've had mold twice, the issue is probably your jar — switch to a clean jar at every feeding for a while, and make sure your storage spot isn't near a fruit bowl (fruit flies carry acetobacter, and the fruit itself releases mold spores).

4. Smells Like Nail Polish Remover (Acetone)

That's ethyl acetate. It means the starter is over-fermented — it's been sitting too long since the last feeding, and the acid-producing bacteria have dominated. The starter isn't dead; the balance is just off.

The fix: take 10g of starter (seriously, just 10g). Feed it 1:10:10 — so 10g starter + 100g flour + 100g water. The extreme dilution resets the pH balance and gives the yeast a massive food surplus. Do this twice, 12 hours apart. The acetone smell should be gone by the second feeding. If it's still there after three 1:10:10 feedings, the starter may have permanently shifted to a bacterial-dominant culture — start fresh. But I've only seen that happen once, with a starter that had been neglected in the back of a warm fridge for three weeks.

5. Too Sour — Bread Tastes Harsh or Vinegary

Some tang is the point. But if your bread makes people wince, the starter's acid load is too high — usually from infrequent feeding or high fermentation temperatures.

The fix: feed more frequently (every 12 hours instead of 24) or increase the feeding ratio (1:3:3 or 1:5:5). More food means less time in the acid-building stage. Also check your room temperature: above 80°F, the bacteria outpace the yeast and produce more acetic acid. Moving the starter to a spot that's 72-75°F makes a noticeable difference in flavor balance. For a full reset: 1:10:10 feeding, 3 times, 12 hours apart. The starter should go from sharp-vinegar to mild-yogurt in about 36 hours.

6. Starter Is Runny or Separates Immediately After Mixing

A starter that looks like thin pancake batter instead of thick paste has too much water, or the gluten structure has been completely digested by enzymes. This happens when the starter over-ferments: protease enzymes break down the gluten proteins until there's nothing left to hold water. The flour solids settle to the bottom and a watery layer rises to the top within an hour of mixing.

The fix: feed more frequently so it never reaches the "completely digested" stage. If it's currently runny, do a stiff feeding: reduce the water slightly (e.g., 50g flour + 40g water instead of 50g + 50g) for one or two feedings. The stiffer environment favors yeast over bacteria and helps rebuild the gluten structure. Then go back to 100% hydration once it thickens up.

7. Starter Doubles but Bread Still Doesn't Rise

The starter passes the float test. It doubles reliably. But the loaf comes out dense and flat anyway. This one drove me crazy for weeks.

The issue is usually timing, not strength. A starter that doubles in 4 hours at 1:1:1 is strong — but if you use it 6 hours after feeding when it's already collapsing, you're adding exhausted yeast to your dough. The yeast has eaten all the food and gone dormant. Use the starter at its peak — domed top, just before it starts to recede. If your schedule means you can't catch the peak, feed at a higher ratio (1:3:3 or 1:5:5) the night before so it peaks when you're ready to mix dough in the morning.

Other possibilities: your dough is under-proofed (the bulk fermentation needs more time — sourdough is much slower than commercial yeast), your shaping knocked all the gas out, or your oven wasn't hot enough. Storing in the Fridge — and Waking It Back Up

Feeding twice a day forever is not sustainable for most people. Once your starter is mature (consistent doubling, at least 3 weeks old), the fridge is your friend.

Putting it to sleep: Feed the starter at 1:2:2 or 1:3:3. Let it sit at room temperature for 1-2 hours — just enough for fermentation to start (you'll see a few bubbles). Then close the lid (loosely — remember the venting thing) and put it in the fridge. The cold slows everything down by about 10×. A starter that peaks in 5 hours at room temp takes roughly 50 hours in the fridge.

Weekly maintenance: pull it out once a week. Discard most of it, feed at room temperature, let it peak, and put it back. Skip a week? It'll be fine — a mature starter can go 2-3 weeks refrigerated without feeding. The hooch layer will be thicker than usual, and it'll need 2-3 revival feedings before it's strong enough to bake, but it won't die.

Waking it up to bake: plan on 2-3 days. Take it out of the fridge on Wednesday to bake on Saturday. Feed it right away. Feed again when it peaks. By the third feeding (Friday night or Saturday morning), it should be doubling consistently within 4-6 hours and smell pleasantly tangy. That's your signal it's ready. Use it at peak for the best oven spring.

If you've neglected it for a month: don't panic. Pour off the hooch (there will be a lot). Scrape away any dried crust on the sides of the jar. Take 25g from the center — that's where the surviving yeast is most concentrated. Feed it 1:2:2. It might take 4-5 feedings over 2-3 days to bounce back. Be patient. The yeast colony shrinks during long fridge storage but almost never dies completely. I've revived a starter that sat untouched in the back of the fridge for 5 weeks. Took three days and five feedings, but it came back.

Make a Backup — It Takes 2 Minutes

Spread a thin layer of your fed, active starter onto a piece of parchment paper. Let it dry completely at room temperature — 24 to 48 hours, depending on humidity. It'll crack into flakes. Crumble those flakes into a zip-top bag or a small jar, label it, and stash it in the pantry. Dried starter flakes are viable for at least a year at room temperature.

To revive: take 1 tablespoon of dried flakes + 2 tablespoons of water. Let it rehydrate for 30 minutes. Add 2 tablespoons of flour. Wait 12 hours. Feed again at 1:1:1. It'll be back to full strength within 3-4 feedings. This backup has saved me twice — once after a 10-day power outage during a summer storm, and once when I knocked the jar off the counter and shattered it on the tile floor. A backup costs 2 minutes and zero dollars. Make one now, before you need it.

📋 Starter Success Checklist

Every starter problem I've ever had traces back to one of these. Tape it inside your cabinet door.

  • Weigh everything — a $12 scale eliminates the biggest variable. Flour weight varies by 30% when measured by volume. Flour cups → grams
  • Filtered water — chlorine suppresses yeast. Let tap water sit out overnight, or use filtered. If your city uses chloramine, you need a filter.
  • Right ratio for your schedule — 1:1:1 for twice-a-day feeding, 1:5:5 for once a day. Warmer kitchens need higher ratios.
  • Temperature between 72-80°F — the yeast is happiest here. Above 85°F, bacteria dominate and it gets too sour. Below 65°F, everything crawls.
  • Use at peak — domed top, just starting to level. Not while still rising (too young). Not after it collapses (exhausted).
  • Clean jar every few feedings — crusty buildup on the sides invites mold. Switch to a clean jar at least once a week.
  • Keep a dried backup — spread thin on parchment, dry 24 hours, store in a bag in the pantry. Costs nothing. Saves everything.
  • Whole wheat or rye for reviving — a tablespoon of whole grain flour in the feeding boosts activity noticeably. Keep a small bag of rye in the freezer.

A sourdough starter is the closest thing in baking to a pet. It needs regular food, a comfortable temperature, and someone who pays attention to its signals. The difference is that if you accidentally kill a starter, you can make a new one in two weeks with flour and water. Try that with a goldfish.

The learning curve is real — most people's first few sourdough loaves are disappointing, and that's fine. The starter gets stronger with age. So does the baker. By month three, you'll be reading your starter's rise and smell the way you read a recipe: with the kind of intuition that only comes from doing it repeatedly and messing up occasionally. The work you put in up front pays off in decades of bread, pancakes, pizza dough, and the quiet satisfaction of pulling a perfectly scored boule out of the oven and knowing — really knowing — that you made every part of it from scratch, starting with nothing but flour and water.
