Cake Flour vs All-Purpose vs Bread Flour: Which One Actually Belongs in Your Recipe

I once baked a pound cake with bread flour. It came out of the pan golden and tall and completely convincing — right up until the knife bounced off it. What I had made was not cake. It was sweet bread with ambitions. Same recipe, same oven, same hands. The only thing that changed was the bag of flour.

Flour is the one ingredient most bakers treat as interchangeable, and it is also the one that punishes that assumption hardest. Swap butter for margarine and you get a slightly different cookie. Swap cake flour for bread flour and you get a different category of food. The flour decides how much structure your batter will build, and structure is what separates a crumb that melts from a crumb that bounces.

All of that comes down to one number printed on the side of the bag: protein percentage. It is the most predictive thing you can read on a package, and almost nobody reads it. Here is what it does, what the other differences between flours actually are, and what to do at 9pm when the recipe says cake flour and your pantry says no.

⚡ Quick answer: which flour should I use?

Cake flour (7–10% protein, depending on the product) for tender layer cakes, sponge, chiffon, and angel food. All-purpose (10–12%) for everything that is not fussy — cookies, muffins, quick breads, most cakes, and passable bread. Bread flour (12–14%) for yeast bread, pizza, bagels, and pretzels. Out of cake flour? Whisk ¾ cup + 2 tbsp all-purpose flour (105g) with 2 tbsp cornstarch (14g) and sift twice. Out of bread flour? All-purpose works, but cut the liquid by 15–30g and read the dough earlier.

Protein is the most useful number on the bag

Wheat flour contains two proteins you have probably heard named without ever meeting: glutenin and gliadin. Dry, they do nothing. Add water and start mixing, and they link into gluten — gliadin supplying stretch, glutenin supplying snap-back. The more of those two proteins a flour contains, the more gluten it can build, and the stronger the network that traps the gas your leaveners produce.

Strong network, high rise, chewy bite. Weak network, low rise, tender crumb. That single tradeoff is the whole reason flour comes in more than one bag:

Notice that whole wheat has more protein than bread flour and still makes a dense, squat loaf. Protein is potential, not destiny. Whole wheat also carries bran and germ, and those particles interfere with the way the gluten network forms and take up water on their own schedule, which is why the extra protein does not translate into extra structure. A cup of it weighs about 120 grams — the bran does not make whole wheat heavier, it makes it thirstier. It is a good reminder that the number predicts behavior, it does not guarantee it.

The percentages above are the industry's usual ranges, and the spread is real. King Arthur publishes its own figures — 10% for its cake flour, 11.7% for all-purpose, 12.7% for bread flour, 14.2% for high-gluten — and the company notes plainly that other brands are often more variable, which is why two bags both labeled "all-purpose" can behave like different ingredients. If you bake the same recipes often, buy the same brand and note the number on the bag. It is the cheapest upgrade available to you.

Protein is not the whole story — grinding and bleaching count too

Two flours can share a protein percentage and still handle differently, because protein is only one of the levers.

Grinding fineness. Cake flour is milled to a powder so fine it feels like cornstarch. Finer particles blend into butter and sugar more evenly, aerate more readily when you sift, and — counterintuitively — absorb more liquid than coarser flour of the same weight, because there is more surface area for the water to reach. That is part of why cake batters stay fluid and loose even at very high sugar and fat ratios.

Bleaching. In the US, most cake flour is bleached with chlorine gas, and the bleaching does three separate jobs. It softens the flour proteins, it partially breaks down the starch granules so they take up more water and sugar, and it nudges the flour slightly acidic, which helps the batter set at the right moment. None of that is cosmetic. A cake baked with bleached cake flour and the same cake baked with an unbleached low-protein flour will not be identical, even at the same protein number.

Worth knowing if you bake across borders: the UK and the EU prohibit flour bleaching outright. European cake and sponge recipes are developed around unbleached flour and often lean harder on starch, extra yolks, or mechanical aeration to get the tenderness that a chlorinated flour supplies for free in the US. If you move a recipe between the two, expect to adjust more than the flour brand.

This is also the honest limit of the DIY substitute further down the page. Blending all-purpose flour with cornstarch copies one variable — the protein percentage — and nothing else. It cannot reproduce the mill, because commercial cake flour is ground finer than anything a home kitchen can manage. And it cannot reproduce the chlorine treatment, which is what softens the protein and alters the starch in the first place. So the blend behaves like a lower-protein all-purpose flour: close enough in a butter cake, where fat and sugar do a lot of the work, and short of the real thing in an angel food cake, where the protein level and the starch behavior carry the whole bake with no fat to hide behind.

What actually changes when you swap flours: the water

Here is the adjustment almost every substitution guide skips. How much water a flour can hold depends on its protein and on how much starch was fractured during milling: intact starch granules hold relatively little water, while damaged ones soak up several times their own weight. Higher-protein flours are generally milled harder and carry more of both. So when you change flour, you have quietly changed how thirsty the recipe is, and the liquid that was correct for one bag is now wrong for the other.

Swap bread flour for all-purpose at the same hydration and the dough comes out wetter, softer, and more spread out than the recipe intended, because it is not being absorbed. The dough will feel slack and tacky and it will not hold a shape. The fix is not more flour — that just changes the recipe a second time. The fix is less liquid.

  • Replacing bread flour with all-purpose: cut the liquid by about 15–30g (roughly 5%) for a standard loaf, then judge by feel. All-purpose can also reach its useful level of gluten development sooner than bread flour, so do not blindly carry over the long kneading schedule the recipe was written around. Judge by the dough rather than the clock — a weaker network is easier to over-work into a tight, tough loaf.
  • Replacing all-purpose with bread flour: the dough will be drier and stiffer at first and want a bit more water. Add it a tablespoon at a time. Bread flour also tolerates longer kneading, so converting a recipe to bread flour gives you more room before the dough tightens — but the dough still decides, not the clock.
  • Replacing anything with whole wheat: add 10–15% more liquid and let the dough rest 15 minutes after mixing so the bran has time to hydrate. Even then, do not replace more than about 30–50% of the white flour unless the recipe was written for whole wheat from the start.

The professional rule of thumb — the one bread bakers repeat to each other — is that when you switch flours you adjust hydration by the feel of the dough, not by the number in the recipe. A recipe is a record of what one bag of flour did in one kitchen. Change the bag and you have to read the dough again. If you are not sure what "fully developed" should feel like, the windowpane test turns it into something you can see rather than guess.

Texturally, expect a real difference even when you get the water right. All-purpose generally gives a softer, less lofty loaf than the same recipe made with bread flour, with a tighter crumb and a softer crust, and the gap widens in high-hydration doughs and heavily structured breads, where the gluten network has more work to do. It is not a failure. It is a different loaf.

The two swaps that work, and the ones that will ruin your bake

Turning all-purpose into cake flour

Cornstarch contains no protein at all, so mixing it into all-purpose flour dilutes the protein percentage and interrupts the gluten strands as they try to form. King Arthur's published method is the one to use: whisk together ¾ cup plus 2 tablespoons (105g) all-purpose flour and 2 tablespoons (14g) cornstarch, then sift it at least twice. By volume that reads as "measure 1 cup of all-purpose flour, remove 2 tablespoons, add 2 tablespoons of cornstarch."

Two things decide whether this works. First, the sifting is not optional — cornstarch clumps, and clumps bake into gummy streaks that are worse than just using all-purpose straight. Twice through a fine sieve is the minimum. Second, measure the flour by spooning and leveling, never by scooping from the bag, or you will pack in up to 150g in a single cup and defeat the entire exercise.

Where it holds up: layer cakes, cupcakes, muffins, quick breads. Where it does not: angel food cake, chiffon, and genoise, which depend on a precise protein level and give you no fat to hide behind if you miss. Use real cake flour for those, or accept a denser result. And while you are working by weight, note that this substitute changes your flour weight for the recipe — the blend is lighter per cup than plain all-purpose, which is exactly the point. The flour to grams converter covers all-purpose, bread, and cake flour separately if you want to see the numbers side by side, and 1 cup of cornstarch to grams handles the other half of the blend.

Turning all-purpose into bread flour

Vital wheat gluten is exactly what the name says: the gluten proteins extracted from wheat and dried into a powder. Whisked into all-purpose flour, it raises the effective protein percentage back toward bread flour territory. Start with 1½ teaspoons per cup of all-purpose flour, whisked into the dry flour before any liquid touches it — added to wet dough it clumps into rubbery pockets that never disperse. Treat the spoon as a starting point rather than a formula: the right amount depends on the protein of the flour you began with and how strong you want the finished dough, so adjust from there.

Be aware that this is a home-kitchen trick rather than a standard: published amounts range from 1 to 2 teaspoons per cup depending on the brand of gluten and the target protein, with no single authoritative figure. Start at 1½ teaspoons, and if you want to reason it out, the arithmetic is simple enough. A cup of 11% flour weighs about 120g, so raising it to 13% means adding roughly 2.4g of protein; vital wheat gluten is about 75% protein, so a little over 3g — call it 1½ teaspoons — does the job.

Two caveats. Subtract the gluten you add from the flour, or you have added volume to the recipe. And use the dough the same day where you can: dough built on supplemented all-purpose does not keep as well as dough made with real bread flour.

When a straight 1:1 swap is genuinely fine

You do not always need to do arithmetic. Plenty of recipes have enough fat, sugar, and liquid to soften whatever extra gluten a substitute brings:

  • All-purpose in place of cake flour, no adjustment: snack cakes, brownies, loaf cakes, and muffins. Slightly denser, slightly chewier, absolutely fine.
  • All-purpose in place of bread flour, with less water: dinner rolls, sandwich bread, cinnamon rolls, focaccia. The results are softer and more tender than the bread-flour version, which for these recipes is usually the point.
  • Bread flour in cookies, on purpose: if you want a thick, chewy cookie that does not spread into a puddle, bread flour does that deliberately. Not a substitution, a technique.

When it will genuinely ruin the bake

These are the combinations where the flour is not a detail, it is the recipe:

  • Bread flour in a light cake. This is how you get the pound cake that bounced. A strong gluten network in a high-sugar batter produces something elastic and tough, and the crumb can actually feel rubbery when you press it.
  • Cake flour or pastry flour in bread. Not enough protein to build a network that holds gas through a long ferment. You get a flat, pale loaf with no oven spring and a crumb that falls apart instead of pulling. Bagels, pizza, and ciabatta are the worst candidates — ciabatta in particular depends on a strong, extensible dough and does not forgive a weak flour even with added gluten.
  • Bread flour in pie crust, scones, or biscuits. Everything you want in a pie crust — flakiness, tenderness, the way it shatters — depends on limited gluten. Bread flour makes it tough.
  • Bread flour in a delicate cake recipe "just to add structure." If a cake is collapsing, the answer is technique — folding, oven temperature, not opening the door — not a stronger flour. Read why your cake sinks in the middle instead.
  • Self-rising flour where all-purpose is called for. You are adding leavening and salt the recipe already accounts for. The bake will dome and overshoot.
  • Almond or coconut flour at 1:1 for wheat flour. These are not lower-protein versions of wheat flour, they are structurally different ingredients that absorb far more liquid and contain no gluten at all. That swap changes the recipe, not the flour. If you are baking with them on purpose, the almond flour to grams page has the real densities.

00 flour is not cake flour, and it is not bread flour either

The "00" on an Italian flour bag describes how finely the flour was sifted, not how much protein it contains. The Italian system grades flour 00, 0, 1, and 2, from finest to coarsest, and that is the entire meaning of the number. It says nothing about strength.

In practice, Italian 00 flours run from about 9% protein to 13% depending on the wheat and the brand — a range that overlaps all-purpose and bread flour completely. So you cannot substitute "00 flour" for cake flour by default, and you cannot assume it is a bread flour either. Look at the protein percentage on the bag, not the zeros.

The American "00 pizza flour" you find in supermarkets is a different animal again: often around 11–12% protein, though the range varies considerably by brand and by the job the flour was blended for, milled fine, and formulated for home ovens that top out well below the 900°F of a wood-fired pizza oven. It is a good product, just not a category you can reason about from the label alone.

How to decide in ten seconds

Work down this list and stop at the first answer that applies:

  1. Does the recipe name a flour? Use it. Recipe developers test with a specific bag, and that is the variable they built the liquid around.
  2. Is it a yeast bread, pizza, or bagel? Bread flour. If it is a soft enriched bread — rolls, brioche, sandwich loaves — all-purpose is a legitimate choice and gives you a more tender crumb.
  3. Is it a light cake, sponge, or chiffon? Cake flour. Layer cakes tolerate all-purpose; foam cakes do not.
  4. Is it pie crust, scones, or biscuits? Pastry flour if you have it, all-purpose if you do not, and keep your hands off it while mixing.
  5. Everything else — cookies, muffins, brownies, pancakes, quick breads — all-purpose. That is what it is for.

Then the last question, and the one that saves the most weekends: can you actually buy the bag the recipe wants? A dedicated bag of cake flour costs a few dollars and lasts months in the freezer. If you bake cakes more than occasionally, that bag is a better investment than any pan or gadget, because it removes the one variable you cannot taste-test your way around. And if you are scaling a recipe up or down at the same time, run the quantities through the recipe scaler first so you are not fixing two problems at once — and price the batch with the cost calculator if it is a sale batch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use all-purpose flour instead of cake flour?

Yes, and there are two ways. The reliable way is to blend your own: 3/4 cup plus 2 tablespoons (105g) all-purpose flour whisked with 2 tablespoons (14g) cornstarch, sifted twice — that lowers the effective protein into cake-flour territory. The lazy way is to use all-purpose straight, knowing the cake will be slightly denser and chewier. The lazy way is fine for snack cakes and brownies. It is not fine for angel food cake, chiffon, or genoise, where the batter has almost no fat to hide behind and the protein level decides whether the cake rises or collapses.

Can I use all-purpose flour instead of bread flour?

Yes, with two adjustments. All-purpose absorbs less water than bread flour, so cut the liquid by roughly 15–30g (about 5%) or the dough will be slack and sticky. And do not assume the same kneading schedule — all-purpose can reach its useful level of gluten development sooner, so judge by the dough rather than the clock, because the weaker network is easier to over-work into a tight, tough loaf. Expect a softer, less lofty loaf with a tighter crumb; the difference is modest in a sandwich loaf and more noticeable in a high-hydration or heavily structured bread. For dinner rolls and sandwich bread that is often an upgrade. For bagels, ciabatta, and artisan boules it is a downgrade you will notice.

Is cake flour the same as pastry flour?

No, though they are close cousins. Cake flour usually runs about 7–10% protein depending on the product, and it is milled extremely fine, and in the US it is usually bleached — the bleaching is not cosmetic, it softens the protein and helps the flour absorb more sugar and liquid. Pastry flour sits around 8% protein, is milled less aggressively, and is normally unbleached. Cake flour goes in cakes and sponges. Pastry flour goes in pie crust, scones, biscuits, and tarts, where you want tenderness with a little more structure to hold a flaky shape.

Does bread flour make cookies tough?

It makes them chewier and less spread, which is a deliberate choice in some recipes and a mistake in others. The extra protein builds a stronger gluten network that resists spreading in the oven, so cookies baked with bread flour come out thicker and with more bite. If you want soft, thin, spread-out cookies, use all-purpose or cake flour. If you want a chewy chocolate chip cookie with a thick center — the kind that stays soft for two days — bread flour is the right bag, sometimes blended half-and-half with all-purpose.

What is 00 flour?

The 00 is an Italian milling classification, not a protein rating. Italian flour is graded by how finely it is sifted — 00 is the finest, followed by 0, 1, and 2 — and it says nothing at all about protein content. Italian 00 flours range from about 9% protein up to 13% depending on the brand and the wheat. So 00 flour is not cake flour and it is not bread flour; it is a very finely milled flour that happens to be excellent for pasta and high-heat pizza. Check the protein number on the bag before you assume what it will do.

Can I substitute self-rising flour for all-purpose flour?

Not one-for-one. Self-rising flour is all-purpose flour with baking powder and salt already mixed in — typically about 1 1/2 teaspoons of baking powder and 1/4 teaspoon of salt per cup. If you swap it into a recipe that also lists its own leavening and salt, you double both and the bake will overshoot, dome, or taste salty. You can move in the other direction safely: to make your own self-rising flour, whisk 1 cup of all-purpose flour with 1 1/2 teaspoons baking powder and 1/4 teaspoon salt.

📋 The Flour Decision Checklist

  • Read the protein percentage on the bag — it predicts behavior better than the label name does
  • Cake 7–10% · Pastry ~8% · All-purpose 10–12% · Bread 12–14% · High-gluten 14%+
  • Recipe names a flour? Use that flour. The liquid was built around it
  • Switching to a lower-protein flour? Cut the liquid by 15–30g and read the dough earlier — do not carry over a long kneading schedule
  • Switching to a higher-protein flour? Add water a tablespoon at a time and read the dough, not the recipe
  • No cake flour: 105g all-purpose + 14g cornstarch, sifted twice — for layer cakes and cupcakes, not angel food
  • No bread flour: start with 1½ tsp vital wheat gluten per cup all-purpose, whisked in dry, used the same day
  • Never 1:1 almond or coconut flour for wheat flour — different ingredient, different liquid rules
  • Bake cakes often? Keep a bag of cake flour in the freezer. It is cheaper than a failed bake
  • Store flour airtight, cool, and dry — whole wheat and nut flours go rancid fastest

Flour is not a commodity, it is a specification. Two bags on the same shelf can differ by four percentage points of protein, and that gap is the difference between a crumb that melts and one that fights back. Read the number, buy the bag that matches the job, and when you have to improvise, change the water along with the flour — because the flour you swapped did not just change the structure of the bake, it changed how much liquid the recipe actually needs.
