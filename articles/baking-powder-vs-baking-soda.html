Baking Powder vs Baking Soda — When to Use Which (and Why Some Recipes Use Both)

They're both white powder, both make things rise, and they're absolutely not the same thing. Here's what each one actually does, the dead-simple test to check if yours still works, and why your grandma's recipe calls for both.

I once swapped baking powder for baking soda in a batch of biscuits because the canisters looked identical and I was in a hurry. The biscuits came out flat, pale, and had a metallic aftertaste that lingered for an hour. I threw the whole batch out and stared at the two containers on my counter, genuinely angry at how similar they looked.

Turns out they're completely different chemicals that happen to share a shelf. Here's how to never mix them up again — and more importantly, how to know which one a recipe actually needs.

The 30-second version

Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate. It needs acid to work — buttermilk, yogurt, vinegar, lemon juice, brown sugar, molasses, cocoa powder. Without acid, baking soda produces no gas, which means no rise — zero. Instead, it leaves behind unreacted sodium bicarbonate, which tastes metallic and soapy. That aftertaste you can't place? That's baking soda with nothing to react against.

Baking powder is baking soda with the acid already mixed in (usually cream of tartar). It just needs liquid and heat. Most baking powder sold today is "double-acting" — it reacts once when you add liquid, and again in the oven.

That's the whole difference in one paragraph. The rest is how to use that knowledge.

When a recipe uses baking soda

If you see baking soda in an ingredient list, look for the acid. Buttermilk pancakes. Yogurt-based muffins. Vinegar in red velvet cake. Brown sugar and molasses in gingerbread. Natural cocoa powder in chocolate cake. The baking soda is there to react with that specific acid — not just to provide lift, but to neutralize the acidity so the final flavor isn't sour.

A side effect worth knowing: baking soda promotes browning. That's why recipes with baking soda often produce darker, more golden crusts — the alkaline environment speeds up the Maillard reaction. Chocolate chip cookies with baking soda come out deeper brown. Without it, they stay pale.

If your recipe has baking soda but no obvious acid, double-check the ingredient list. Brown sugar counts. So does natural cocoa (not Dutch-process). So does honey. If none of those are present, the recipe might have a typo — it happens more than you'd think. Use our baking powder grams converter if you're double-checking quantities by weight.

When a recipe uses baking powder

Baking powder shows up in recipes that don't have a strong acidic ingredient — vanilla cake, sugar cookies, scones, biscuits made with milk. It provides its own acid, so it works in neutral batters.

"Double-acting" means exactly what it sounds like. The first reaction happens when the powder hits liquid — you'll see tiny bubbles forming in the batter. The second reaction kicks in around 140°F (60°C) in the oven. This two-stage lift is why you can mix a baking powder batter, let it sit for a few minutes, and still get a decent rise — the second reaction hasn't fired yet.

The balanced formula rule — why some recipes use both

This is the one that confused me for years. If baking powder already contains baking soda, why add more? The answer is a principle that professional recipe developers use constantly: match the leavener to the acid in the batter, then supplement with baking powder for the rest of the lift.

Here's how that plays out. A recipe with buttermilk has a fixed amount of acid. That acid can only react with a fixed amount of baking soda — roughly ¼ tsp of baking soda per ½ cup of buttermilk. If the recipe needs more lift than that reaction can provide, you can't just add more baking soda — the extra would go unreacted and ruin the flavor. So you add baking powder, which carries its own acid and provides clean additional lift without touching the flavor.

You see this pattern in buttermilk pancakes, chocolate chip cookies with brown sugar, and some banana breads. The acid is there for flavor and tenderness. The baking soda neutralizes it and contributes browning. The baking powder provides the extra lift without needing more acid. Three ingredients, three distinct jobs.

A practical rule: if a recipe has an acidic ingredient and still calls for baking powder, the soda is handling the acid and the powder is handling the lift. They're not redundant. And if you're scaling a recipe with both leaveners, our teaspoon-to-grams converter for baking powder keeps the ratios right — the balance is fragile at scale.

How to test if yours is still good — in 10 seconds

Both lose potency over time. An open can of baking powder is usually dead within 6–12 months. Baking soda lasts longer — 2–3 years — but absorbs fridge odors and picks up off-flavors. If your baked goods suddenly stopped rising and you didn't change anything else, check your leaveners.

LeavenerHow to TestStill Good If…
Baking soda½ tsp soda + a splash of vinegar or lemon juiceFizzes immediately and aggressively
Baking powder½ tsp powder + 2 tbsp hot waterFizzes and bubbles within 5 seconds

If nothing happens, the can is dead. Throw it out. The test for baking powder is especially useful because you can't tell by looking — dead baking powder looks identical to fresh.

Can you substitute one for the other?

Not directly — but in an emergency, you can approximate:

  • To replace 1 tsp baking powder: mix ¼ tsp baking soda + ½ tsp cream of tartar. Use immediately — the reaction starts on contact with liquid.
  • To replace baking soda: you can't do it with baking powder alone. You'd need about 3× the amount of baking powder to match the lift, but you'd lose the browning and acid-neutralizing effects. The flavor will be off. If a recipe calls for baking soda, find a way to use baking soda.

If you're measuring in grams rather than teaspoons, 1 tsp baking powder = 4.6g. These are small quantities where a scale helps more than spoons.

Quick FAQ

Can I use baking powder instead of baking soda?

Technically yes — but you'll need about 3 times as much, and the result won't taste the same. Baking powder contains only about ⅓ baking soda by weight, so you need more to get the same lift. More importantly, baking powder is pH-neutral — it won't brown your baked goods or neutralize acidic flavors. A cookie made with baking powder instead of soda will be paler, less chewy, and lack the deep caramelized notes that baking soda enables. It'll rise. But it won't be the same cookie.

Why do my cookies taste metallic?

Metallic taste almost always means unreacted baking soda. The soda was added but there wasn't enough acid in the batter to fully neutralize it. Common culprits: using too little brown sugar (which provides the acid), swapping Dutch-process cocoa for natural cocoa (Dutch is pH-neutral and won't react with soda), or simply over-measuring the soda. If it keeps happening with the same recipe, cut the baking soda by ¼ tsp and see if the flavor improves. The fix is small.

What happens if I use too much baking soda?

Too much baking soda does three things simultaneously — all of them bad. First, it leaves a soapy-metallic aftertaste because the excess can't find acid to react with. Second, it over-alkalizes the batter, which weakens gluten structure — the baked good rises fast then collapses because the protein network can't hold the gas. Third, it can turn certain ingredients strange colors: cocoa goes reddish, blueberries turn green. If you've ever seen a chocolate cake with a weird red tint, too much soda is often the reason. Use our teaspoon converter if you're unsure about the measurement — 4.6 grams is very different from a heaping spoonful.

Does baking soda expire?

Yes — but much slower than baking powder. Unopened baking soda lasts about 3 years. Once opened, it absorbs moisture and odors from the air, which degrades its potency over 6–12 months. The fix is the vinegar test described above — 10 seconds, zero cost. If it fizzes hard, it's still good. If it barely bubbles, it'll still work for cleaning but not for baking. Replace the can.

Why does my baking powder say 'aluminum-free' — does that matter?

Standard baking powders use sodium aluminum sulfate as the heat-activated acid. It's safe, but some people detect a faint metallic-bitter note — especially in delicate recipes like sugar cookies or vanilla cake. Aluminum-free baking powder uses monocalcium phosphate instead, which reacts entirely at room temperature with no heat-activated second stage. The trade-off: aluminum-free powder must go into the oven faster after mixing because all the gas releases early. If you're sensitive to off-flavors, switch to aluminum-free. If you've never noticed a problem, the standard kind is fine.
