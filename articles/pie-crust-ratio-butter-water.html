Pie Crust Ratio: Butter and Water by Weight, Not 3-2-1

Almost every pie crust guide opens with the same rule: three parts flour, two parts butter, one part water. I used it for years. It works, and it is also the reason a lot of home pie crusts come out sturdy instead of flaky, because three-to-two puts less fat in the bowl than a tested recipe does, and it hands you one water number where the honest answer is a range. Here is what the weights actually come to, and which of the three ingredients you are supposed to adjust by feel.

⚡ Quick Answer: 100 Flour, 76 Butter, 19–38 Water, 2 Salt

One 9-inch shell: 150 g all-purpose flour, 113 g cold butter (one stick), 29–57 g ice water, 3 g salt. That is the whole formula, and it doubles cleanly.

Fat is the number that moves: 3-2-1 gives you 67 percent fat against the flour. King Arthur's tested all-butter crust is 76 percent. The extra 9 points is the difference between a crust that holds a shape and one that shatters.

Water is the number you cannot fix on paper: 19–38 percent, because flour hydration varies and because butter already carries 16 percent water — one stick quietly adds 18 g.

The one rule worth memorising: one stick of butter per 150 g of flour. It scales up without arithmetic and it lands on the fat percentage that works.

If you are using salted butter, cut the added salt to 2 g per 150 g of flour — about a quarter teaspoon less than the line above, because a stick arrives with roughly 0.3 tsp already in it.

My first twenty pie crusts were made on 3-2-1, because it was the only rule I could remember and it let me skip the recipe. They were fine. They were also consistently a bit tough, and they slumped down the sides of the pan in a way I blamed on my crimping. When I finally weighed a batch from a tested recipe instead of measuring it from memory, the butter came to 227 g against 300 g of flour — not 200 g. Twenty-seven grams is nine percent of the flour. That is not a rounding difference in pastry. It is most of what separates a crust that flakes from one that merely holds.

The water is the opposite problem. Every guide gives you a number, and the number is always wrong for somebody, because flour from a dry winter bag absorbs differently from flour in a humid kitchen, and because butter is not a pure fat — it is about 16 percent water, and that water is in the bowl whether your recipe counts it or not. King Arthur's own all-butter crust lists a quarter to a half cup of ice water, which is a factor-of-two range inside a single tested recipe. They are not being vague. They are being accurate.

The short version: hold the flour at 100, put the butter at 76, treat salt as 2 percent, and let the water land wherever the dough says it should between 19 and 38. Everything below is the arithmetic for each batch size, how to read the dough instead of the spoon, why the fat percentage explains most failures, and what to do when the crust shrinks anyway.

#1 Where 3-2-1 Comes From, and What It Leaves Out

The rule is a French one. Pâte brisée, the unsweetened short crust behind quiche and savoury tarts, is taught as three parts flour to two parts fat to one part water by weight. It is a genuine ratio, it scales, and it is old enough that nobody owns it. As a teaching device it is excellent: three numbers you can hold in your head, and a dough that comes out workable on the first try.

What it encodes is a crust built for structure. Sixty-seven percent fat against the flour is enough to shorten the gluten and give you tenderness, but not so much that the dough fights you on the bench or falls apart in the pan. That is exactly what a quiche needs, because a quiche shell has to hold a custard for an hour without leaking. It is also why 3-2-1 is the version that gets recommended to beginners.

What it leaves out is the direction the last fifty years of American pie baking went. Compare it against a modern tested formula and the gap is all in one column:

FormulaFlourFatWaterFat vs flourWater vs flour
3-2-1 300 g 200 g 100 g 67% 33%
King Arthur all-butter 300 g 227 g 57–113 g 76% 19–38%
Difference same +27 g range +9 pts point → range

Two things are going on in that table, and they are not equally important. The water change is about honesty — 33 percent is inside the 19 to 38 range, so 3-2-1's water is fine, it is just one point on a line that has to be a band. The fat change is a different formula. Nine percentage points more butter is what turns a crust that holds into a crust that shatters, and it is the single highest-leverage edit you can make to a 3-2-1 habit.

I am not going to tell you 3-2-1 is wrong. It makes a real pie crust and it will not embarrass you. I am telling you it is the lean version, so if your crusts have been coming out a little firm and a little flat-tasting, you have been baking the lean version on purpose without knowing it.

#2 The Weights, Worked Out for Each Batch

Here is the formula at 100 : 76 : 19–38 : 2, converted to the amounts you actually weigh. Cups are given because most people still own a cup, but the gram column is the one that repeats.

FlourButter (76%)Ice water (19–38%)Salt (2%)Makes
150 g (1¼ cups) 113 g · 1 stick 29–57 g 3 g · ½ tsp One 9-inch shell
200 g (1⅔ cups) 152 g · 1⅓ sticks 38–76 g 4 g · ⅔ tsp One deep 9-inch or 10-inch shell
300 g (2½ cups) 227 g · 2 sticks 57–113 g 6 g · 1 tsp Two 9-inch shells, or one double-crust pie
400 g (3⅓ cups) 304 g · 2⅔ sticks 76–152 g 8 g · 1⅓ tsp Two shells plus a lid, or three singles

The 150 g line is the one I would write on the inside of a cupboard door, because 150 g of flour and one stick of butter is a coincidence worth exploiting: the two amounts you would reach for anyway are already in the right proportion. It makes exactly one 9-inch shell with a little overhang to crimp, which covers most of what a household bakes.

Two notes on the table. First, cups for flour are approximate by nature — 150 g is 1¼ cups only if you spoon and level, and a cup scooped straight from the bag can run 20 g heavy. Weigh it if the crust matters. Second, if you want a sweet crust for a fruit pie, King Arthur adds 2 tablespoons of sugar per 300 g of flour, which is about 25 g or 8 percent. It browns faster and it is worth having; add it with the salt. The cups to grams guide has the full flour table if you are converting a recipe written in volumes.

#3 Why the Fat Number Is the One That Moves

Fat does two jobs in pastry, and they are not the same job. Mechanically it coats flour proteins before the water can reach them, which physically blocks gluten from forming long strands — that is what "shortening" means, and it is why a high-fat dough stays tender even if you handle it badly. Structurally, the pieces of solid fat that survive mixing become pockets. They melt in the oven, the water inside them flashes to steam, and the steam lifts the dough apart into layers.

Both jobs get better with more fat, up to a point. Push the butter past about 85 percent of the flour and the dough stops holding together and starts behaving like shortbread — delicious, structurally useless for a pie. Under about 60 percent and you are making something closer to a cracker. The usable window is roughly 65 to 80 percent, and 76 is where a shattering crust lives.

The structural job is also where the shrinkage question lives, which I will come back to. For now the point is simpler: when a crust is tough rather than flaky, the fat percentage is the first thing to check, before technique. A 3-2-1 crust handled perfectly is still a 67-percent-fat crust. It will be tender-ish and it will be crisp, but it will not shatter, and no amount of cold butter or gentle folding will add the missing nine points.

#4 Water: Why the Answer Has to Be a Range

Water is the only ingredient in the formula whose correct amount depends on things you cannot see. Flour absorbs differently depending on the wheat, the milling, the season, and how long the bag has sat in a dry cupboard. A winter bag and a summer bag from the same brand can take ten grams differently in a 300 g batch. Humidity in the room does the same thing more slowly.

Then there is the part most formulas quietly ignore: butter is not pure fat. USDA lists unsalted butter at 16.2 percent water, so 113 g of butter brings about 18 g of water into the bowl whether or not anybody writes it down. That is why a dough made with a stick of butter and 29 g of added water can come together at all — the total liquid is closer to 47 g, which is 31 percent of 150 g of flour, right where it should be.

FlourAdded ice waterWater inside the butterTotal liquidTotal vs flour
150 g 29–57 g 18 g 47–75 g 31–50%
300 g 57–113 g 37 g 94–150 g 31–50%
400 g 76–152 g 49 g 125–201 g 31–50%

Read the last column across: the ratio holds steady no matter the batch size, which is what a ratio is supposed to do. What varies is only the added water, and it varies by a factor of two inside the same formula. If you want the single most useful fact in this article, it is that the low end of that range is more often the right answer than the high end. Dry dough can be fixed in ten seconds with a teaspoon of water. Wet dough cannot be un-wet without adding flour, which changes every other ratio in the bowl.

#5 Judging Water by Feel, Not by the Spoon

Recipes tell you to add water a tablespoon at a time until the dough "just holds together." That phrase is doing a lot of work and it is worth unpacking into something you can check against.

Squeeze test. Take a handful and press it. If it holds as a lump with a few cracks at the edges, stop. If it falls apart, add a teaspoon of water, toss, and squeeze again. If it holds but leaves half the lump stuck to your palm, you have gone past it.

Bowl test. Tip the bowl. Dry flour and crumbs should still be loose at the bottom even when the rest has clumped — those last crumbs get picked up when you press the dough into a disc, and that is exactly the margin you want. A bowl that is completely clean means you added too much.

Visual test. You should see butter. Not a uniform sandy mixture with everything blended in, but streaks and flat shards running through the flour, ranging from pea-sized down to coarse meal. That mixture of sizes is what produces layers of different thickness, and it is the thing most often destroyed by over-mixing.

Crack test. Pinch off a piece and press it flat. The edge should look ragged. A clean, smooth, elastic edge means the gluten has been worked and the dough will fight you when you roll it.

The three failures all have one-step fixes. A dough that will not hold wants water, a teaspoon at a time. A dough that is sticky and greasy went too far, and the repair is a tablespoon of flour plus thirty minutes in the fridge, not more flour alone. A dough that crumbles when rolled has usually been under-watered and over-chilled; let it sit five minutes on the counter and roll from the centre outward rather than pressing down.

#6 Salt: Two Percent of the Flour

Salt in pastry is not seasoning in the decorative sense. Under-salted pie crust tastes like nothing, and because a crust is mostly fat and starch with no sugar to carry it, "a pinch" is not enough. King Arthur's tested all-butter crust uses 1 teaspoon of table salt for 300 g of flour, which is 6.1 g, or 2.0 percent. Their technique guide quotes half a teaspoon per cup of flour, which works out nearer 2.5 percent. Both are inside the range that tastes right, and the difference between them is below what most people can detect in a blind test.

Two practical points matter more than the exact figure. Use fine salt, not flaky. Pie dough contains so little liquid that a coarse crystal has nothing to dissolve it, so it survives into the baked crust as a salty speck. Fine table salt disperses through the flour in the first toss.

If your butter is salted, subtract. Salted butter carries about 0.3 teaspoon of salt per stick, which is 1.85 g, and King Arthur's rule of thumb is to drop a quarter teaspoon of added salt for every half cup of salted butter. At the 150 g / one-stick size, that takes the added salt from 3 g down to about 1.5 g. Salt levels vary by brand and are not printed on the wrapper, so if you care, read the sodium line on the panel and do the conversion. The full arithmetic — including why a quarter teaspoon rather than a third — is in salted vs unsalted butter.

And if you measure salt by volume, know which salt you own. A teaspoon of Diamond Crystal kosher holds about 2.8 g against 6.1 g for table salt, so the same spoon delivers less than half the salt. That is not a small error in a dough this lean.

#7 Which Flour, and Why All-Purpose Wins

Flakiness depends on limited gluten, so the instinct is to reach for the lowest-protein flour on the shelf. That instinct is half right. King Arthur's own guidance is the most useful thing written on this, because they sell both products and still tell you to use all-purpose: pastry flour at 8 percent makes a more tender crust, but it is harder to roll without cracking and it can split at the seams while the pie bakes.

The numbers behind the choice:

FlourProteinIn pie crust
All-purpose11.7%The default. Rolls without fighting, browns well, forgives a heavy hand.
Pastry flour blend10.3%A little more tender, a little less forgiving.
Pastry flour8.0%Tenderest, and the most likely to tear or split. Not a beginner's flour.
Bread flour12.7%Wrong tool. More gluten means tough, and tough is the one thing a crust cannot be.
Cake flour10.0%Chlorinated, so it behaves oddly with fat and gives a crumbly rather than flaky result.

If you only have bread flour, do not make pie. Make bread. Substituting it and adding more fat to compensate produces a crust with the texture of a cracker that has been left out. #8 Butter, Shortening, or Both

The fat you choose changes two things at once: how the crust tastes and how it behaves on the way into the oven. The trade is almost entirely about melting point.

FatMelts nearFlakinessFlavourWorks like
Butter90–95 °F / 32–35 °CHigh, shattersBestSoftens fast, slumps in the oven
Shortening~117 °F / 47 °CModerate, tenderNoneStable, holds crimps, hard to ruin
Lard100–110 °F / 38–43 °CHighestDistinct, savouryBetween the two
Half butter, half shorteningMixedHighGoodMost forgiving blend

This is why all-butter crusts lose their crimp and shortening crusts keep it. Butter is already softening at 90 °F, and an oven is 400. The fat at the edge of your crust gives up its shape before the structure around it has set, so the pretty fluted rim relaxes into a slope. Shortening, twenty degrees further from melting, sets first and holds.

King Arthur's test kitchen recommends the blend for exactly this reason, and American bakeries use it widely. My own habit is all butter for a fruit pie where the crust is competing with the filling for attention, and a 50/50 blend for a decorative top crust or a custard pie where the shape has to survive the bake. If you go all-butter, freeze the butter for fifteen minutes before cutting it in, and chill the assembled shell for thirty minutes before it goes in the oven. That buys back most of the stability.

Whatever fat you use, it must be cold and it must stay cold. Cold butter stays plastic, and plastic butter holds its shape in the flour until the oven melts it into steam. Softened butter smears into the flour during mixing and never forms pockets, which is a mealy crust, which is a shortbread texture, which is not pie. The mechanism, including what happens to creaming when butter is too warm, is in room temperature butter vs cold.

#9 Resting, Rolling and Fitting

Rest. One hour in the fridge is the floor, two is better, and overnight is better again. The rest does two things: it lets gluten strands relax so the dough rolls without springing back, and it re-firms the butter so the pieces stay discrete. This is not a food-safety step and it is not optional. A dough rolled immediately after mixing will shrink, every time.

Shape before you chill. Press the dough into a flat disc, not a ball. A disc rolls into a circle; a ball rolls into a shape with thick corners. If you know which pan you are using, shape the disc to match it.

Thickness. One eighth of an inch, 3 mm. Thinner tears and cannot hold a filling; thicker than a quarter inch bakes unevenly and the centre stays pale while the edge scorches. If you roll between two sheets of parchment, a pair of chopsticks or the rings on a rolling pin will hold the thickness for you.

Size. For a 9-inch pan, roll to 12 or 13 inches. You need about an inch of overhang to crimp and another inch of slack so you are not stretching the dough to reach the rim.

Fit by lifting, never by stretching. This is the step where most crusts are lost. Roll the dough loosely around the pin, lift it over the pan, and unroll it, then ease it down into the corners with your knuckles. If you pick the dough up and pull it to fit the pan, you have loaded it like a rubber band, and it will contract in the oven no matter how well you rested it.

Chill again, briefly. Fifteen to thirty minutes in the freezer after fitting, before trimming and crimping, sets the shape and makes the final trim cleaner. It also means the crimp survives.

#10 Why Crusts Shrink

Shrinkage gets blamed on the oven and on the pan, and it is almost always one of three things in the dough. In order of how often they are the real cause:

Not enough rest. Gluten that has not relaxed is elastic, and elastic means it retracts. An hour in the fridge is the fix, and it is the cheapest one you have.

Stretching the dough into the pan. Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the one that happens after you have done everything else right. Easing the dough in costs four seconds and saves the pie.

Too little fat. This is where the ratio comes back. Fat physically separates flour proteins, so a dough with 67 percent fat has more connected gluten than one with 76 percent, and more connected gluten pulls harder. If you have been baking 3-2-1 and fighting shrinkage, the two facts are related. Adding the missing butter is a structural fix, not a technique fix.

Two smaller contributors. A very high oven can melt the fat before the structure sets, so the crust slumps and then contracts; 400 to 425 °F is high enough, and beyond that you are racing. And pastry flour, being low in gluten, sounds like it should shrink less, but the dough is also weaker and more prone to tearing at the seams, which reads as failure even when the shrinkage itself is mild.

#11 Blind Baking Without Guesswork

Blind baking means baking the shell empty, and it exists for two reasons: a wet filling will soak an unbaked crust into a soggy layer, and a custard needs a shell that is already set so it does not turn the bottom into a sponge. The technique is not hard; the confusion is that published temperatures run from 375 to 425 °F, which is a 50-degree spread and enough to make you think the number matters more than it does.

What matters is the two stages. Everything below assumes a 9-inch shell, docked with a fork.

StageTemperatureTimeWhat it is doing
1. Weighted425 °F / 220 °C15 minSets the shape before the fat can slump. Parchment plus weights, dried beans or rice.
2. Unweighted375 °F / 190 °C10–15 minDries the base and browns it. Pull it when the centre stops looking wet.
Fully baked, for a no-bake filling375 °F20–25 minGolden all over, including the centre. Let it cool completely before filling.
Par-baked, for a wet filling425 → 375 °F15 + 5 minSet but pale. The filling finishes the job.

If your recipe says 400 °F for the whole thing, that works too — it is a compromise between the two stages, and it costs you a little shape and a little browning. The stage structure is the part that matters.

One extra step worth the thirty seconds: brush the inside of a warm par-baked shell with lightly beaten egg white and put it back for two or three minutes. The protein sets into a film that keeps a wet filling from soaking through — the standard insurance against a soggy bottom on pumpkin and custard pies. For a fruit pie, a thin dusting of flour and sugar, or fine breadcrumbs, does the same job by absorbing juice before it reaches the pastry.

Two of those rows are worth more than the rest. The butter-size row is the answer to most "why is my crust not flaky" questions, and the stretching row is the answer to most "why did it shrink" questions — both of which get blamed on the recipe when the recipe was fine.

One last note on storing. A disc of dough wrapped in film keeps three days in the fridge and three months in the freezer, and it is genuinely better after a week than on the day it was made, because the flour has had time to hydrate fully and the dough rolls out more evenly. Making a double batch and freezing half is the single cheapest improvement available to a pie habit. The timings for dough and for finished pies are in how to freeze cake and baked goods.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the ratio for pie crust?

By weight, a workable all-butter crust runs about 100 parts flour, 76 parts butter, 19 to 38 parts ice water, and 2 parts salt. For a single 9-inch shell that is 150 g flour, 113 g butter, 29 to 57 g ice water and 3 g salt. The widely quoted 3-2-1 rule, which is 100 parts flour to 67 parts fat to 33 parts water, sits inside that water range but about nine percentage points low on fat. It makes a crust that is sturdier and less flaky, not a wrong one.

How much water goes into pie crust?

Between 19 and 38 percent of the flour weight, which is 29 to 57 g per 150 g of flour. The range is wide because flour absorbs differently from bag to bag and because butter already carries about 16 percent water, so 113 g of butter quietly supplies another 18 g. Add water a teaspoon at a time and stop when a squeezed handful holds together without crumbling and the bowl has only dry crumbs left at the bottom. Under-watered dough cracks when rolled. Over-watered dough is sticky, shrinks, and bakes up tough.

Why did my pie crust shrink?

Three causes, in order of how often they are the real one. Too little fat leaves enough gluten connected to pull the crust in as it bakes. Too little rest means the gluten never relaxed, so one hour in the fridge is not optional. And stretching the dough to fit the pan, rather than lifting and easing it in, pre-loads the dough like a rubber band so it retracts in the oven. Pastry flour and under-fat doughs shrink more, which is why 3-2-1 crusts are prone to it.

Can I use all butter instead of shortening?

Yes, and the flavour is the reward. The cost is handling. Butter melts around 90 to 95 F, so it softens while you work and it slumps in the oven, which is why crimped edges lose their shape and all-butter crusts shrink more than ones cut with shortening. Shortening melts near 117 F and holds its shape. A half-and-half fat blend, which many American bakeries use, keeps most of the flavour and most of the structural stability at the same time.

How long should pie dough rest?

At least one hour in the refrigerator, and two is better. The wait does two jobs: it lets gluten strands relax so the dough rolls without fighting back and shrinks less, and it re-firms the butter so the pieces stay discrete and turn to steam in the oven instead of soaking into the flour. The dough keeps two days refrigerated and three months frozen as a flat disc, and it rolls better after a week than on the day it was made because the flour has hydrated fully.

🥧 The Pie Crust Check, In Five Questions

Answer these in order. Each one has a number attached, and the first that fails is the one to fix.

  1. How much flour? 150 g per 9-inch shell. Weigh it — a scooped cup runs 20 g heavy.
  2. Is the butter 76 percent of that? 113 g per 150 g of flour. If you are using 3-2-1, you are nine points short.
  3. Did the water start at 19 percent? 29 g per 150 g of flour, then add by the teaspoon. Under-shoot on purpose.
  4. Is there 2 percent salt, and is it fine? 3 g per 150 g of flour. Subtract if the butter is salted.
  5. Will it rest an hour? Not optional, not shortened, and the freezer countdown starts after it is fitted to the pan.

If all five pass: you have a dough at 76 percent fat, 31 to 50 percent total liquid, and 2 percent salt, which is the formula behind every shattering crust worth eating. Anything that goes wrong after that is technique, and technique is easier to fix than arithmetic.

If you take one thing from this article: one stick of butter per 150 g of flour, and start the water at 29 g. That is a 9-inch shell at 76 percent fat, and it is the number that turns a workable crust into a flaky one.

Second thing: the water is the only ingredient you judge by hand, and the low end of the range is right more often than the high end. A dry dough takes ten seconds to fix. A wet one cannot be fixed without changing the formula.

Third, if your crust shrank: check the stretching before you check the oven. Most crusts are lost at the moment they are pressed into the pan, not in the bake.
