The Windowpane Test — How to Know Your Dough Is Kneaded Enough

You've been kneading for ten minutes. Is it done? The windowpane test answers that in 15 seconds — no timers, no guesswork, no "it feels about right." Here's how to read what your dough is telling you.

I under-kneaded bread for two years. Every loaf was fine. Edible. Even good, if you weren't paying close attention. But the crumb was always a little tight, the oven spring a little modest, and I had no idea why. I thought that was just how homemade bread looked.

The problem was simple: I didn't know how to tell when the dough was ready. The recipe said "knead until smooth and elastic." Smooth I could see. But elastic — how elastic? I'd poke the dough, watch it bounce back, shrug, and move on. Half the time I was wrong.

Then a baker I was staging with ripped off a piece of my dough, stretched it between her fingers, held it up to the light, and said: "See? Not there yet. Three more minutes." She was right. Three minutes later the same test produced a thin, translucent sheet — like stained glass made of dough. The bread from that batch rose higher than anything I'd made before.

That was the windowpane test. It took 15 seconds. It changed everything.

What the windowpane test actually is

The windowpane — bakers also call it the "gluten window" — is a direct visual check on gluten development. You pinch off a piece of dough, stretch it between your fingers, and look at what happens.

If it stretches into a thin membrane you can see light through — without tearing — the gluten network is fully formed. The dough is kneaded enough.

If it tears right away, it's not ready. Keep kneading and test again.

No timers. No "it should feel like an earlobe" (a saying I've never understood). No guessing based on how many minutes the recipe said. You look at the dough, and the dough tells you.

Why it works — the 30-second science version

Flour contains two proteins that matter for bread: glutenin and gliadin. Dry, they're inert. Add water and start working the dough, and they link into long, stretchy chains — gluten. Those chains form an elastic network that traps CO₂ from yeast fermentation. That network is your bread's structure. It determines rise, crumb, chew, everything.

Kneading aligns those protein chains and builds the network. Under-knead and the chains are short, disorganized, weak — gas leaks out, loaf is dense. Knead correctly and the chains are long, aligned, elastic — gas stays trapped, loaf rises tall.

The windowpane test physically demonstrates whether that network exists yet. You're not testing "dough softness" or "stickiness." You're testing one thing: can this dough hold gas under tension?

How to do it

  1. Stop kneading. Pinch off a golf-ball-sized piece of dough.
  2. Flatten it slightly between your fingers into a small disc.
  3. Stretch it gently outward using the thumbs and forefingers of both hands. Rotate as you go — picture opening a tiny curtain from the center outward.
  4. Keep stretching until light passes through the center of the stretched membrane.

That's the test. You're looking for a thin, even sheet — translucent but not so thin that it's about to disintegrate. The dough should hold the stretched position without snapping back or tearing.

If your hands are dry and the dough is sticking to them, wet your fingers slightly. If the dough tears before you can get it thin enough to see light through, it needs more kneading.

How often should you test?

Don't perform the windowpane test every 30 seconds — you'll tear up your dough and drive yourself crazy. Space your tests sensibly:

  • Hand kneading: Test every 3–5 minutes. Most lean doughs reach full gluten in 10–15 minutes by hand, so you'll test 2–3 times total.
  • Stand mixer: Test every 2 minutes. A dough hook on medium speed develops gluten faster — 6–8 minutes total is typical — so checking every 2 minutes keeps you from blowing past the window.
  • After autolyse: If you've let the dough rest 10–20 minutes before kneading (see below), test at minute 5, then minute 8. The autolyse does half the work for you.

The dough gets slightly stickier and warmer as gluten develops — you'll learn to feel the windowpane approaching. But until that becomes second nature, a test every few minutes is cheap insurance.

Reading the results

What you see in that stretched membrane tells you exactly where you are:

ResultMeaningAction
Tears immediately, zero stretchUnder-kneaded — gluten hasn't started formingContinue kneading, test again in 3–5 min
Stretches some, then tears with a ragged edgeAlmost ready — network is building but not continuousKnead 3–5 more min, then test again
Stretches into a thin, even sheet. Light shines through. It holds.Ready — gluten is fully developedStop kneading. Proceed with the recipe.
Stretches paper-thin with almost no resistance. Dough feels slack.Over-kneaded — gluten strands have started breakingReduce mixing time next batch. This loaf may still bake OK.

A deeper read on each result:

  • Tears immediately: You're maybe 2 minutes into kneading. For a same-day loaf, keep going. For a no-knead recipe with a long ferment, this is fine — time will do the work.
  • Ragged tear: The gluten chains are forming but they're still short and tangled. Three to five more minutes of kneading and you'll likely hit the windowpane.
  • Clean windowpane: This is your target for sandwich loaves, dinner rolls, baguettes, pizza dough — any standard wheat-based yeast bread.
  • Slack and paper-thin: This is hard to do by hand (you'd need 20+ minutes of aggressive kneading) but a stand mixer on high can reach this point in under 10 minutes. The dough won't hold its shape well during proofing.

When the test misleads you

The windowpane test assumes a standard wheat-based yeast dough. Step outside that box and the rules change:

Enriched doughs — brioche, challah, cinnamon rolls

Butter and egg yolks coat gluten strands and physically interfere with development. You will not get a clean windowpane right after mixing an enriched dough, no matter how long you knead. Don't panic. Test after the first rise instead — gluten continues developing during fermentation, and enriched doughs catch up during the bulk proof. This is also true for doughs heavy in oil or sugar. If you've made brioche before and found the dough oddly "shaggy" after 15 minutes of mixing, this is why. It's not you. It's the fat. See our butter temperature guide — butter state matters even more in enriched doughs than in lean ones.

High-hydration doughs — ciabatta, focaccia, above 75% water

These doughs are wet and sticky. You'll struggle to get a clean stretch with dry hands — wet your fingers first. The membrane will be thinner, more fragile, and the test is harder to read. But the principle holds: if you can stretch it thin enough to see light through, the gluten is there. If you're working with hydration ratios you're unsure about, our baker's percentage guide covers how to calculate and adjust water content by weight.

Rye, spelt, and gluten-free doughs

Don't bother with the windowpane test here. Rye has gliadin but almost no glutenin — you will never get a stretchy membrane from a 100% rye dough no matter what you do. These doughs rely on pentosans (a type of carbohydrate) for water absorption and structure, not gluten. Spelt has gluten but it's fragile and tears easily. Gluten-free flours have none at all. The windowpane test is a wheat thing. For everything else, you go by feel, time, and experience.

Hand kneading vs. stand mixer — rough timing

By hand, most lean doughs (flour, water, salt, yeast) reach full gluten development in 10–15 minutes of steady kneading. With a stand mixer and dough hook on medium speed — roughly speed 4 on a KitchenAid — it's more like 6–8 minutes.

One trick that cuts kneading time by roughly a third: mix until the dough barely comes together, then walk away for 10–20 minutes before you start kneading. This rest — bakers call it autolyse — lets the flour fully hydrate without mechanical work. Gluten starts forming on its own. When you come back and knead, the dough is already halfway there. After the rest, test at minute 5, then minute 8. You'll hit the windowpane faster and with less effort.

If you're scaling a bread recipe up or down, weigh your flour. A cup of bread flour weighs about 130 grams — noticeably more than all-purpose — and that difference matters when hydration ratios are tight. See our flour measuring guide if you're still scooping from the bag.

Do professional bakers always use the windowpane test?

Not always — and this is worth understanding. In a production bakery, a baker making the same dough five days a week develops a feel for it. They know the dough is ready by how it pulls away from the sides of the mixer, how it feels under their hands, how much resistance it gives when they tug a corner. They don't need to stretch a windowpane every batch because they've already done it a thousand times.

But — ask that same baker to troubleshoot a new flour, a new hydration level, or a dough they've never made before, and many of them will reach for a windowpane. It's the most direct, least ambiguous test there is. You don't need to have mixed ten thousand loaves to read it.

The windowpane test is not a beginner crutch. It's the easiest, most reliable way to verify gluten development. Masters may not always need it. But nobody outgrows it.

Quick FAQ

Can you over-knead dough?

By hand — almost impossible. You'd need to knead aggressively for 20+ minutes to break down gluten to the point where the dough loses structure. Your arms will give out first. By stand mixer on high speed — yes, it's possible in under 10 minutes. The dough will go from smooth and elastic to slack, sticky, and unable to hold shape. If this happens, the loaf won't rise properly, but it'll still make decent flatbread or pizza. Just reduce the mixing time on the next batch.

Does sourdough pass the windowpane test?

Yes — eventually. Because sourdough ferments longer than commercial yeast dough, gluten develops partly through kneading and partly through time. If you perform the test right after mixing, a sourdough may not show a clean windowpane. Test again after the first stretch-and-fold or 30 minutes into bulk fermentation; the gluten network strengthens as fermentation proceeds. With sourdough, the windowpane test is most useful mid-fermentation, not right after mixing.

Does whole wheat dough pass the windowpane test?

It can — but it's harder to read, and it tears sooner. Whole wheat flour contains bran and germ particles that physically cut through gluten strands as you knead. You'll rarely get a perfectly translucent membrane from a 100% whole wheat dough. What you're looking for is a stretchy, slightly mottled sheet — not crystal clear, but still thin enough to let some light through and strong enough to not tear immediately. For 50/50 whole wheat to bread flour blends, the test works more cleanly.

Can I skip the test?

You can — plenty of home bakers do, and their bread turns out fine. But skipping the test means you're guessing. The cost of guessing wrong is a dense loaf with poor oven spring. The test takes 15 seconds. If you're happy with your bread, skip it. If you've ever been disappointed by a loaf that didn't rise the way you wanted, run the test once. You'll know in 15 seconds whether kneading was the problem or something else.

Does the test work with enriched doughs like brioche?

Yes, but not right after mixing. Butter, eggs, and sugar interfere with gluten development during kneading — the fat coats the strands and blocks them from linking. Test enriched doughs after the first rise instead, when fermentation has given the gluten time to catch up. See the enriched doughs section above for details.

📋 Windowpane Test — Quick Cheat Sheet

  • Golf-ball-sized piece of dough, stretched between thumbs and forefingers
  • Stretches thin enough to see light through, doesn't tear = done kneading
  • Tears immediately, no stretch = keep kneading, test again in 3–5 min
  • Stretches some then tears ragged = almost there, 3 more min
  • Dough too sticky to stretch? Wet your hands — don't flour them
  • Enriched dough (butter, eggs)? Test after first rise, not after mixing
  • Rye or gluten-free? Skip the test — it won't work
  • No-knead / overnight dough? Time did the work — test anyway to confirm

The windowpane test doesn't make you a better baker. It makes you a more confident one. You stop checking the clock. You stop second-guessing the recipe. You knead until the dough tells you it's ready, and then you stop — not a minute before, not a minute after.

That shift — from following instructions to reading the dough — is the difference between someone who bakes and a baker.

Next time you're making bread, rip off a piece and stretch it toward the light. You'll know in 15 seconds. And once you've done it a few times, you'll start to notice the windowpane forming earlier in the knead — you'll see the gluten developing in real time. That's when it clicks.
