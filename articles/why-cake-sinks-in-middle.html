Why Your Cake Sinks in the Middle — 7 Common Causes & How to Fix Each One

Seven specific reasons cakes collapse — with exact fixes. Not baking theory. Stuff I've actually run into in my own kitchen.

If you've had a cake collapse on you, you know the feeling. You pull what looks like a perfect bake out of the oven. Golden top. Springs back when you press it. Ten minutes later — crater. Right down the middle. No warning.

I've been baking long enough now that I can usually spot what went wrong, but there was a time when every sunken cake felt like a personal failure. It's not.

Cakes sink for specific, mechanical reasons. Each one is fixable. Once you recognize which one got you, that particular mistake is off your list forever.

These seven I've hit personally. More than once, in a couple of cases.

1. You opened the oven door too early

This is the one that got me. The cake was almost done, and I wanted to check. So I cracked the oven door.

Cold air hit the oven. The temperature dropped maybe 40°F in ten seconds. The cake — which was just at the point where its structure was setting — couldn't handle the shock. The middle collapsed.

The fix: Don't open the oven door until at least 75% of the bake time has passed. For an 8-inch cake that bakes 35 minutes, that means no peeking before minute 26. If you absolutely must check, use the oven light.

2. Underbaking — the "golden on top, raw inside" trap

Top was brown. Toothpick came out clean. Must be done, right?

Not necessarily. Toothpicks can lie — especially with chocolate cakes where melted chocolate chips coat the toothpick and look "clean." What actually happened was the outside set, the inside hadn't, and the whole thing collapsed as it cooled.

The fix: Use a thermometer. Cakes set at around 200–210°F (93–99°C) internally. If your instant-read says 195 in the center, give it 5 more minutes. If it's a large cake (10-inch or bigger), tent foil over the top if it's browning too fast — but don't pull it early.

3. Too much leavening (baking powder / baking soda)

A baker I know doubled a vanilla cake recipe. Doubled the flour, doubled the sugar, doubled the butter. Then doubled the baking powder again on top of that — because the can said "1 tsp per cup of flour" and she did the math.

Her cake rose like a hot air balloon in the first 20 minutes. Then the whole structure gave out. The middle collapsed so hard it was concave, like a soup bowl. I saw the photo. I believed it.

What happened: Too much leavening creates gas faster than the gluten structure can hold it. The air pockets burst, and the whole thing collapses.

The fix: The ratio is roughly 1 to 1.25 teaspoons of baking powder per cup of flour — use our baking powder grams calculator to nail the exact weight, because a heaped vs level teaspoon of baking powder can differ by over a gram. If you're scaling a recipe, scale the original amount — don't recalculate based on generic rules. Also, if you're at high altitude (above 3,000 ft), reduce leavening by about 15–20%.

4. Wrong oven temperature — usually too hot

Most home ovens are off by 10 to 25 degrees. Mine was running 15°F hot for who knows how long before I checked.

If your oven is too hot, the outside of the cake sets and browns quickly while the center is still liquid. The trapped steam in the middle tries to push up, can't escape through the crusted top, and creates a dome that looks done — then sinks as it cools.

The fix: Get an oven thermometer. Place it dead center in your oven and compare what it reads to the dial. If your oven says 350 but the thermometer says 375, you now know to set it to 325 for cakes. Check every few months — ovens drift. If you're scaling a recipe up or down while you're at it, use the flour-to-grams converter to keep your dry-to-wet ratios intact.

Also — convection vs conventional matters. If a recipe was written for a conventional oven and you're using convection, reduce temperature by 25°F. Convection circulates hot air and bakes faster.

5. Overmixing the batter (this one is sneaky)

Once you add flour to wet ingredients, every stir develops gluten. Some gluten is good — it gives cake structure. Too much, and you get a dense, rubbery crumb that can't expand properly during baking. The cake rises, strains against the tight gluten, and collapses in the middle.

The fix: When the recipe says "mix until just combined," it means just combined. Stop the mixer the moment you can't see streaks of flour. Finish folding by hand with a spatula if you need to. For sponge and chiffon cakes where you fold in whipped egg whites: fold gently, use a figure-8 motion, and stop as soon as the whites are no longer visible.

6. Wrong pan size (this one is math, not skill)

Used an 8-inch pan for a recipe written for a 9-inch. That doesn't sound like a big difference — it's only 1 inch. But pan volume scales with area, not diameter.

An 8-inch round holds about 20% less batter than a 9-inch. Pour the same amount of batter into a smaller pan, and it's now twice as deep. The center takes way longer to bake than the recipe's stated time. The outside overcooks, the middle stays liquid, and — you guessed it — collapse.

The fix: If you must substitute pan sizes, fill pans to no more than 2/3 full. Any extra batter goes into a cupcake liner or a ramekin. Use our pan size converter to get the exact scale factor and bake time adjustment for your specific pan swap. A general rule: if you go down a pan size, add 5–8 minutes and lower the temperature by 10°F.

7. Taking the cake out of the pan too soon

The cake was fully baked. It looked perfect resting on the cooling rack. Then I tried to flip it out of the pan after 5 minutes. Half the top stayed in the pan.

Cakes are fragile right out of the oven. The crumb structure is still setting. If you unmold too early, mechanical force literally tears the middle apart.

The fix: Let the cake cool in the pan on a wire rack for 10–15 minutes. Then run a knife around the edge, invert onto the rack, and let it cool completely before frosting. For delicate cakes (chiffon, angel food), cool them upside down in the pan — yes, literally flip the whole thing over — to prevent the crumb from compressing.

📋 Quick Checklist — Save This

  • Oven thermometer in place and confirmed accurate
  • Baking powder measured exactly (don't eyeball) — use a teaspoon-to-gram converter for precision
  • Batter mixed until just combined — then stop
  • Pan filled no more than 2/3 full
  • Oven door closed for first 75% of bake time
  • Instant-read thermometer says 200°F+ in center
  • Cooled in pan 10–15 min before unmolding

Some cakes sink a little and it doesn't matter. Pound cakes and butter cakes often settle a tiny dip in the center because the top crust is heavier than the crumb below. That's normal. Frost over it.

The problem is when it sinks unevenly or more than about half an inch. That's a signal — one of the seven things above went wrong.

Next time it happens, don't take it personally. Diagnose it.
