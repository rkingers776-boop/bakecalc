How to Tell When Bread Is Done: Internal Temperature for Every Loaf

The crust goes brown on a schedule that has almost nothing to do with the middle of the loaf. That is the entire reason bread looks finished twenty minutes before it is.

Every baker has done this once. The loaf came out of the oven the colour of good toast, the kitchen smelled like a bakery, you tapped the bottom and it made a convincing noise. You waited, maybe not long enough. Then you cut into it and the middle was damp and heavy, with a faint sheen on the crumb that tells you the starch never finished setting.

The frustrating part is that nothing you did was obviously wrong. You followed the time. You followed the colour. The problem is that both of those signals are measurements of the outside of the loaf, and the outside of a loaf is a completely different environment from the inside. The crust is a thin layer of dough that has been sitting in 450°F air for half an hour and has had its surface water stripped away. The center is a mass of wet dough surrounded by more wet dough, and it cannot get hotter than the water inside it allows. Those two things are never going to agree, and once you accept that, the whole business of judging a loaf collapses down to one number.

This article is about that number: what it is for each family of bread, why lean dough and butter dough finish nearly twenty degrees apart, where to put the probe so the reading is real rather than decorative, and why the traditional hollow thump is a fine test that people apply to exactly the wrong loaves.

⚡ Quick answer: what temperature is bread done at?

Lean dough — flour, water, salt, yeast — is done at 200–210°F (93–99°C) at the center, with sourdough and rye wanting the top of that range. Enriched dough — butter, milk, eggs or sugar in real quantity — sets earlier, at 180–195°F (82–91°C). Quick breads finish at 200–205°F (93–96°C). Insert the probe from the side into the thickest point, not down through the top, wait until the oven spring is over before you pierce the loaf, and pull it 3–5°F below target because the center keeps climbing after it leaves the oven.

1. Why "done" is not a single number

Start by getting rid of the idea that bread has a doneness temperature the way chicken has one. Chicken has a safety threshold, because pathogens are involved and the number is a legal line that does not care what you think of the texture. Bread has nothing like that. Flour, water and yeast do not carry a pathogen risk that a crumb temperature resolves, and once the crumb has set the loaf is safe to eat whether it reads 190°F or 210°F.

What the number actually measures is texture. It is a dial, not a stop sign. That is why published figures wander so much. ThermoWorks prints one chart showing lean dough at 200–210°F and rich dough at 190–200°F, and a second, longer guide on the same site showing lean dough at 190–210°F and rich dough at 180–190°F. Neither is wrong. They are describing the same dough finished to slightly different crumb structures, and the range is wide enough to hold both.

The most useful piece of evidence here comes from King Arthur Baking, who baked the same sandwich dough twice and pulled one loaf at 190°F and one at 205°F, then cut both through the center. Both were fully baked. There was no raw streak in either. The difference was in the eating: the 190°F loaves were moister and softer, and the 205°F loaves were drier with a thicker crust. They ran the same experiment on a dense whole-grain round, a loaf where the center sits as far from the edge as bread geometry allows, and reached the same conclusion. The 190°F loaf was not gummy or underdone. It was simply a softer loaf, and it stayed softer for longer.

So the honest way to read any bread temperature is this: the low end of a range gives you a softer, moister crumb, the high end gives you a drier, firmer one with a crustier shell, and picking a number inside the range is a preference rather than a correctness test. The one thing you cannot do is guess, because a loaf that looks done can easily be sitting twenty degrees below the bottom of its range.

The fermentation half of the temperature story is a separate question and worth reading alongside this one. What temperature your dough rises at decides how much gas and flavour it develops before it ever reaches the oven, which is why dough temperature is worth controlling from the mixing bowl rather than only at the end.

2. What is actually happening inside the loaf

The reason the reading works at all is that a loaf goes through its phases in a fixed order, and each phase is driven by a temperature band. Read the center and you know which phase you are in.

The first ten to fifteen minutes are about gas. The oven spring is the dough's last expansion: yeast still alive in the outer layers, carbon dioxide from fermentation warming up and pushing outward, steam forming and stretching the gluten network. Yeast activity fades out as the dough passes roughly 140°F, and the window for oven spring closes when the structure stiffens. This is why every guide says to wait before probing. Push a probe into a loaf that early and you are poking a soft, still-expanding dough, which can deflate it and which will not hold the probe steady enough to give a stable reading anyway.

Then the starch sets. Starch granules absorb water and swell, releasing their ordered structure and going rigid — gelatinization. In a bowl of batter that happens relatively low, because there is water everywhere. In a bread dough it happens higher, because water is scarce and much of it is already bound up with flour proteins and gluten. The gelatinization front moves inward from the crust as heat penetrates, and the crumb is "set" when that front has passed through the center. The center is the last place to gel, which is exactly why the center is where you measure.

And the water leaves. This is the part that explains the ceiling on the whole temperature conversation. Evaporating water absorbs a great deal of energy, and as long as the center of a loaf is wet, that evaporation holds it near the boiling point of water. At sea level a loaf's center cannot climb far past 212°F, because any additional heat goes into turning water into steam rather than raising the temperature. The practical result is that the useful measurement window for bread is compressed into a band roughly twenty degrees wide, from about 180°F to about 210°F, sitting just under a hard physical lid. A loaf cannot "overcook" to 250°F. The moisture runs out first and then the crust burns, which is why an overbaked loaf is dry and dark rather than simply hot.

Once you see those three phases stacked in order — gas, then starch, then water leaving — the temperature targets stop looking arbitrary. A lean dough holds the most water and needs the longest trip through the starch-setting phase, so it finishes at the top of the band. A dough carrying fat and sugar has a softer setting point and less free water, so it finishes lower.

3. The internal temperature for each kind of bread

The published ranges disagree by up to ten degrees in places, which is why this chart shows bands rather than single numbers. The bands are the ones that survive across sources: the Home Baking Association's baking temperature chart, which draws on King Arthur Baking, Cook's Illustrated, Rose Levy Beranbaum and Stella Parks, the ThermoWorks baking guide, and King Arthur's own bench tests.

Lean sandwich loaf — 190–200°F (88–93°C). Flour, water, salt, yeast, maybe a spoonful of sugar to feed the yeast. King Arthur Baking treats 190°F as the benchmark for this family and notes that their own loaves are sometimes taken to 194°F to be certain the structure is fully set. Aim low if you want soft slices you can press flat for a sandwich; take it to 200°F if you want a crumb that holds up to a wet filling without going soggy.

Sourdough boule or batard — 200–210°F (93–99°C). Higher hydration means more water to drive off, and a long slow ferment leaves a more acidic dough, both of which push the finish higher. If you want the open, chewy crumb that makes sourdough worth the trouble, work at the top of the band: 205–210°F. Pull a sourdough at 190°F and you will not get food poisoning, but you will get a moister, denser slice than the style is supposed to have, and if you are diagnosing a gummy loaf the reading is the first thing to check — that failure and its fixes are covered in the sourdough troubleshooting guide.

Rye and heavy whole-grain loaves — 205–210°F (96–99°C). Rye flour holds water stubbornly and its starch behaves differently from wheat starch, so the center lags well behind a wheat loaf of the same size. Whole-grain loaves are dense and dark, which defeats crust colour as a cue, and this is the family where a thermometer earns its price fastest. If you are scaling a bread formula and want to know how much of the weight is water, the sourdough hydration calculator turns that into a number before the loaf ever goes in.

Enriched dough — 180–195°F (82–91°C). Brioche, challah, milk bread, Japanese shokupan, soft rolls. Fat and sugar lower the temperature at which the crumb sets and slow heat transfer through the loaf, so these finish earlier than a lean dough and yet have a crust that browns faster. That combination is why enriched bread is the single most common source of the gummy-center complaint: the top looks finished while the middle is twenty degrees short. The tangzhong method is a way of pushing this family the other way, holding moisture in so the crumb stays soft for days without needing to be pulled early.

Quick breads — 200–205°F (93–96°C). Banana bread, pumpkin bread, muffins, cornbread, soda bread. These are chemically leavened and much wetter than a dough, so they behave closer to cake than to bread, and they need the higher finish precisely because that extra water has to go somewhere. Recipes almost always give a time rather than a temperature, so start checking with an instant-read probe at the low end of the stated time range. Gluten-free quick breads want about 5°F more, because the flours hold water differently.

Baguettes and thin loaves — use crust colour instead. A baguette has so little distance between crust and center that the reading stops separating done from overdone, and King Arthur flags baguettes specifically as the one loaf in their test series that did not behave like the rest. Judge these by the colour and the crust, with the thermometer as a loose sanity check rather than the deciding vote.

4. How to probe a loaf so the number means something

A thermometer is only as good as the reading you take, and a reading taken in the wrong place is worse than no reading at all because it gives you confidence you have not earned.

Insert from the side or the end. Not down through the top. King Arthur puts it plainly in their whole wheat loaf recipe: go in through the end or the side, not through the top, if you care about how the loaf looks. There is a technical reason on top of the cosmetic one. A probe entering from above travels through the crust and the outer crumb, which are the hottest parts of the loaf; if the tip stops short of the true center it reports a temperature several degrees higher than the middle actually is, and you pull a loaf that is not ready.

Aim for the geometric center of the thickest part. On a pan loaf that is the middle of the long side, halfway down. On a round boule it is the middle of the dome measured from the base. On rolls it is the center of the roll. The center is the last place to reach temperature, so it is the place that decides when the loaf is finished.

Keep the probe off the pan. The tip touching metal, or sitting in a metal tin's corner, spikes the reading instantly. If you get a number that looks too good, withdraw a few millimetres and read again. A reading that climbs and then falls is also a signal: it means the tip has passed the hottest point, so back it out until you find the highest steady value. That highest steady value is your number.

Wait for the oven spring to finish before you pierce anything. Ten to twenty minutes into the bake, depending on the loaf, the structure has set enough to hold a probe without collapsing. Before that you are measuring soft expanding dough, you risk deflating the loaf, and the probe will not sit still. The Home Baking Association's baking temperature chart makes the same point in its own words: wait until after the oven spring, when the structure is set and beginning to brown, before piercing breads or cakes with a probe so you do not deflate tender doughs.

Pick the right instrument. A thin-tipped digital instant-read thermometer is the default, because a thick probe reads an average of crust and crumb and therefore runs high. A leave-in probe thermometer with an alarm is better still for bread: you insert it once, set the alarm a few degrees below target, and stop opening the oven door to check, which keeps the oven temperature stable and your loaf rising. The old dial thermometers that came with oven doors are too slow to register a center temperature before you have lost the heat you paid for by opening the door.

On a large loaf, take two readings. One at the center, one nearer the base where the crumb is densest and most likely to hide a damp pocket. If the two disagree by more than a few degrees, the lower one is the truth and the loaf goes back in. A bake that was pulled early usually shows it in the bottom third.

If you want to know whether the reading you just took is trustworthy in the first place, that depends on the oven, and that is a separate and very fixable problem: testing your oven's real temperature before you blame the bread is the step that makes every other number on this page mean what it says.

5. The thump test, and the loaves it lies about

Tapping the bottom of a loaf and listening for a hollow ring is the oldest doneness test there is, and it is not nonsense. A hollow thump is the sound of a rigid shell resonating over a free space. When the crust has dried out sufficiently and the crumb beneath it has set, the loaf rings. That is a real physical event and on a country loaf with a properly baked crust it lines up with doneness often enough to have survived centuries.

Where it falls apart is any loaf whose crust never becomes rigid. Butter, milk and sugar keep the crust soft and pliable, and a soft crust does not ring no matter what the center is doing, so on brioche, challah, milk bread, soft rolls and most tender sandwich loaves the test simply encodes nothing. The failure runs the other way too: a lean loaf with a hard, dark crust can ring clearly while its center is still below 180°F and the starch has not finished setting, because the crust hardened long before the crumb did. Whole-grain and heavily seeded loaves add a third problem, since a rough crust absorbs and scatters the sound.

ThermoWorks has been blunt about this for years, calling the hollow thump humbug in the same breath as recommending internal temperature instead. King Arthur takes the gentler line, that if you can reliably judge a loaf by sound then you should carry on, but that a thermometer is easier and more accurate — and that turning a half-baked, blistering hot loaf out of its pan to reach the bottom carries a real risk both to the loaf and to your hand. Cakes have the same problem with the toothpick test, and the answer there is the same one: a positive test that fires too early is worse than no test, because you stop looking.

Use the thump as a rough first pass on a lean crusty loaf, if you enjoy it. Never let it be the deciding vote on a soft-crusted or enriched bread.

6. The other cues, and how much to trust each one

Temperature is the primary measurement, but three secondary cues are worth stacking against it, in roughly descending order of usefulness.

Crust colour and depth. For most yeast breads you want a rich golden brown, not a tan. A pale loaf generally tastes flat and can feel gummy even when the center is technically set, because the browning reactions that build flavour have not run far enough. This is the cue you lean on for baguettes and thin loaves and the cue you largely ignore for dark-pan, whole-grain and Dutch-oven bakes, where a deep crust is the recipe's intent rather than an error signal.

Weight, judged in the hand. A finished loaf feels lighter than it looks, because most of the water you started with has left. It is a surprisingly reliable check once you have handled a few dozen loaves and know what the same recipe feels like at twenty minutes versus forty. It is also useless on your first bake of a recipe, since you have no baseline.

Release from the pan. A pan loaf that has pulled cleanly away from the sides is close to done. The catch is that the pull-away happens when the crust has firmed, which for an enriched loaf can be well before the center is at temperature. Useful as a "start checking now" signal, not as a finish line.

What none of these do is tell you the difference between 190°F and 205°F, which is the difference between a soft slice and a firm one. Colour and weight tell you a loaf is nearly there. Only the number tells you it has arrived, and only the number tells you where in the range it landed.

7. Carryover cooking: pull a few degrees early, on purpose

When a loaf comes out of the oven, the outer crumb is hotter than the center, and heat keeps flowing inward while the loaf sits. The center therefore continues to rise for several minutes after the pan leaves the oven. For a large loaf this carryover can be five degrees Fahrenheit or more, which is why pulling a loaf at exactly its target temperature reliably overshoots it.

The fix is to bake to a number slightly below your target — three to five degrees for a full-size loaf, less for small rolls — and let the rest of the climb happen on the cooling rack. This matters most at the top of a range where you are deliberately aiming for a firmer crumb, since overshooting there is what turns a good loaf into a dry one. It is not a licence to pull a loaf at 175°F and hope. Carryover finishes a bake that is nearly finished. It does not rescue one that is twenty degrees short.

Two habits belong to this same stage of the process. Leave the loaf in its pan for a minute or two before turning it out, which lets the structure firm up so the loaf does not slump or take a dent from the cooling rack — King Arthur suggests a minute for a firm loaf and up to five for a particularly soft one, but warns that longer than that steams the side and bottom crust and makes it leathery rather than tender. And then leave the loaf alone until it is cool. Slicing hot bread lets a large share of the internal moisture escape through the newly exposed face, and the crumb sets in a slightly compressed state and never recovers its full height. A gummy slice from a loaf that hit its target temperature is almost always a slice taken too early.

Cooling is also where the loaf finishes becoming the thing you wanted. The crumb tightens, the crust crisps as residual moisture migrates outward, and the flavour settles. How you handle the loaf after that is its own subject, and the bread storage guide covers what happens next, including why the refrigerator is the worst place in the house for it.

8. Gummy center after cooling: what went wrong and what to do

A damp, heavy center on a fully cooled loaf has three common causes, and they need different responses.

You measured the wrong spot. This is the most common one and the hardest to notice, because the thermometer gave you a confident number. A probe that stopped short of the center, entered from the top, or touched the pan will read high. The loaf gets pulled early and the only symptom is a gummy middle underneath a good crust. The test for it is simple: take a second reading from the side, at the center of the thickest part, before you decide the loaf is done. If the two readings disagree by more than a few degrees, believe the lower one.

The loaf was sliced hot. A warm loaf will feel damp when cut even when it was baked correctly. If the crumb is gummy only near the cut face and the rest of the slice is fine, the temperature was right and the knife was early.

The oven was too hot for the loaf. This is the cause when the crust is already dark or even burnt and the middle is underbaked. The outside set early and insulated the center, or the top browned before heat had a chance to penetrate. Loaves baked in dark metal or glass pans show this more than light metal, because those materials run hotter against the crust than the oven air does. The fix is a lower oven temperature and a longer bake, not a longer bake at the same temperature — and it is worth checking the oven's true temperature first, since a dial that reads 350°F while the oven sits at 400°F will produce exactly this pattern on every bake you make in it.

If the loaf is already out and the center is genuinely underbaked, it can usually be saved. Return it to a 325–350°F oven, tented loosely with foil if the crust is already as dark as you want it, and give it ten to fifteen minutes, checking the center every five. A loaf that has cooled will need longer than one that is still warm, and a loaf that has already been sliced will dry out from the exposed faces while it finishes. Do not microwave a whole loaf in the hope of fixing the middle; you will get a rubbery crumb and a crust that goes from crisp to leathery in under a minute.

9. Altitude, pans, and loaf size: the three things that move the number

The targets on this page assume sea level, a light-coloured metal pan, and a loaf of the size the recipe intends. Change any of those and the numbers shift, and it is worth knowing which direction each one goes, because two of them point opposite ways and that is what confuses people.

Altitude lowers the target. Water boils at a lower temperature the higher you go, roughly two degrees Fahrenheit for every thousand feet, and that lower boiling point pulls the whole measurement band down with it. At 5,000 feet, where water boils near 203°F, a sea-level target of 208°F for a sourdough loaf corresponds to something closer to 199°F. Meanwhile the same rules of altitude also mean you should raise your oven temperature, not lower it, because a hotter oven drives the structure up before it can collapse — which is the part of high-altitude baking that trips people up. Raising the oven setting and lowering the internal target at the same time looks contradictory and is not: one is about the air, the other is about the water inside the loaf. If you are converting between scales or working from a recipe written in Celsius, the oven temperature converter handles the arithmetic.

Pan material changes how fast the crust runs ahead. Dark metal and glass pans hold and radiate more heat into the loaf's surface, so the crust browns earlier and the center lags. When you switch a recipe from a light tin to a dark one, drop the oven temperature about 25°F and expect to add a few minutes. What you must not do is use the darkened crust as your doneness signal, because on a dark pan the crust goes deep brown well before the center reaches target. This is also why a Dutch oven bake behaves differently from the same dough on a stone: the preheated pot blasts heat into the loaf from every side at once, which is superb for oven spring and quite capable of burning the base if you judge by the top.

Loaf size changes the time, not the target. This is the most useful thing ThermoWorks says about bread, and it saves a lot of anxiety when you divide a recipe: resizing a loaf changes how long it takes to get there but does not change the internal temperature it needs to reach. A small roll and a two-pound boule from the same dough are both done at the same center reading; the big one just takes longer to arrive. So if you scale a formula up, keep the target and watch the clock; if you scale it down, keep the target and start checking much earlier.

10. The short version

Bread is done when the center of the crumb has set, and the only convenient way to know that is to read the temperature where the crumb sets last. Lean dough needs 200–210°F, with sourdough and rye at the top of that band. Enriched dough needs 180–195°F. Quick breads need 200–205°F. Insert a thin probe from the side into the geometric center, keep it off the pan, take the highest steady reading, and wait until the oven spring has finished before you pierce anything.

Then adjust for what you want. Choose the bottom of the band for a soft, moist crumb and the top for a firm, chewy one. Pull three to five degrees early and let carryover finish the job. Leave the loaf in its pan for a minute, cool it on a rack, and do not cut it until it is cold. Judge crust colour and weight as cross-checks rather than answers, and treat a hollow thump as a pleasant ritual on a crusty country loaf and as nothing at all on a soft-crusted or butter-rich one.

The equipment that makes all of this work is a single inexpensive thermometer, and it earns its money faster in bread than almost anywhere else in the kitchen, because bread is the one thing you bake where you cannot see the part that decides the outcome. Pair it with a reading of your oven's real temperature and you have the two numbers that explain most of the differences between a loaf that works and a loaf that does not.

Frequently Asked Questions

What internal temperature should bread be when it is done?

It depends on the dough, not on the loaf shape. Lean dough, which is flour, water, salt and yeast, finishes between 200 and 210 degrees Fahrenheit (93 to 99 degrees Celsius) at the center, with sourdough and rye wanting the top of that range and a soft sandwich loaf happy near the bottom. Enriched dough, which carries butter, milk, eggs or sugar, sets earlier, at 180 to 195 degrees Fahrenheit (82 to 91 degrees Celsius). Quick breads, banana bread and muffins land at 200 to 205 degrees Fahrenheit (93 to 96 degrees Celsius). The single number most American recipes print, 190 degrees Fahrenheit, comes from King Arthur Baking and is a reliable benchmark rather than a ceiling: their own side-by-side test found loaves pulled at 190 were fully baked but noticeably moister, while loaves taken to 205 were drier and had a thicker crust.

Why does my bread sound hollow but the inside is still gummy?

Because the thump test measures the crust, not the crumb. A hollow tap tells you the crust has dried out and become rigid enough to resonate. On a lean, crusty loaf with a properly developed crust that happens roughly when the crumb is set, which is why the test earned its reputation. On a butter-enriched loaf the crust stays soft and supple, so it can sound dull rather than hollow for the whole bake and the test gives you no signal at all. The reverse failure is also common: a loaf with a hard crust can ring hollow while the center is still below 180 degrees Fahrenheit and the starch has not set. If a soft-crusted loaf sounds hollow and the center is gummy after cooling, the loaf was underbaked. Take the temperature before you trust the sound.

Can you tell when bread is done without a thermometer?

For a lean crusty loaf, usually yes, if you stack three cues together. The crust should be a deep golden brown rather than tan, the loaf should have pulled away from the sides of the pan, and it should feel lighter than it looks because it has driven off water. For baguettes and other thin loaves there is no practical alternative, since a thin loaf is mostly crust and its center runs up to the boiling point of water so fast that the reading stops distinguishing between done and overdone. For enriched doughs, whole-grain loaves and anything baked in a dark pan, the visual cues are unreliable enough that a thermometer is the only dependable answer. A loaf can look finished while the center is still damp.

Should I take the temperature from the top or the side of a loaf?

From the side or the end, not down through the top. There are two reasons. The cosmetic one is that a top-down probe leaves a visible crater in the most attractive surface of the loaf, whereas a side insertion hides the hole in a cut end. The technical one matters more: a probe entering from above passes through the crust and the outer crumb, which are the hottest parts of the loaf, so if the tip stops short of the true center it reads several degrees high. Side insertion puts the tip on the horizontal line through the middle of the loaf, which is where the center actually is. Aim for the geometric center of the thickest part, keep the probe off the pan because contact with metal spikes the reading, and take the highest steady number as your answer because a reading that climbs and then falls means the tip has moved past the hottest point.

Does bread keep cooking after you take it out of the oven?

Yes, and it is worth planning for. The outer crumb of a loaf is hotter than the center when it comes out, so heat keeps flowing inward as the loaf sits, and the center continues to rise for several minutes. For a large loaf that carryover can be five degrees Fahrenheit or more, which is why pulling a loaf at exactly its target temperature usually overshoots. Set a leave-in probe alarm or take the loaf out three to five degrees Fahrenheit below target and let the rest happen on the rack. The crumb also finishes setting as it cools, which is why slicing a hot loaf gives you a gummy slice even when the temperature was right. King Arthur Baking notes that cutting into hot bread lets a large share of its internal moisture escape through the cut face and that the loaf sets in a compressed state, so it never regains its full height.

One probe, inserted from the side, at the right moment, turns a guess into a number — and once you know your number for a given loaf, you can move it up or down on purpose instead of hoping.
