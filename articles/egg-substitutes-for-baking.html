7 Egg Substitutes for Baking — Which One Actually Works for Your Recipe

No eggs? Seven swaps that work — and three that'll backfire. Exact ratios, which substitute goes with which bake, and the chemistry behind each one.

You crack open the carton and there's one egg left. The recipe needs three.

It happens to every baker eventually. You're either out of eggs, baking for someone with an allergy, or trying to use up the overripe bananas on the counter. Whatever the reason, you need a substitute — and the internet has about forty suggestions, most of them vague and at least half of them wrong.

Here's the thing: an egg isn't one ingredient. It's several. A good substitute has to cover whichever of those jobs the egg was doing in your particular recipe. Nobody tells you that part.

1. Flax Egg — Best for Cookies, Brownies & Hearty Muffins

Ratio: 1 tablespoon ground flaxseed + 3 tablespoons water = 1 egg

A flax egg is the workhorse of vegan baking. Mix ground flax with water, wait 10 to 15 minutes, and you get a gel that looks unsettlingly like egg white and behaves almost like it too.

What it does well: binding. Flax gel holds your dough together the same way egg proteins would. The fat in flax also adds a little richness. What it doesn't do: lift. A flax egg won't help your bake rise, so it's best in recipes where eggs are the glue, not the balloon.

Where it shines: chocolate chip cookies, brownies, oatmeal muffins, pancakes. The nutty flax flavor disappears behind chocolate or cinnamon. In a plain sugar cookie? You'll taste it — not necessarily bad, but noticeable.

Skip it for: sponge cakes, angel food cake, anything that depends on eggs for volume.

2. Unsweetened Applesauce — Best for Cakes & Quick Breads

Ratio: 1/4 cup (60g) unsweetened applesauce = 1 egg

Applesauce is the closest thing to a universal egg substitute for cake batters. It's wet, slightly acidic, and contains pectin — a natural thickener that helps bind the batter together.

The trade-off: applesauce adds moisture but not structure. Your cake will be softer and denser than the egg version. In most cases that's fine — a chocolate cake with applesauce instead of eggs is still a very good chocolate cake. A genoise sponge is not.

One thing people get wrong: they don't adjust the sugar. Applesauce is already sweet, even the unsweetened kind. If your recipe calls for a full cup of sugar, pull back by about 2 tablespoons per egg you're replacing. Otherwise the cake comes out cloying.

Where it shines: layer cakes, banana bread, zucchini bread, carrot cake, brownies.

Skip it for: anything crisp or crunchy — applesauce makes cookies cakey. If you want thin, chewy chocolate chip cookies, applesauce is not your friend.

3. Mashed Banana — Best When the Flavor Already Fits

Ratio: 1/4 cup (about half a medium banana, well mashed) = 1 egg

Banana is the easiest egg substitute — every kitchen has one going brown on the counter. It binds, it moisturizes, and it tastes like banana. That last part is both the appeal and the problem.

In banana bread? Perfect — you were putting banana in anyway. In chocolate chip muffins? Actually works, the chocolate covers most of the banana and what's left reads as "complex." In vanilla cake? You just made banana cake. Which might be delicious, but it's not what the recipe promised.

The texture is heavier than applesauce. Banana is pure starch and sugar — there's no protein at all, so you lose the egg's binding strength. Your bake will be moist but crumbly if you push it too far. Don't replace more than 2 eggs with banana in any recipe.

Where it shines: pancakes, waffles, banana bread (obviously), chocolate muffins, spice cakes.

Skip it for: anything delicate, sugar cookies, white cake, lemon bars.

4. Yogurt or Buttermilk — Best for Dense Cakes & Pound Cakes

Ratio: 1/4 cup (60g) plain yogurt or buttermilk = 1 egg

If applesauce is the generalist, yogurt is the specialist. It brings protein (structure), fat (tenderness), and acidity (reacts with baking soda for lift). That's two of the three things an egg does — the only thing missing is the emulsifying power of the yolk.

In practice, that means yogurt works beautifully in anything thick and moist: pound cakes, coffee cakes, carrot cakes, dense muffins. The final crumb is slightly tighter than the egg version, but still tender. In pancakes, yogurt or buttermilk actually improves the result — the extra tang makes maple syrup taste better.

Use full-fat yogurt. Nonfat yogurt adds water without adding structure and you end up with a gummy center. Greek yogurt is fine but thin it with a splash of milk — it's too thick straight from the tub and won't incorporate evenly.

If you don't have buttermilk on hand either, here's the fix: 3 easy buttermilk substitutes using regular milk plus lemon juice or vinegar.

Where it shines: pound cake, coffee cake, carrot cake, scones, thick pancakes.

Skip it for: meringues, macarons, anything that needs stiff peaks.

5. Aquafaba — The Only Substitute That Whips

Ratio: 3 tablespoons aquafaba (chickpea can liquid) = 1 whole egg. 2 tablespoons = 1 egg white.

Aquafaba sounds like a gimmick. The liquid from a can of chickpeas — whipped into meringue? It shouldn't work. But chickpea cooking water contains proteins and starches that, when beaten, trap air almost exactly like egg whites do. It's the only vegan substitute that can produce genuine foam.

Pour the liquid from a can of unsalted chickpeas into a mixer bowl. Add 1/8 teaspoon of cream of tartar per 1/4 cup of aquafaba. Whip on high for 5 to 8 minutes until stiff peaks form. You now have something that bakes, browns, and tastes remarkably close to egg white meringue.

Aquafaba goes from bean water to stiff peaks in about 6 minutes — it's the only vegan substitute that produces real foam.

The catch: aquafaba collapses faster than egg whites. Fold it gently, get it in the oven immediately, and don't open the door during baking. It's also more fragile in the finished bake — an aquafaba meringue will soften in humidity faster than an egg version. Still, for someone who can't eat eggs, it's a revelation.

Where it shines: meringues, macarons, mousses, angel food cake, anything that depends on whipped egg whites.

Skip it for: cookies and brownies — aquafaba is overkill and messy for simple batters where flax or applesauce would work fine.

6. Carbonated Water — The Weird One That Sometimes Works

Ratio: 1/4 cup (60ml) plain seltzer or club soda = 1 egg

I was skeptical. Water with bubbles — replacing an egg? But here's the logic: the carbonation creates tiny pockets of gas in the batter, simulating the leavening effect of beaten eggs. There's no binding power at all, so this only works in recipes where the flour's gluten can hold things together on its own.

In a boxed cake mix (which is already engineered to work with water), replacing each egg with 1/4 cup of seltzer produces a cake that's genuinely light — lighter than the applesauce version, sometimes lighter than the egg version. In a from-scratch cake recipe, you need to be more careful. The batter has to be thick enough to trap the bubbles. If it's thin, the carbonation fizzes out before the oven sets the structure.

This is the lowest-effort substitute on the list. No prep, no measuring flax or mashing fruit. Just pour and stir. But it's also the least reliable — treat it as an emergency backup, not your go-to.

Where it shines: boxed cake mixes, pancake batter, thin waffle batter.

Skip it for: thick batters (muffins, quick breads), cookies, anything that needs binding.

7. Commercial Egg Replacer — The Predictable Backup

Ratio: follow the package (typically 1½ teaspoons powder + 2–3 tablespoons water = 1 egg)

Bob's Red Mill, Ener-G, and a few other brands sell powdered egg replacer — usually a blend of potato starch, tapioca flour, and leavening agents. Mix it with water, it forms a slurry, you add it to the batter.

It's engineered for consistency, and it delivers. The flavor is neutral, the binding is reliable, and the lift is better than flax or applesauce (the built-in leavening helps). It won't produce anything spectacular — no one will ask for your recipe because of the egg replacer — but it won't ruin anything either.

The downside: it's a specialty product. If you have it in your pantry, use it. If you don't, one of the first four on this list is probably staring at you from the fridge right now.

Where it shines: everything — that's the point.

Skip it for: meringues. Even the commercial stuff can't foam like an egg white.

Three Swaps That Sound Smart but Ruin Your Bake

🛢️ Oil Alone

Zero protein. Won't bind.
Greasy, crumbly result.

💧 Plain Water

Adds moisture, nothing else.
Sunken, gummy center.

🥣 Cornstarch Paste

Binds like wet paper.
Cookies spread flat.

❌ Oil (alone)

"An egg is mostly fat, so oil should work, right?" Wrong. Oil adds moisture and tenderness, but zero protein, zero binding, zero leavening. If you pour 1/4 cup of oil into a cake batter to replace an egg, the cake won't hold together. It'll be greasy, crumbly, and flat. Oil can replace the yolk's fat — but you still need something to replace the white's protein. That's why recipes that use oil as a substitute always pair it with something else (usually baking powder and extra flour).

❌ Plain Water

Water adds moisture and nothing else — no structure, no binding, no air. Your batter looks right going into the pan and comes out a sunken, gummy mess. If you're genuinely out of everything else, use carbonated water (substitute #6 above). At least the bubbles do something.

❌ Cornstarch + Water Paste

Cornstarch and water make a thick slurry that's useful for thickening sauces and puddings — and useless for baking. It has the binding power of wet paper. A cookie made with cornstarch paste will spread into a puddle, and a cake won't rise at all. Skip it.

Two Things Nobody Mentions About Egg-Free Baking

1. You'll Probably Need More Leavening

Eggs provide natural lift through protein coagulation and trapped air. Remove them and your batter is heavier. Adding an extra ¼ teaspoon of baking powder per egg replaced usually compensates — especially when using flax egg or applesauce, which add weight without adding gas.

2. The Bake Time Changes

Egg-free batters are typically wetter than the original. That means they take longer to bake through. Start checking 5 minutes later than the recipe says, and trust the toothpick test more than the timer. A thermometer in the center of a cake should read about 98°C / 208°F when it's done — same as with eggs.

The Bottom Line

Eggs are hard to replace because they do so many jobs at once. The secret isn't finding one perfect substitute — it's matching the right substitute to the specific thing you're baking.

If I had to stock a pantry for egg-free baking, I'd keep three things: ground flaxseed (shelf-stable forever), unsweetened applesauce (the individual snack cups are perfect — one cup is exactly 1/4 cup), and a can of chickpeas for the aquafaba. Between those three, you can handle almost anything.

And if you're baking for someone with an egg allergy, the aquafaba meringue is worth the effort. The first time you pull a tray of egg-free meringues out of the oven and they actually worked — that's a good moment.
