How to Make Self-Rising Flour — and How to Swap It Both Ways

I bought a five-pound bag of self-rising flour for one biscuit recipe and then spent two years trying to use up the rest. That is how I learned the thing almost nobody writes about: making the stuff takes one line, and the direction people actually get stuck on is the other one — a recipe that calls for self-rising flour and a cupboard that only has plain. Both directions need arithmetic, and one of them needs subtraction that most conversion charts leave out.

⚡ Quick Answer: The Ratio

For every 1 cup (120 g) of all-purpose flour, whisk in:

  • 1½ teaspoons baking powder — 6.9 g
  • ¼ teaspoon salt — 1.4 g

Whisk for 30 seconds, or sift twice. The leaveners add about 8 g per cup of flour — a batch mixed from four cups weighs 513 g, not 480 g.

Going the other way, a recipe that calls for self-rising flour: use plain flour plus the amounts above, then subtract whatever leavening and salt the recipe already lists. Skip that step and you double both.

The bag sat in the cupboard for two years because self-rising flour is a one-recipe ingredient for most people. You buy it for buttermilk biscuits, you make the biscuits, and now you own a five-pound sack of flour that cannot do the job of ordinary flour without an argument.

Then I moved to a place at 4,200 feet and discovered the second half of the problem. Self-rising flour is a fixed formula — the leavening is already in it and you cannot take it out. At altitude, where the whole job is turning the leavening down, a pre-mixed flour is not a shortcut. It is a locked door.

So this is two articles in one. The first half is the ratio and how to mix your own, which takes about a minute. The second half is the part I actually needed: what to do when a recipe asks for self-rising flour and all you have is plain, and what happens when you try it the other way round.

If you only have ten seconds: 1 cup plain flour + 1½ tsp baking powder + ¼ tsp salt. Everything below is about the cases where that one line is not enough — because the leavening in it is either too much, too little, or in the way.

#1 The Ratio, and Where the 1½ Teaspoons Comes From

Self-rising flour is flour with baking powder and salt blended through it. That is the whole product. The only real question is the dose, and there are two camps.

The 1½ teaspoons per cup version is the American Southern convention, and it is the one to use if you are making biscuits, scones, pancakes or any quick bread where you want height. It works out to about 5.8% baking powder by weight — 6.9 g of baking powder in a 120 g cup of flour, using the 4.6 g per teaspoon that the baking powder conversion page carries.

The 1 teaspoon per cup version shows up in older British and Australian books, and in some American cake formulas. That is about 3.8% by weight. You can see why the two exist by looking at what each is for: a cake wants a fine, even crumb and turns coarse and domed when it is over-leavened, while a biscuit wants as much lift as it can get and has no other leavener to fall back on. Same idea, different dose.

Which means the honest answer to "how do I make self-rising flour" depends on what you are about to bake, and 1½ teaspoons is the safe default because it is the dose the recipes are written against. If a biscuit recipe calls for self-rising flour and you hand it 1 teaspoon per cup, you get a flatter biscuit than the recipe was designed for. The reverse — giving a cake 1½ teaspoons per cup — gives you a domed top and a crumb you could read a newspaper through.

The salt is not a rounding error either. A quarter teaspoon per cup is 1.4 g, or about 1.2% of the flour weight. That is deliberately less than bread's usual 1.8–2%, because most self-rising recipes add their own salt on top, and because a biscuit dough is usually seasoned again at the table. It is enough to matter for flavour and browning and not enough to make the flour unusable in a sweet recipe — which is exactly why you cannot simply ignore it when you convert.

One number worth fixing in your head before anything else: the leaveners add about 8 g to every cup of flour you start with, so a batch mixed from four cups weighs 513 g rather than 480 g. That alone makes a gram-based self-rising recipe impossible to convert cup for cup — and the arithmetic cuts the other way too. A cup of the finished blend scoops to roughly 113 g, which is less than the 120 g of plain flour that went into it, because baking powder and salt are far less dense than flour and they push the flour particles apart. The blend ends up lighter than the flour it was made from, even though it contains more material.

The practical consequence: if a recipe gives self-rising flour in cups, mix your own and scoop it loosely the way you scoop plain flour. If it gives grams, weigh the blend and do not assume the number is a flour weight. That distinction comes back in section 4, and it is the largest single source of failed conversions between the two systems.

#2 A Batch Table That Scales Without Rounding Errors

Multiplying 1½ teaspoons by six is where people start guessing. Here is the arithmetic done once, in both volumes and grams, so you can match whichever your recipe uses.

Two things make this table easier to use than it looks. First, every second cup is a tablespoon, because 3 teaspoons make a tablespoon and 1½ × 2 = 3. So 2 cups is exactly 1 Tbsp and 4 cups is exactly 2 Tbsp, with no awkward fractions in between. Second, the salt line runs ¼, ½, ¾, 1 — if you can remember the cup count, you can remember the salt.

If you bake in grams, work from the percentages rather than the table: 5.75% baking powder and 1.19% salt, by flour weight. That is the version that survives a recipe scaled to 1,340 g of flour, which is the kind of number a recipe scaler throws at you and a teaspoon-based table does not.

The 500 g row is worth a note because it is the one metric recipes actually use. 500 g of flour needs 29 g of baking powder, which is a hair over 6 teaspoons, and 6 g of salt, which is a hair over 1 teaspoon. Round both up slightly — 30 g and 6 g — and you will be inside the tolerance of any home recipe.

#3 How to Mix It So It Stays Mixed

Whisk, do not stir. A fork moved through the bowl twice leaves channels of pure flour and clumps of pure baking powder, and a clump of baking powder in one corner of a biscuit dough leaves a bitter spot and an uneven rise. Thirty seconds with a balloon whisk, lifting from the bottom, is enough — you are not trying to aerate it, you are trying to distribute something that makes up 6% of the total.

Sift if the flour is lumpy at all. Self-rising flour is more prone to lumps than plain flour because it has been sitting in a humid cupboard absorbing water into the salt and the leavening as well as the starch. A sieve does two jobs at once: it breaks the lumps and it distributes the powder.

Then store it in a sealed container in a cool, dry cupboard — not the refrigerator. The fridge is the one place in a kitchen where the temperature cycles every time the door opens, and every cycle is a chance for condensation to condense on the inside of the lid. Baking powder's first stage is activated by moisture; a jar that fogs is a jar that is slowly losing its lift.

Write the date on it. A commercial bag has a best-by date printed on it, but a jar you filled yourself does not, and the whole value of a homemade batch is that you know what went into it. A piece of tape on the lid with the date and "1½ / ¼" is genuinely all the record-keeping this needs.

If you make a large batch and use it slowly, shake the jar before each scoop. It is not that the components separate dramatically; it is that the leavening is a small fraction of the total, and any fraction that small drifts out of balance long before the flour itself changes. A five-second shake resets it.

#4 Recipe Calls for Self-Rising Flour, You Only Have Plain

This is the direction that actually gets people. Adding leavening is easy. Remembering to remove the leavening the recipe already lists is the step that gets skipped, and it is the difference between a biscuit and a hockey puck.

The method, in three passes:

  1. Count the flour. Cups or grams, whichever the recipe gives. If it gives both, trust the grams.
  2. Add 1½ tsp baking powder and ¼ tsp salt per cup of plain all-purpose flour (or read the amounts off the table above).
  3. Subtract what the recipe already lists. Any baking powder, any baking soda, any salt that appears as a separate line has to come off the total, not be added on top of it.

Step 3 is where it goes wrong, and it goes wrong in a way that looks fine going into the oven. Too much baking powder does not stop a batter rising — it makes it rise too fast, the bubbles coalesce into large tunnels, the top domes and cracks, and the sodium leaves a soapy aftertaste. On a cake it can rise beautifully and then collapse, because the structure never had time to set around bubbles that size.

Here is the subtraction worked out for the cases that come up. All examples assume 2 cups (240 g) of plain flour replacing 2 cups of self-rising:

What the recipe already listsWhat you addWhy
Nothing — self-rising flour is the only leavener1 Tbsp baking powder + ½ tsp saltThe straightforward case. Supply the full 3 tsp and ½ tsp the flour would have carried.
½ tsp baking powder2½ tsp baking powder + ½ tsp saltSubtract the ½ tsp from the 3 tsp total. The extra leavening was deliberate — do not drop it.
1 tsp baking soda, with buttermilk1 Tbsp baking powder + ½ tsp salt, soda unchangedThe soda is there to react with the acid and to brown the crust. It is not the lift the self-rising flour was providing.
¾ tsp salt, listed separately1 Tbsp baking powder + salt to tasteYour ½ tsp of salt is already most of the way there. Taste the dough before adding the rest.

The soda row is the one people misread. A Southern biscuit recipe that calls for self-rising flour and a pinch of soda and buttermilk is not using the soda to make the biscuits rise — it is using it for browning and a little extra puff against the acid in the buttermilk. Convert the flour and leave the soda alone. Subtract the soda and you get pale, flat-topped biscuits that taste right and look wrong.

The grams case is different, and this is the bit that trips up anyone converting a metric recipe. If a recipe asks for 500 g of self-rising flour, that 500 g is the mix, not the flour. Work back through the ratio: 500 g of mix is about 468 g of flour, 27 g of baking powder and 5.5 g of salt. Use 500 g of plain flour instead and you have added 32 g more flour than the recipe was built on, which is a 7% error — enough to notice in a biscuit dough, not enough to ruin a loaf of quick bread.

One more subtraction that is easy to forget: self-rising flour is not salted enough to season a savoury bake on its own. A quarter teaspoon per cup is about half of what a biscuit or a savoury scone wants. So when you convert to plain flour, add the ¼ tsp per cup and keep whatever salt the recipe lists, unless it lists a lot. Recipes that specify self-rising flour often leave salt out entirely because they assume the flour supplies it — in that case, add your own: ½ tsp per 2 cups is a reasonable starting point for a savoury bake.

#5 Recipe Calls for Plain Flour, You Only Have Self-Rising

This direction is harder to make safe, because you cannot take the leavening out. Everything you bake with self-rising flour will rise, and it will taste of the salt that came with it. So the only question is whether those two things are a problem for the recipe in front of you.

Where a straight swap works. Biscuits, scones, pancakes, waffles, muffins, drop biscuits, and most loaf-style quick breads — the ones where the recipe would have called for 1 to 1½ teaspoons of baking powder per cup anyway. In those cases, swap the flour one-for-one, then omit the recipe's baking powder and its salt, because the flour is already carrying both. If the recipe calls for 1 teaspoon per cup and you swap in a 1½ teaspoon flour, you have added 50% more leavening than intended: usually fine in a pancake, visible in a scone.

Where it does not work.

  • Yeast bread. This is the clearest failure, and it fails for two reasons at once. The flour is usually milled from soft wheat at around 8–9% protein, below ordinary all-purpose and well below bread flour's 12–14%, so you lose gluten strength. And the baking powder adds a second, chemical lift on top of the yeast's, which gives a cakey crumb and a salty crust. There is no adjustment that saves it — use plain or bread flour and add the salt separately.
  • Angel food and chiffon. These get all of their lift from whipped egg white and none from chemistry. Adding baking powder to a foam does not make it taller, it makes it coarser, and the salt destabilises the foam. They also want a bare, ungreased pan, which has nothing to do with the flour — but it is worth knowing that the whole category is designed around not having a chemical leavener in it.
  • Laminated doughs. Croissant, puff, rough puff. The lift here is steam trapped between layers of butter, and a chemical leavener changes the crumb in a way that fights the lamination. It also adds salt to a dough that is already seasoned.
  • Anything that rests. Sourdough, overnight waffles, a batter held for a couple of hours. Baking powder's first stage goes off as soon as it is wet, so a dough that sits loses a chunk of its lift before it ever reaches the oven. Self-rising flour is an ingredient for batters that go straight in.
  • Anything with a lot of its own salt. A recipe at 2% salt plus the flour's 1.2% is 3.2% — inedibly salty, and no amount of extra sugar hides it.

And the one case that belongs in both lists: baking at altitude. That gets its own section below, because it is the single most common reason someone standing in a kitchen at 5,000 feet should not use self-rising flour at all — and it is the case where the swap looks like it should work right up until you cut the cake.

#6 What Is Actually In the Bag

Commercial self-rising flour is not all-purpose flour with a sachet tipped in at the factory, and treating it as such causes conversion errors that no amount of leavening arithmetic fixes.

In the United States, the brands that built the category are milled from soft winter wheat, at roughly 8–9% protein. That is below the 10–12% of ordinary all-purpose and well below the 12–14% of bread flour — the numbers the flour comparison works through in detail. Lower protein means less gluten, a shorter crumb, and a tenderness that is exactly right for a biscuit and exactly wrong for a loaf.

Not every self-rising product is soft-wheat. Some are all-purpose based. The label tells you: look at the protein figure per 100 g and you will see 8, 9, 10 or 11. If it is in the eights, you are holding a biscuit flour. If it is at ten or eleven, it is closer to ordinary all-purpose and will behave more like the flour you are used to.

This matters for conversions in a way that is easy to miss. When you swap self-rising flour into a recipe written for all-purpose, you are changing two variables, not one: the leavening and the protein. When the result is worse than you expected, the protein is often doing more damage than the extra baking powder. A cake made with soft-wheat self-rising flour is not just over-leavened; it is also short, crumbly, and quick to stale, because there is not enough gluten to hold the crumb together as it cools.

The practical version of all this: match the flour to the job before you match the leavening. If a recipe needs strength, no amount of leavening correction will give a soft-wheat flour strength. And if it needs tenderness, a strong flour plus extra chemical lift will not get you there either — it will just be a tough cake that also happens to be coarse.

#7 Why It Makes Biscuits and Never Bread

The biscuit is the reason the product exists, and understanding why it works tells you what will not.

A biscuit needs two things that pull in opposite directions: a lot of lift and almost no gluten development. Bread gets its rise from a gluten network holding gas, which means kneading and time. A biscuit gets its rise from chemistry in a hot oven, which means it can use a weak, low-protein flour and be handled as little as possible. Self-rising flour is the perfect input for that problem — soft wheat for shortness, a high fixed dose of baking powder for lift, and no need to add anything else.

The dose is the point. At 1½ teaspoons per cup, the flour is at about 5.8% baking powder by weight. A cake formula is usually at 3.8–4.8%, and a cake that goes much above that turns coarse, domes in the middle and stales fast. A biscuit can carry the higher number because it is thin, it is baked hot and fast, and it is supposed to have a ragged, open interior instead of an even crumb. Push the same flour into a cake and you get all of the downsides and none of the lift you wanted, because a cake's structure sets around the bubbles and does not want them that big.

Then there is buttermilk, which is where the chemistry gets misunderstood. Double-acting baking powder carries its own acid, so buttermilk is not what makes a self-rising biscuit rise — the recipe would work with plain milk, it would just brown less and taste flatter. The buttermilk is there for flavour, for tenderness (acid weakens gluten a little), and for browning, and a pinch of soda in a self-rising biscuit recipe is there to push the browning further against the acid. That is also why a buttermilk substitute built on vinegar or lemon works fine in a self-rising biscuit: the lift never depended on the acid in the first place.

The last thing the flour does not do is make layers. Those come from the fat — cut in cold, left in visible pieces — and from folding the dough two or three times before the final roll. You can make a self-rising biscuit with no layers by over-working it, and you can make a layered biscuit with an ordinary flour plus your own leavening. The flour supplies lift and tenderness; the technique supplies structure. If your biscuits are flat and tender rather than tall and layered, the flour did its job and the folding did not.

#8 The One Adjustment You Cannot Make at Altitude

At altitude, the entire problem is that there is less air pressure above the batter, so bubbles expand further and faster than they would at sea level. The standard response is to reduce the leavening — how much depends on the elevation, but the direction never changes.

Self-rising flour makes that response impossible. The baking powder is already in the bag, evenly distributed, at a fixed ratio. You cannot take 20% of it out. So above roughly 3,000 feet, self-rising flour stops being a shortcut and becomes the problem: a batter leavened for sea level will rise fast, dome, crack, and then collapse in the thin air, and the higher you go the more reliably it does all four. At 6,000–7,000 feet the same recipe can go from "slightly domed" to "cratered" with no other change.

The fix is to abandon the mix and use plain flour plus leavening you can adjust. Take the recipe's original self-rising flour amount, convert it back with the subtraction method in section 4, then apply the altitude reduction to the baking powder you just calculated — which is exactly the order that the high-altitude calculator runs in. The salt does not need adjusting; it is not affected by pressure.

Same logic applies to every pre-mixed product in the cupboard. Biscuit mix, pancake mix, self-raising flour in a British recipe, a boxed cake mix: all of them lock in a leavening dose that was designed for sea level. At altitude they are not impossible, but they are no longer convenient, because you have to work backwards through the box before you can start.

#9 Shelf Life, and the Ten-Second Test

Flour keeps for a year in a cool cupboard. Baking powder does not, and that is the half of the mix with a clock on it.

Double-acting baking powder works in two stages. The first is triggered by moisture at room temperature — the moment it gets wet it starts producing gas, and it keeps going for a while. The second is triggered by heat in the oven, which is the part that matters for the rise. In a sealed jar of self-rising flour, the first stage is running very slowly the whole time, because a kitchen cupboard is not dry; it is just not wet enough to use the powder up quickly. The lift bleeds away over months rather than minutes.

Which gives you practical windows rather than a single expiry date:

  • Commercial self-rising flour: good until its printed date if unopened. Once opened, treat it as a six-month ingredient and expect the last batches to rise a little less than the first.
  • A batch you mixed yourself: about three months, dated from the day you mixed it. It is only as good as the baking powder that went in, so if the jar was already open, shorten that.

The test takes ten seconds. Put a teaspoon of the mix into a small glass and pour over two or three tablespoons of hot tap water. Fresh mix fizzes at the surface almost immediately and keeps a ring of fine bubbles for half a minute. If nothing happens, the leavening is dead — the flour itself is fine, and will still thicken a sauce or dredge a piece of chicken.

What you should not do is try to rescue it by adding more baking powder. You do not know how much activity is left, so any amount you add is a guess, and a guess that overshoots gives you the soapy, coarse, domed failure from section 4. If the mix is flat, use it as plain flour — add fresh baking powder for the lift and cut back the salt in the recipe by about a quarter teaspoon per cup to account for what is already in there.

#10 Error Detection — Match the Symptom to the Mistake

The good news about self-rising flour problems is that they are unusually diagnosable. Each mistake has a signature.

What you seeWhat caused itWhat to change
Flat, pale, no oven springDead leavening — old mix, or a batter that sat before bakingRun the hot-water test; mix and bake back to back
Domed, cracked top, big open tunnelsToo much baking powder — nearly always double-counted in a conversionSubtract the recipe's own leavening line
Soapy or metallic aftertasteThe same overdose; it is the sodium, not the flourSame fix — this is the taste of excess, not of staleness
Aggressively salty, very dark crustSalt counted twice: flour's quarter teaspoon plus the recipe's full lineCut the listed salt by ¼ tsp per cup of flour used
Tough, chewy, bready crumbWrong protein — self-rising substituted into a bread formula, or strong flour used to fake self-risingUse a soft or all-purpose flour for biscuits, bread flour for bread
Scattered tunnels, uneven riseLeavening not distributed — dumped in instead of whiskedWhisk 30 seconds; sift if the flour is lumpy
Burnished crust, raw middleNot a flour problem — the oven is running hot or the bake is shortCheck the real oven temperature before changing the formula

If two rows describe what you got, fix the upper one first. Over-leavening produces a set of symptoms — dome, tunnels, soapy taste, cracked top — that will drag a second, smaller problem along with it, and the second problem often disappears once the first is corrected. A batch that is both over-leavened and under-baked looks exactly like an over-leavened batch until you cut into it, so probe the centre before deciding.

The one symptom that is never the flour is a gummy middle with a good crust. That is heat and time, not chemistry, and no leavening adjustment will change it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much baking powder is in self-rising flour?

About 1½ teaspoons per cup of flour, plus ¼ teaspoon of salt. On this site's numbers that is 6.9 g of baking powder and 1.4 g of salt per 120 g cup of all-purpose flour, so a batch mixed from one cup of flour weighs about 128 g — while a cup of the resulting blend scoops to roughly 113 g, because the leaveners are less dense than the flour. Some books use 1 teaspoon per cup instead. The higher dose is meant for biscuits and quick breads, which need maximum lift, and the lower one for cakes, which turn coarse when over-leavened.

Can I use self-rising flour instead of all-purpose flour?

Only when the recipe has no leavening of its own, or when you are willing to drop what it does list. Self-rising flour brings baking powder and salt with it and you cannot take them back out. A straight swap usually works in biscuits, pancakes, scones and muffins, because you would have added roughly that much leavening anyway. It fails in yeast bread, angel food and chiffon cakes, laminated doughs, and anything baked above about 3,000 feet.

Does self-rising flour go bad?

The flour keeps, the leavening does not. Baking powder's first stage is triggered by moisture and keeps running slowly even in a sealed jar, so a batch you mixed yourself is best used within about three months and a commercial bag within a few months of opening. Test a teaspoon of the mix in hot water: if it does not fizz at the surface within ten seconds, the lift is gone. The flour still thickens sauces and dredges for frying.

🔧 Before You Swap — Three Questions in Order

Answer these before you touch the flour. The first one that fails, stop.

  1. Does the recipe have leavening of its own? If yes, you can still convert — but you must subtract, not add. If it lists baking soda against an acid (buttermilk, yogurt, lemon), the soda stays and only the baking powder comes off.
  2. Does the bake need gluten strength? Bread, pizza, laminated dough, a chewy cookie: no self-rising flour. Soft-wheat at 8–9% protein will not build the network, and the chemical lift makes it cakey on top of that.
  3. Is the oven above 3,000 feet? Then the fixed leavening dose is the problem and there is no adjustment that fixes it from the outside. Convert to plain flour and reduce the baking powder yourself.

If all three pass: swap one-for-one, drop the recipe's baking powder and its salt, and expect a slightly coarser crumb than the original. That is the flour doing what it is for.

If you take one thing from this article: write the ratio on the lid of the jar — 1½ teaspoons of baking powder and ¼ teaspoon of salt per cup of flour. It is the only number you need in either direction, and the second half of the problem, the subtraction step, only exists because the recipe was already holding some of it.

Second thing: the flour is a formula, not an ingredient. That is why it makes a biscuit better than you can by adding leavening by hand, and why it cannot make a loaf of bread no matter what you do to it. Match the flour to the job before you start adjusting anything else.

And if you are standing at 5,000 feet wondering why a biscuit recipe that worked in Georgia is cratering in Colorado: it is not the flour's fault and it is not yours. The bag was dosed for sea level. Weigh the flour, mix your own, and turn the leavening down.
