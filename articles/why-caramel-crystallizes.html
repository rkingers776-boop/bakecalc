Why Does Caramel Crystallize? Sugar Stages and How to Fix It

A batch of caramel can go from glossy amber to a sandy mess in about ten seconds, and it usually has nothing to do with your recipe. It is sugar doing the one thing sugar is built to do.

Caramel has a reputation for being difficult, and most of that reputation comes down to one failure. Not burning, although that happens too. Crystallizing. You have a pan of clear syrup at exactly the right color, you look away for a moment, and it goes cloudy, then gritty, then white and solid, all before you can work out what you did wrong.

The confusing part is that it fails for the opposite reason people expect. Caramel does not crystallize because it got too hot or because you used the wrong sugar. It crystallizes because sugar wants to be a crystal, and you spent the whole cooking process fighting that, and then something tipped the balance back.

Understanding that fight is what this article is about: what cooked sugar is actually doing as the temperature rises, what the stage names mean and where each one lands in degrees, why a batch goes grainy, and what to do for the batch already sitting in your pan.

⚡ Quick answer: why does caramel crystallize?

Because sucrose molecules, once loose in a supersaturated syrup, snap back into a crystal lattice at the first opportunity. A single undissolved grain — stuck to the pan wall, dropped from a spoon, left over from the last batch — acts as a seed and the whole pan copies it. The fix is three habits: add an interfering ingredient (a spoonful of corn syrup, or a squeeze of lemon juice or a pinch of cream of tartar), keep the pan and utensils spotlessly clean, and stop stirring once it boils — swirl the pan instead. If it has already gone grainy but has not burned, add a tablespoon of water and reheat gently until the crystals dissolve.

1. What heating sugar actually does

Start with what is in the pan: sugar, which is almost pure sucrose, dissolved in water. At room temperature the water is holding far less sugar than it could, so everything stays dissolved and clear. Then you apply heat, and the water starts to leave as steam.

That is the whole process. As the water boils off, the sugar left behind becomes a larger and larger share of what is in the pot. A sugar syrup cannot get hotter than its own boiling point, and that boiling point climbs as the syrup concentrates. Plain water boils at 212°F (100°C) at sea level. A syrup that is 85% sugar boils more than thirty degrees higher. So the temperature of the syrup, read on a thermometer, is not really a measure of heat at all — it is a measure of concentration. When a recipe says "cook to 240°F," it is shorthand for "boil off water until the syrup is about 85% sugar."

This is why the stages all have temperature ranges. Each one corresponds to a particular amount of water still in the syrup, and that amount decides what the cooled candy is like. More water means a softer, chewier result. Almost no water means hard, glassy, brittle. The names — thread, soft ball, hard crack — come from a time before candy thermometers were affordable, when cooks tested a spoonful of syrup in cold water and looked at what it formed. The names stuck even after the thermometers arrived, which is why recipes still read like they were written in a Victorian kitchen. If you are baking with anything that starts as a syrup, including honey, this same water-content logic sits underneath it: honey carries its own water and behaves differently at the same temperature.

2. The seven stages of cooked sugar

Published charts differ from each other by a few degrees, and that is worth knowing up front rather than treating any single table as gospel. What every source agrees on is the order and the rough boundaries. Use the ranges below to know where you are; trust your own thermometer and your eyes for the final call.

Thread (223–235°F / 106–112°C) is barely a candy stage. The syrup is thin enough to drip from a spoon in a fine string, and it is used for things that stay liquid: dessert syrups, glazes, the sugar syrup brushed over a cake layer. Anything you are not trying to set at room temperature lives here.

Soft ball (234–240°F / 112–115°C) is where fudge and fondant land. Drop a little into cold water and it forms a ball that holds together but flattens when you pick it up. The cooled result is soft and holds a shape only loosely, which is exactly what you want from a piece of fudge.

Firm ball (242–248°F / 117–120°C) is the caramel stage in the candy sense, and also the stage used for Italian meringue, where hot syrup is poured into whipped egg whites. Letting the syrup get this hot is what cooks the whites as it goes in and gives that meringue its unusual stability. If you have ever made stiff-peaked egg whites and wondered why the Italian version is so much more forgiving, this temperature is the reason.

Hard ball (250–266°F / 121–130°C) gives nougat, marshmallow, and divinity their structure. The ball is rigid but still pliable when you squeeze it in cold water.

Soft crack (270–290°F / 132–143°C) is where the syrup starts forming threads that are firm but still bend instead of snapping. Saltwater taffy, butterscotch, and most toffee sit here, and the flavor begins to pick up a caramel note.

Hard crack (295–310°F / 146–155°C) is the brittle stage. Threads formed in cold water are stiff and break cleanly, which is what makes a piece of peanut brittle or a lollipop crack instead of bend.

Caramel (320–360°F / 160–182°C) is a different process entirely, and it is not about water any more. The syrup is effectively sugar now, the water is gone, and what happens next is the sugar molecules themselves breaking apart. That is caramelization, and it is what this article is really about. Anything past roughly 375°F (190°C) is burning: the sugar blackens, goes bitter, and there is no fixing it.

3. Why caramel turns grainy

Here is the mechanism, and once you have it, every rule about caramel suddenly makes sense.

Sucrose, the molecule in ordinary granulated sugar, is unusually good at packing. In a solid lump or a bag of crystals, the molecules sit in a neat repeating pattern, a crystal lattice, held together because the identical molecules fit against each other perfectly. Dissolved in water, they are separated, each one surrounded by water molecules holding it apart, and in that state the sugar cannot crystallize no matter what you do.

As the syrup boils, the water leaves, and the dissolved sugar becomes supersaturated: there is more sugar in the water than it can comfortably hold. This is the dangerous middle of the cook, roughly from when the sugar has fully dissolved up to when caramelization begins. Any loose sucrose crystal that appears during that window can act as a template. A single crystal is enough. The molecules in the syrup latch onto its surface, the lattice grows, and the new surface attracts more, in a chain reaction that can turn the whole pan from clear to cloudy to solid in under a minute.

Where do the loose crystals come from? Usually from the pan itself. As the syrup boils, droplets splash up the sides above the liquid line, and the water in those droplets evaporates fast, leaving behind pure dried sugar. A spoon dragged through the syrup can knock those crystals off the wall and back into the pot, or can splash fresh droplets up there to begin with. A speck of dried sugar left on a spoon or a whisk from an earlier step does the same. So does an unclean pan, or a grain of sugar still sitting undissolved at the very start.

The counterintuitive part is that crystallizing is easier early and harder late. Once the sugar passes into real caramelization, above roughly 320°F, the sucrose has started breaking down into hundreds of different compounds, and those compounds are all the wrong shape. They physically get in the way of any new lattice trying to form. This is why a deeply caramelized sugar almost never crystallizes, and a lightly cooked syrup is fragile: the more you have cooked it, the more its own breakdown products are protecting it.

⚠️ A note on the temperature you are working at

Sugar syrup at the ball and crack stages is hotter than boiling water, and it is thicker, which means it sticks to skin instead of running off. A splash of 300°F syrup causes a far worse burn than the same splash of boiling water. Use a pan that is deeper than you think you need, keep the handle turned away, never lean over the pot, and keep bare skin, including faces, out of the path of any drop that spits up. This is a good reason to work alone rather than with children in the room, and to keep a bowl of cold water within reach.

4. The three things that keep it smooth

Everything that prevents grainy caramel is one of three moves, and each one attacks a different part of the mechanism above. If you do all three, a crystallized batch becomes rare rather than routine.

Add an interfering ingredient. If the problem is that identical sucrose molecules stack neatly, the fix is to make sure some molecules of a different shape are present. There are two ways to do that, and you only need one. The first is to add a different sugar directly: a tablespoon or two of corn syrup or glucose syrup per cup of sugar. Corn syrup is mostly glucose, a smaller molecule with a different shape, and it wedges between the sucrose molecules and blocks the neat lattice, the way a handful of round stones keeps rectangular bricks from stacking tightly. The second is to make your own interfering sugars by adding a little acid, such as a squeeze of lemon juice or about a quarter teaspoon of cream of tartar per cup of sugar. Heat plus acid splits some of the sucrose back into its two halves, glucose and fructose. That mixture is called invert sugar, and it does not crystallize the way sucrose does. Both routes work; the corn syrup route is the more predictable of the two, because you are adding a measured amount of something you can control rather than waiting for a reaction to happen on its own.

Keep the pan and tools clean. Because it takes only one crystal to seed a batch, anything that could carry a stray grain is a risk. Wash the saucepan and dry it rather than wiping it out, since a towel can leave lint. Use a clean spoon or whisk that has not touched sugar before. Do not reuse a spatula that was sitting in a bag of sugar. This sounds fussy, and it is the move most people skip, and it is the single most common real cause of a ruined batch.

Stop stirring once it boils. Stirring is useful at the start, when you are helping the sugar dissolve and the mixture is still cool. Once the syrup boils, a spoon does two harmful things: it splashes syrup up the walls where it dries into seed crystals, and it can knock those same crystals back down into the pot. If you need to move the syrup or even out a hot spot, pick the pan up by the handle and swirl it. This is the habit that feels the most unnatural, and it is the one that saves the most batches.

Two more details worth knowing. Where the syrup does splash up the walls anyway, a pastry brush dipped in cold water and run around the inside of the pan dissolves those crystals before they cause trouble, which is an old technique and a good one to have ready. And a pan with a heavy bottom heats more evenly than a thin one, so there are fewer hot spots where sugar scorches while its neighbours are still melting; if you make caramel more than once or twice a year, the heavy pan is worth it. 5. Wet caramel versus dry caramel

There are two ways to get sugar from solid to caramelized, and the choice changes how much attention the pan needs.

Wet caramel starts with water in the pan along with the sugar. The sugar dissolves into the water and heats evenly as it comes up to the boil, so the mixture caramelizes at a fairly uniform rate and is hard to scorch. It takes longer, because all that water has to boil off before the sugar can get hot enough to brown, and the extra minute or two is exactly the window in which a stray crystal can seed the batch, which is why wet caramel recipes so often include corn syrup or an acid. That pairing is not a coincidence: the slower method is the one that needs the most protection from crystallizing, and the protection is easy to add up front.

Dry caramel is sugar alone, melted straight in the pan. It gets to color faster and can taste deeper, because every grain browns rather than sitting in water first. The catch is that not all the grains melt at the same moment, so you can have liquid caramel around the edges while the middle is still solid sugar, and if you disturb it while it is half-melted it seizes into lumps. It also has no interfering ingredients at all, so once it is fluid, it is more prone to going grainy. Dry caramel is faster and more intense, and it does not tolerate a wanderer.

If you are new to this, use the wet method. It is slower and much harder to ruin, and the flavor difference, while real in a side-by-side, is not large enough to justify the extra risk until you have the technique down.

6. Testing the stage without a thermometer

You can place the syrup in the stage chart with nothing but a glass of water and your fingers, and there are good reasons to learn it even if you own a thermometer.

Put a glass of very cold water, ideally with ice, next to the stove. When you want to check, take the pan off the heat first, so the syrup does not keep cooking while you test. Drop about half a teaspoon of syrup into the water and let it sit for a few seconds. Then reach in and pick it up with your fingers and press it. Thread stage breaks into a thin string. Soft ball forms a ball that flattens when you squeeze it. Firm ball holds its shape unless you press hard. Hard ball is rigid but still yields to pressure. Soft crack forms threads that bend. Hard crack forms threads that snap. This is exactly how cooks placed sugar before affordable thermometers existed, and the stage names are simply descriptions of what the cooled syrup does.

Two things make this more reliable than it sounds. First, use fresh cold water for every test, since water that already has syrup in it will throw off the result. Second, and more interesting, the cold water test is self-correcting for altitude in a way a thermometer is not. Water boils at a lower temperature the higher you are, so a candy thermometer that reads 240°F at sea level is reading the equivalent of a hotter syrup in Denver than the same number would mean at the coast. The cold water test does not care, because it measures the physical property you actually want, not how hot the syrup had to get to reach it. 7. When it has already crystallized

If the syrup is still in the pan, has gone grainy, and has not darkened past a normal caramel color, it is almost certainly recoverable. Crystallization is reversible as long as the sugar has not burned, because you are just putting back the water you boiled off.

Add a tablespoon or so of water to the pan, set it over low heat, and stir gently and continuously. The crystals dissolve back into solution, and the syrup turns clear again. This is the one time stirring while hot is allowed, because you are deliberately re-dissolving. Once the mixture looks smooth, stop stirring, put a lid on the pan for a minute or two, and let the steam condense and wash down the sides — the same trick as brushing the walls, but hands-off. Then take the lid off and carry on. The critical thing is to get every last grain dissolved before the temperature starts climbing again, because one surviving crystal will simply seed a second batch. If the caramel has already gone very dark and smells sharp or acrid, do not try to save it: the bitterness is a flavor problem, and adding water will only leave you with a thinner bitter caramel.

If the caramel has already set into candy and is slightly grainy, there is a second rescue. Spread it on a baking tray and warm it in a low oven, around 250°F, for a few minutes. The heat melts the smaller crystals and smooths the texture, though a batch that set very grainy will never come back as glossy as one that never went wrong. It is a decent last resort for something that is otherwise fine to eat.

8. Adding cream or butter, the dramatic part

Almost every caramel sauce recipe ends the same way: the sugar has reached its color, and now you add cream or butter. This is the step where people get splattered, and it is worth knowing why before you do it.

Caramelized sugar at 350°F is well above the boiling point of the cream about to go into it. The moment the two meet, the water in the cream flashes to steam, the mixture foams violently up the sides of the pan, and the temperature plunges as the cold liquid absorbs heat. That plunge is the point: it stops the caramelization before the sugar tips into burnt, which is why a recipe tells you to have the cream ready and to add it promptly rather than admiring the color.

Two things make it safer and smoother. Warm the cream first, which reduces the temperature shock and the violence of the boil. And pour it in a slow, steady stream while stirring, rather than dumping it in at once, so the heat is drawn out gradually instead of in one burst. Expect a lot of steam and a serious amount of bubbling; that is normal, and it settles within seconds of stirring. If the mixture seizes into lumps anyway, return the pan to low heat and stir gently until it melts back together, which it usually will as long as nothing has burned. From there, the dairy is where the flavor gets deep: once cream and butter are in, the milk proteins start reacting with the sugars too, so what started as caramelized sugar ends up tasting far more complex than the sugar it came from. 9. Why altitude and humidity move the numbers

The temperatures in every sugar chart assume you are cooking at sea level on a dry day. Change either of those and the numbers shift, and knowing that saves you from chasing a reading that was never going to appear.

Altitude lowers the boiling point of everything, sugar syrup included. Water boils at 212°F at sea level and about 203°F at 5,000 feet, and a sugar syrup at 5,000 feet reaches every stage at a reading roughly nine degrees lower than the same stage would read at the coast. So a chart's 240°F soft ball corresponds to about 231°F in Denver. If you cook by the chart without adjusting, you will overshoot every stage. This is the same physics that makes high-altitude baking require so many adjustments, and it is the reason a candy thermometer has a small screw or a calibration step: the instrument is accurate, but the target it is being compared against was set at a different pressure. The temperature conversion tool helps you move between scales, though altitude correction is something you apply on top.

Humidity shifts things in the other direction. On a humid day, sugar is already pulling moisture from the air, and that moisture fights the concentration the syrup is trying to reach, so the candy needs to be cooked a little hotter and a little longer to get to the same place. Published guidance puts the difference at only a couple of degrees, but those couple of degrees are the difference between a firm caramel and a soft one, and on a rainy day they are the reason a batch that works every other week comes out underdone. If you want a repeatable result, calibrate your thermometer and note the weather, because a syrup is the one thing in the kitchen that cares about the air.

10. Reading the color, but trusting the number

Every caramel recipe leans on color, and telling you to "cook until deep amber" is not wrong, but it is a softer instruction than it looks.

Color is a real signal, because the same caramelization reactions that build flavor also build the brown pigments, so a darker syrup really is a further-along syrup. The problem is that the color you see depends on more than the temperature. A deep pan makes a small amount of syrup look darker than it is. A dark nonstick finish and a bright stainless one reflect differently. The light in your kitchen changes between afternoon and evening. Two cooks following the same recipe in the same kitchen can genuinely disagree about whether the caramel is amber or already too dark, and only one of them is right.

The fix is to use color as a rough check and the thermometer as the decision. Watch the syrup go from clear to straw to amber to reddish-brown so you learn what the progression looks like, but make the call to pull it off the heat by temperature, and use color to confirm. And keep one distinction straight, because it trips people up: caramelization is a pure-sugar reaction, but the moment you add cream or butter you have also started the Maillard reaction, in which the dairy's proteins and sugars brown together. Both are happening in a finished caramel sauce, and separating them in your head is what makes it clear why a sauce tastes richer than the sugar it was built from.

11. The short version

Grainy caramel is not a mystery and not a sign that you are bad at this. It is one clear physical event: sucrose in a supersaturated syrup finding a template and snapping back into crystal. Everything you do to prevent it is one of three things, and you can hold all three in your head at once.

Add something of a different shape, so the crystals cannot stack neatly: a spoonful of corn syrup, or a little lemon juice or cream of tartar. Keep the pan and the tools clean, so no stray grain starts the chain. And once the syrup boils, stop stirring and swirl the pan, so you do not splash seed crystals up the walls. If it does go grainy and it has not burned, add water and heat it back smooth. If it has burned, throw it out without regret and start again.

One piece of equipment pays for itself faster than anything else here: an accurate thermometer. Sugar is one of the few things in baking where a single reading tells you everything about what you are making, and once you trust that reading over the color of the syrup in the pan, caramel stops being a gamble. Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my caramel crystallize?

Almost always because a few sucrose molecules found each other and stacked back into their crystal shape, and once that started it did not stop. Sucrose is a very orderly molecule with a strong pull toward its own kind. As long as it is fully dissolved in water it cannot crystallize, but as the syrup boils the water leaves and the sugar becomes supersaturated. At that point any loose crystal, a grain stuck to the side of the pan, a speck of dried sugar on a spoon, even a few crystals that formed where the syrup splashed up the wall and dried, can act as a seed. The whole batch then copies that seed and turns cloudy and gritty within seconds. Stirring during boiling is the other common trigger, because it splashes syrup onto the pan walls where it dries into exactly those seed crystals. It is almost never the recipe, and almost never the thermometer.

Can you fix caramel that has crystallized?

If the syrup is still in the pan and has not burned, yes. Add about a tablespoon of water, set the pan over low heat, and stir gently and continuously until the grains disappear back into the liquid. Once it looks smooth again, stop stirring, put a lid on the pan for a minute or two, and let the steam condense and run down the walls to dissolve any crystals clinging there. Then carry on with the recipe. The one thing that matters is dissolving every last grain before the temperature climbs again, because a single survivor will seed a second crystallization. If the caramel has already gone dark and smells acrid, it is past saving, since the bitterness is a flavor problem and no amount of water undoes it. A slightly grainy caramel candy that has already set can often be saved by spreading it on a tray and warming it in a low oven, around 250°F, which melts the smaller crystals.

What is the difference between wet and dry caramel?

Wet caramel starts with sugar and water together in the pan. The water helps the sugar dissolve and heat evenly, so it is slower but far more forgiving, and it is what most recipes and most guides recommend for anyone who has not made caramel many times. Dry caramel is sugar alone, melted directly in the pan. It is faster and can reach a deeper, more complex flavor, but the sugar melts unevenly, some of it browns before the rest has liquefied, and there are no interfering ingredients to hold off crystallization, so it scorches and seizes far more easily. Both end up in the same place. The practical difference is how much attention the pan needs while you get there. The one habit that is the same either way: once the sugar has dissolved and the syrup boils, stop stirring and swirl the pan instead.

What temperature do I cook caramel sauce to?

For a pourable caramel sauce, somewhere around 350°F (175°C), pulled off the heat just before you add the cream or butter. Caramelization itself starts near 320 to 340°F (160 to 170°C), and below that you are only boiling a sugar syrup, not making caramel. As the temperature climbs the flavor changes: at the low end the sugar is still very sweet and mild, in the middle it turns the deep amber of a good sauce, and past roughly 375°F (190°C) bitter compounds accumulate and the caramel starts to taste acrid. A common working range for sauce is 350 to 375°F. If you want hard candy or brittle instead of sauce, do not stop at sauce temperatures at all, keep going to the hard crack stage at 295 to 310°F before any liquid goes in, and remember that sugar syrup temperatures run about 30 to 40 degrees above plain water for the same stage.

Is caramelization the same as the Maillard reaction?

No, they are two different browning reactions that are often confused because they can happen in the same pan. Caramelization is what pure sugar does when it is heated past roughly 320°F: the sucrose molecules break apart and recombine into hundreds of new compounds that give caramel its color and its bittersweet, buttery flavor. It needs nothing but sugar. The Maillard reaction needs both a sugar and a protein or an amino acid, and it runs at lower temperatures, often from around 285°F. It is what browns bread crust, toast, and seared meat. When you add cream or butter to caramelized sugar, both reactions are happening at once: the sugar is caramelizing, and the milk proteins are Maillard-reacting with the sugars in the dairy. That double reaction is a large part of why a caramel sauce tastes more complex than the plain caramelized sugar it started as.

📋 The No-Crystal Checklist

  • Use a heavy-bottomed pan so the sugar heats evenly and cannot scorch in a hot spot
  • Start with a spotlessly clean, dry pan and dry utensils — one stray grain can ruin the batch
  • For wet caramel, add sugar and water together and stir gently until every grain dissolves
  • Add an interfering ingredient: 1–2 tablespoons of corn syrup per cup of sugar, or ¼ teaspoon of cream of tartar or a squeeze of lemon juice
  • The moment the syrup boils, stop stirring and swirl the pan by the handle instead
  • Keep a pastry brush and cold water ready to wash down any crystals that creep up the walls
  • Use an accurate thermometer, and place the syrup by temperature first and color second
  • Pull sauce off the heat around 350°F (175°C); take candy to its stage before adding any liquid
  • Warm the cream or butter before it goes in, and add it in a slow stream while stirring
  • At altitude, expect every stage to read about nine degrees lower per 5,000 feet
  • If the syrup goes grainy but not burned, add water and reheat gently until the crystals dissolve
  • If it has gone dark and smells acrid, throw it out and start again — that one cannot be saved
  • Keep the pan and your bare skin apart; syrup at the ball and crack stages burns worse than boiling water

Crystallized caramel is one of the few baking failures that is fully explained by a single physical fact, and once you see it that way it stops feeling like bad luck. Sugar wants to be a crystal; your job is to keep it from getting the chance. Clean pan, an interfering ingredient, no stirring, and the pot calm enough not to splash — do those and the syrup stays clear, and when it does not, you will know exactly which one you missed.
