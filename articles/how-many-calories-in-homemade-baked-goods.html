How Many Calories Are in Your Homemade Baked Goods?

Someone at the farmers market asked me how many calories were in my banana bread and I said "a normal amount." Wrong answer. Here are the real numbers — and the method that lets you calculate any recipe in about two minutes.

The first time a customer asked me how many calories were in a muffin, I panicked and guessed "around 200." It was 375. I wasn't trying to lie — I genuinely had no idea, because I'd never once added up what went into the bowl. Most home bakers are in the same boat. We bake by feel, by family recipe, by "a stick of butter and two cups of sugar," and the calorie count stays invisible until someone asks.

So this is the article I wish someone had handed me. Real calorie numbers for the bakes you actually make, the two-minute method for calculating your own recipe, and the two ingredients that are quietly doing almost all the damage. No diet lecture — just the math, so you can answer that question with a real number instead of a shrug.

Butter and sugar are almost the whole answer

Here's the number that reframes everything. A gram of fat is 9 calories. A gram of protein or carbs is 4. That single fact means one ingredient dominates every bake you've ever made:

  • Butter — 717 calories per 100g. A single stick (113g) is about 810 calories. That's a third of an entire loaf's calories before you've added anything else.
  • Oil — 884 calories per 100g. The most calorie-dense thing in your kitchen, full stop.
  • Sugar — 387 calories per 100g. A cup (200g) is about 774 calories.
  • Flour — about 364 calories per 100g. Not innocent either, but lighter than you'd think next to the fat.

Notice what this means: the "healthy" banana or the handful of blueberries you put in a muffin are rounding errors. A whole banana is about 90 calories. The half-cup of oil in the same muffin is 900. If you're trying to make a bake lighter, you don't start by cutting the fruit — you start by looking at how much fat and sugar went in. Everything else is noise.

This is also why "healthy-looking" baked goods fool people. An oat-and-honey muffin sounds virtuous, but oats are dense and honey is still sugar. The calorie calculator is useful for exactly this — it lets you look up a food's real number instead of trusting the vibe. The pattern is the same every time: the more butter, oil, sugar, or chocolate in the mix, the higher the number. A plain lean bread sits around 260 calories per 100g; a chocolate chip cookie is nearly double that, and it's not because of the flour. The two-minute method: calculate any recipe

You don't need an app or a lab. You need a kitchen scale and the 4-4-9 rule: protein and carbs are 4 calories per gram, fat is 9. Here's the whole thing, worked on a real banana bread so you can see it's not abstract.

A standard 9×5 banana bread — the one I actually sell — goes like this:

IngredientWeightCalories
All-purpose flour240g~874
Butter113g (1 stick)~810
Sugar200g (1 cup)~774
Eggs2 large (100g)~286
Bananas3 medium (~350g)~312
Total~1,000g batter~3,056

After baking, the loaf weighs about 800g (water cooks off — that's the difference). Cut it into 10 slices and each slice is about 305 calories. Cut 8 thick slices and it's about 380. Same loaf, different answer, which is why "how many calories in banana bread" is always a range, never a fixed number.

Two things to notice. First, the three bananas add 312 calories to the whole loaf — about 31 per slice — while the butter alone adds 810. The fruit is not your problem. Second, you did the math with a scale and multiplication, not a crystal ball. Convert any cup-based recipe to grams first (the cups-to-grams guide has the numbers, or use flour and butter converters), then run the same table.

The slice is the real variable

Here's a detail nobody warns you about, because it makes "how many calories is this" sound like a trick question. That 3,056-calorie loaf is 305 calories per slice only if you cut it into ten. Cut eight thicker slices and each one is 380. Cut twelve thin ones and each drops to 255. The bread didn't change — the knife did. So when a recipe site tells you "150 calories per slice of banana bread" without saying how many slices their loaf makes, the number is meaningless.

This is why bakery bread comes sliced thin, and why commercial "100-calorie" portions exist at all. Portioning is the calorie strategy. If you're selling or counting, decide your serving count first, weigh the finished bake, and divide honestly. It's the same logic the FDA uses when it sets serving sizes by weight rather than by "one slice" — the nutrition label guide explains that RACC rule in detail.

Homemade isn't automatically lighter

I used to assume that baking at home meant eating healthier. It doesn't, and the numbers make that obvious. A bakery blueberry muffin is around 450-500 calories, but the muffin I bake at home from a standard recipe is 375 calories per 100g — and if my muffin weighs 130g, that's still nearly 490 calories. The calories come from butter, sugar, and flour, and I use the same three ingredients the bakery does. The difference isn't the calorie count; it's that at home I control the recipe, so I can make it lighter if I choose to.

That's the honest framing. Homemade buys you control — less sugar, better fats, no preservatives, no guessing what's in it — but the calorie total is whatever your recipe says it is. If you want the number down, you have to change the recipe, not just the location. Three "low-calorie" moves that don't actually work

This is where the good intentions go wrong. People swap ingredients thinking they're cutting calories, when the swap barely moves the number — or makes it worse.

1. Honey instead of sugar

Honey is 304 calories per 100g versus sugar's 387, so per gram it is lighter. But nobody measures honey by the gram — they measure by the spoon. A tablespoon of honey weighs 21 grams (about 64 calories). A tablespoon of sugar weighs 13 grams (about 50 calories). Measured the way people actually bake, honey is more calorie-dense, not less. It's a fine swap for flavor, and there's a full guide to baking with honey — just don't do it for the calorie savings, because there aren't any.

2. Oil instead of butter

Butter is 717 calories per 100g. Oil is 884. Swapping oil for butter to "lighten" a cake makes it heavier, because oil is pure fat with no water. Butter is roughly 80% fat and 16% water; oil is 100% fat. If the goal is fewer calories, this swap moves the needle the wrong way.

3. Protein powder and nuts as "healthy" add-ins

A scoop of whey is about 120 calories. A half-cup of almonds is over 400. These are nutritious, but "healthy" and "low-calorie" are not the same thing — nuts are some of the most calorie-dense foods you can bake with. Add them for nutrition and flavor, just don't add them expecting the total to drop.

If you genuinely want to cut calories, cut fat and sugar at the recipe level, not by swapping labels. Reduce the butter, reduce the sugar, and accept that the texture will change. That's the honest trade.

From calorie count to a label that sells

If you sell your bakes, the calorie number isn't just trivia — it's the first question a health-conscious customer asks, and it's the raw material for a nutrition label. 📋 The Two-Minute Calorie Checklist

  • Weigh every ingredient in grams — convert cups first with the cups-to-grams guide
  • Multiply each ingredient by its calories per 100g (fat = 9 kcal/g, protein & carbs = 4)
  • Add it all up for the batch total
  • Weigh the finished bake after it cools — water loss changes per-serving math
  • Divide by the number of servings you actually cut
  • Reality-check against the calorie calculator for single foods
  • For anything you sell, carry the number into a nutrition label

Calories aren't a moral score, and this isn't a diet article. It's just the arithmetic of what goes in the bowl. Know it, and you can bake a 500-calorie muffin on purpose or a 200-calorie one — but you'll never again have to guess when someone asks.
