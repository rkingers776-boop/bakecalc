100 g of cocoa powder in cups — the short answer

One hundred grams of cocoa powder is 1 cup, spooned into the measure and levelled off, which is also 16 level tablespoons. So a recipe asking for a cup of cocoa is asking for 100 g, and the two descriptions mean the same thing — as long as nobody scooped.

The Cleanest Cocoa Conversion, and the Least Trustworthy

Everything about 100 g is tidy. It is a round weight, it is exactly one cup, and it divides neatly into halves, quarters, and thirds. The trouble is that it is tidy only at one specific density, and cocoa has no fixed density.

  • Sifted cocoa — 1.18 cups per 100 g, since a sifted cup weighs about 85 g
  • Spooned and levelled — 1.00 cup per 100 g
  • Scooped and levelled — 0.83 cups per 100 g

Read that last line again. A scooping baker measuring what they believe to be a cup of cocoa has actually put 120 g in the bowl. On a 100 g portion, that is a fifth more cocoa, a fifth more starch, and a batter that has quietly absorbed more liquid than the recipe anticipated.

The rounded numbers on this page are therefore an instruction about technique as much as a conversion. One cup equals 100 g if, and only if, you spoon.

Natural Cocoa and Dutch-Process Cocoa

These are not two brands of the same thing. They are two different products made from the same bean.

  • Natural cocoa is ground roasted cocoa solids, nothing added. It is acidic — pH around 5 to 6 — and its flavour is sharp, fruity, and a little sour at the edges. It is paler than most people expect, closer to a dusty reddish brown than to black.
  • Dutch-process cocoa has been washed in an alkaline solution, which neutralises the acid, darkens the colour considerably, and smooths the flavour into something rounder and less astringent.

Both weigh the same, so 100 g of either is a cup. The difference shows up in the oven, and it shows up specifically in the leavening.

The Leavening Swap, Explained

Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate. On its own it does nothing much — it needs an acid to react with, and when it finds one it produces carbon dioxide immediately and completely.

Baking powder is bicarbonate that has already been packaged with a dry acid, so it is self-contained and can be formulated to release gas twice: once when it gets wet and again when it gets hot.

Now the cocoa. A recipe that pairs natural cocoa with baking soda is a balanced system. The cocoa supplies the acid, the soda supplies the base, and the reaction both lifts the cake and neutralises the acidity, which is why the resulting crumb is tender rather than sour.

Swap in Dutch-process cocoa and the acid disappears. The soda has nothing to react with, so the cake rises less, and the unreacted bicarbonate leaves a soapy, metallic edge that people notice without being able to name.

The published fix is straightforward: replace the baking soda with about twice its volume of baking powder. One teaspoon of soda becomes roughly two teaspoons of baking powder once the cocoa is Dutch-process.

What 100 g of Cocoa Brings to a Batter

  • Starch — around 25 g worth, which is close to what four tablespoons of flour contribute. It thickens and firms.
  • Fat — 10 to 12 g of cocoa butter in a typical unsweetened powder, which shortens gluten and tenderises.
  • Fibre — substantial, and it holds water, which is part of why a cocoa cake stays moist.
  • Colour and flavour — 100 g is a deep, fully chocolate result, no longer a tint.

Notice that a cup of cocoa is doing structural work as well as flavouring. That is why breads and enriched doughs generally stop well short of 100 g, and why cakes that go this far usually reduce their flour to compensate.

Cocoa Fat Content and the European Variant

Unsweetened cocoa sold in the United States is typically 10 to 12% cocoa butter. Cocoa sold in parts of Europe is often higher in fat — sometimes markedly so — which makes it denser, richer, and slightly less absorbent per gram.

This matters when following a European recipe by volume. If a continental cocoa runs heavy in the cup, a cup-measured portion will weigh more than 100 g, and the cake will be darker and drier than the recipe intends. The fix is unglamorous and effective: weigh it, and if the result is consistently above 100 g per cup, treat the cocoa as a denser product and adjust.

Measuring 100 g Without a Scale

  • One cup measure, spooned full — drop cocoa into the cup with a spoon and sweep level. This is exactly 100 g.
  • Sixteen level tablespoons — the same thing counted the long way, and forgiving of a half-tablespoon of drift either side.
  • Do not scoop — a scooped cup is 120 g, which is a metric cup of cocoa and not a cup of the recipe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 100g of cocoa powder?

A1: One cup, when the cocoa is spooned into the measure and levelled. Natural and Dutch-process cocoa are both taken at 100 grams per spooned cup.

Q2: Is a cup of cocoa powder really 100g?

A2: By the spooned and levelled measure, yes. Sifting the same cocoa lowers it to about 85 grams per cup, and scooping raises it to about 120, so the figure depends on technique rather than on the cocoa.

Q3: Can I use Dutch-process cocoa instead of natural cocoa?

A3: You can, but not without adjusting the leavening. A recipe built on natural cocoa and baking soda relies on the cocoa's acid to make the soda work, so Dutch cocoa leaves that soda without a partner. Replace it with roughly twice as much baking powder.
