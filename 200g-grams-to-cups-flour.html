200 g Flour to Cups Converter

Convert 200 grams of flour to cups — one and two thirds, and the volume surprise that goes with it.

Cup Equivalent:

Roughly 1 2/3 cups (1.67 cups)

*Calculated using standard US customary baking cups via spoon-and-level method.

200 g of flour in cups — the short answer

Two hundred grams of all-purpose flour is 1.67 cups — one and two thirds, or one cup plus a half cup plus a sixth. In a metric jug the same flour occupies about 394 mL.

That second number is the one that catches people out. Two hundred grams of almost any liquid would be 200 mL, and the two figures look interchangeable on a page. They are not: flour is a solid that sits loosely, so its volume reads close to double its weight.

Why Flour Takes Up Twice Its Weight in Space

A gram of flour is a gram of flour, but how much room it fills depends on how much air is caught between the particles. Spooned loosely into a cup, flour is roughly two thirds air by volume. The solid material is dense — milled wheat is heavier than water — but there is not much of it in any given scoop.

The comparison with a liquid makes the point:

  • One cup of water — 236.59 mL, weighing 237 g
  • One cup of all-purpose flour — 236.59 mL, weighing 120 g

Identical volume, half the weight. Turn it around and the same relationship says that 200 g of flour needs about 394 mL of space, while 200 g of water needs 200 mL. If you are sizing a bowl, a tin, or a food processor by weight alone, flour will always overflow your estimate.

Where 200 g of Flour Shows Up

  • Small loaves — a 200 g flour dough bakes into a loaf of roughly 320 g, which is a two or three day loaf for one person.
  • Round cakes — one eight-inch layer is often 200 to 250 g of flour.
  • Pizza — 200 g of flour makes a single large thin-crust pizza or two smaller ones.
  • Half batches — a 400 g recipe halved lands here, which is the most common reason for the conversion.

Building 1 2/3 Cups Without a Scale

One and two thirds is not a fraction the standard set reaches in one scoop, but it is buildable. One cup, then a half cup, then a third of a cup of the remaining space — except that a third of a cup added to one and a half cups overshoots, landing at 1.83 cups. The accurate assembly is one cup plus a half cup plus two tablespoons and two teaspoons.

Which is a good argument for the scale. Two hundred grams is one of those quantities where the cup arithmetic is fiddly and the weighing is instant, and the difference in effort is worth more than the difference in precision.

The Same 200 g in Other Flours

Because each flour has its own density, 200 g fills a different volume depending on what is in the bag:

  • All-purpose flour — 1.67 cups, about 394 mL
  • Bread flour — 1.54 cups, about 364 mL
  • Cake flour — 1.82 cups, about 430 mL
  • Whole wheat flour — 1.67 cups, about 394 mL
  • Almond flour — 2.08 cups, about 493 mL

Almond flour at 200 g needs almost half a litre of space. Anyone who has tried to fold 200 g of almond flour into a batter sized for 200 g of wheat flour has met this problem directly: the bowl is not big enough, and the batter is much thicker than expected because the dry volume is larger and the flour absorbs differently.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many cups is 200 g of flour?

A1: One and two thirds cups of all-purpose flour, using the 120 grams per cup standard for spooned and levelled flour. Build it as one cup plus a half cup plus a sixth, or measure 200 grams and save yourself the arithmetic.

Q2: Is 200 g of flour the same as 200 mL?

A2: No, and the gap is large. Two hundred grams of spooned flour occupies about 394 mL of space, close to double. Flour is about two thirds air when it is loose, so its volume always reads far higher than its weight.

Q3: Why does 200 g of flour look like so much?

A3: Because it is. Flour at 120 grams per cup is much lighter than water at 237 grams per cup. Any time you compare a flour weight to a liquid volume, the flour looks twice as big for the same number.
