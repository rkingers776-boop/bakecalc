How Many Grams in 1 Cup of Bread Flour?

About 130 grams, spooned and leveled — the figure this converter uses. Bread flour is scooped and levelled exactly like any other flour: fluff it, spoon it in, sweep off the excess with the back of a knife.

Worth knowing before you weigh: published charts for bread flour run from 127 to 130 grams per cup, and one widely used reference puts it at 120, the same as all-purpose. Some of that gap is real and some of it is branding. Hard wheat milled for bread does settle more tightly in the cup than soft wheat, so bread flour genuinely reads a few grams heavier. But mills also publish different figures for their own bags. Anywhere from 120 to 130 grams is defensible; the number only starts mattering when you stop being consistent about it.

What the Extra Protein Actually Does

Bread flour runs 12–14% protein against 10–12% for all-purpose, and that is the number that matters for structure. More gluten-forming protein means a dough that can survive a longer knead, stretch further without tearing, and hold on to more gas as it proofs. That is what buys you chew, oven spring, and the open irregular crumb you are chasing in a bagel, a pizza base, or a rustic loaf.

What the extra protein does not do is slot neatly into your hydration math. Bread flour absorbs more water than all-purpose at the same hydration percentage, so a dough built on it feels noticeably stiffer right after mixing and then slackens as the flour finishes hydrating. If you swap bread flour into a recipe written for all-purpose, expect to add liquid rather than remove it — the same 15–30 g per standard loaf that the reverse swap takes away, in the other direction — and give the dough a full 20 minutes before deciding whether it needs more. The full comparison of all three flours is in cake flour vs all-purpose vs bread flour.

Measuring Bread Flour Without a Scale

Everything above assumes spoon-and-level. Scoop the cup straight into the bag instead and you can drag 140 to 150 grams into it, because the cup compacts the flour as it pushes through. An extra 15 grams of flour in a single-cup recipe is enough to turn a soft sandwich loaf into a dry, tight one — and it is the single most common reason a recipe that worked for someone else fails in your kitchen.

If you bake bread more than occasionally, this is the argument for a scale. Weighing removes the variable permanently, and the kitchen scale guide covers what to buy and how to use tare properly.

Grams of Bread Flour Back to Cups

Divide grams by 130. The amounts that come up most in bread recipes:

  • 1/4 cup ≈ 33 g · 1/3 cup ≈ 43 g · 1/2 cup = 65 g
  • 100 g ≈ 3/4 cup
  • 200 g ≈ a little over 1½ cups
  • 500 g ≈ 3¾ cups
  • 1 kg ≈ just under 7¾ cups

Water, milk, and oil do not use these numbers — a cup of liquid is a different animal entirely. The cooking converter handles liquids and dry ingredients side by side if you are scaling a whole formula.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many grams is 1 cup of bread flour?

A1: About 130 grams with the spoon-and-level method, which is what this converter uses. Because mills publish different figures, the honest range for one cup of bread flour is 120 to 130 grams.

Q2: Is bread flour heavier than all-purpose flour?

A2: Per cup, slightly — around 130 g against 120 g for all-purpose. Part of that is the harder wheat settling more tightly in the cup, and part is simply that different mills publish different numbers for the same flour type.

Q3: Does bread flour absorb more water than all-purpose flour?

A3: Yes. A dough built on bread flour will feel stiffer than the same dough made with all-purpose. If you are converting a recipe, expect to add liquid — roughly 15–30 g per standard loaf — and always give the dough a rest before you judge it.

Q4: Can I substitute all-purpose flour for bread flour?

A4: Yes, with a liquid adjustment. Replacing bread flour with all-purpose means cutting the liquid by about 15–30 g (roughly 5%) for a standard loaf, then judging by feel. You will lose some chew and oven spring, which matters most for bagels, pizza, and crusty loaves.
